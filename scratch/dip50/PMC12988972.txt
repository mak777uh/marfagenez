 Abstract Efficient cryopreservation is essential for maintaining the viability of Saccharomyces eubayanus , a cryotolerant wild yeast of industrial importance as the cold-adapted parent of Saccharomyces pastorianus. This study integrated post-thaw viability and vitality assessments of cryopreserved S. 
 eubayanus CRUB 1568ᵀ with experimental measurements of transient heat transfer and numerical simulation of the freezing stage. Two protocols were evaluated: (A) direct freezing of cryovials in cryoboxes at − 80 °C, governed by convection, and (B) freezing inside a CoolCell® device (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA), where heat transfer occurs by conduction through the insulated plastic material. A mathematical model was developed to numerically solve the transient heat transfer equation with phase change using the finite element method. Experimental temperature–time data validated the simulations, allowing estimation of overall heat transfer coefficients (UA = 18.04 W m −2  K −1 ; UB = 4.76 W m −2  K −1 ) and characteristic freezing times (t𝚌 = 10.9 min; 27.6 min, respectively). Calculated Biot numbers confirmed uniform temperature distribution within cryovials. PROTOCOL A achieved optimal cooling rates (5–7 °C min −1 ) and yielded higher post-thaw viability (71.7 ± 3.5%) compared with PROTOCOL B (51.2 ± 3.6%) after 1 year at − 80 °C. The integration of modeling and experimental data demonstrates that the overall heat transfer coefficient is a key engineering parameter influencing cryopreservation performance. Direct freezing of cryovials in cryoboxes represents a simpler, faster, and lower-cost approach that ensures uniform cooling and higher cell survival, providing a valuable basis for standardizing yeast cryogenic storage in industrial and biotechnological applications. 
 Key points 
 Finite element modeling assessed heat transfer  during yeast cryopreservation. Direct freezing in cryoboxes achieved higher viability than CoolCell®. Overall heat transfer coefficient (U) is key for cryogenic performance analysis.

 Introduction Saccharomyces eubayanus is a cryotolerant wild yeast known as the cold-adapted parent of Saccharomyces pastorianus , the hybrid responsible for lager beer production (Libkind et al. 2011 ; Giorgetti et al. 2025 ). Due to its industrial relevance, efficient cryopreservation methods of this microorganism are essential, although recovery from cold storage presents challenges and difficulties, which limit the long-term preservation of this strategic genetic resource in culture collections. The survival of the model yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae during freezing has been studied in literature and it is strongly dependent on the rate at which it is cooled (Mazur 1970 ). The relationship between survival and cooling velocity shows an inverted U shape curve, with maximum survival occurring at an optimal rate. If cooling is carried out faster or slower than this optimal rate, it leads to a decrease in survival; therefore, two distinct, rate-dependent factors are responsible for cell death during freezing. This phenomenon is explained by the “two-factor hypothesis,” which establishes that different mechanisms cause cell death linked to the cooling rates. At a slow cooling rate, injury is caused by “solution effects” (Bank and Mazur 1973 ), since as the external medium freezes, solutes become concentrated, creating a hypertonic environment that draws water out of the cells. While this dehydration prevents the lethal formation of intracellular ice, prolonged exposure to high solute concentrations and the associated cellular dehydration can be damaging. Slower cooling rates translate to longer exposure to the damaging solution effects. If cells cool faster than the optimal rate, injury is primarily caused by the formation of intracellular ice (Mazur 1963 ). At high rates, there is insufficient time for water to leave the cell and freeze externally. The intracellular water becomes supercooled and eventually freezes inside the cell, causing a lethal event. The optimal cooling rate is therefore a rate which is slow enough to allow sufficient dehydration to prevent intracellular ice formation, but fast enough to minimize the time during which cells are exposed to damaging solution effects. For S. cerevisiae NRRL-Y-2235, extensive data indicates that the optimal cooling rate is approximately 7 °C/min, which yields a maximum survival of about 55%; however, cooling rates below optimal, such as 1.8 °C/min, result in about 43% survival after rapid warming (Seki et al. 2009 ; Mazur 1970 ).
 While considerable experience is now available on the optimal preservation methods for many groups of microorganisms, these procedures may not be applicable as a general rule to all yeast species and microorganisms; particular care is needed with cryo-tolerant genera and species. A greater range of procedures should be assessed and research carried out to determine optimal protocols in laboratories when managing culture collections. It is a widespread practice to apply specific devices such as CoolCell® (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA) or Mr. Frosty™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for the freezing of yeast in cryovials for their long-term cold storage. The CoolCell® container is constructed of closed-cell, cross-linked polyethylene foam and a solid thermo-conductive core, which in combination with a −80 °C freezer provides a freezing rate of 1 °C/min. The CoolCell® design uses a combination of insulation foam, radial symmetry, and a heat transfer core to regulate heat loss. The Mr. Frosty™ container consists of the following parts (i) polycarbonate container, (ii) high-density polyethylene closure, and (iii) a tube holder. It uses isopropyl alcohol to achieve the recommended cooling rate of 1 °C/min. The instructions for both devices are similar: the container should be initially at room temperature, the cryovials are placed at the tube holes or vial chambers inside the container and then inserted into the mechanical freezer at −80 °C for a minimum of 4 h before moving to archive storage. In the case of Mr. Frosty™, special care during handling is needed to avoid spilling of the alcohol inside the container during transfer in and out of the ultrafreezer with cryovials. The procedure using both containers requires the transferring of frozen samples to archive storage; therefore, an insulated pan or container with dry ice must be used when removing the Mr. Frosty™ or CoolCell® from the ultrafreezer. A cryogenic environment is necessary while there is manipulation of cryovials such as removal of samples from the vial chambers and insertion/placement into the cryoboxes or other permanent storage devices. The removal of the cryovials from the containers and relocation into the final storage device may result critical since a temperature rise that leads to cell death must be avoided. The yeast suspension in the cryogenic vial can rise from −80 °C to over −50 °C in less than 1 min if exposed to room temperature (CoolCell® manual). Additionally, it represents a time-consuming activity for the personnel in charge of applying these protocols for culture collection with a limited amount of vials that can be processed in a batch. In these cases (Mr. Frosty™ and CoolCell®), where a container is used, the cryovial is cooled, and the heat transfer is governed by a conduction process since the wall of the cryovial is in intimate contact with a plastic material of the container. Another method that could be used as a simpler and more efficient alternative is the direct freezing of cryovials from ambient temperature to −80 °C in cryoboxes for long-term storage, without the removal from the mechanical freezer or the need to use dry ice in the process. There is no information regarding the performance of this procedure for S. eubayanus in terms of the cooling rate velocity that the system achieves or the survival outcome. It is, however, a better alternative in practical and economic terms since it is less expensive (no container is used) and the cryovials are directly frozen for long-term storage in the cryobox; in addition, no dry ice is used since no extraction or relocation is necessary. In this procedure or protocol, the cryovial is cooled by the surrounding cold air at −80 °C inside the plastic cryobox. The base of the cryobox has holes that allow air to circulate; therefore, heat transfer is governed by a convection process. All the methods mentioned involve different freezing stages governed by transient heat transfer. Key parameters include overall surface heat transfer coefficients (U), which reflect the heat exchange between the cryovial and its surrounded environment. The performance of cryopreservation procedures often relies on the use of cooling rates, which are calculated using the slope of the thermal history curve at a given position in the sample. This calculation when applied to a freezing curve shows three different cooling rates, corresponding to different zones; V1 corresponds to the cooling of the sample until the initial freezing temperature is reached; V2 to the phase change zone or plateau region, and V3 to the cooling of the fully frozen sample. Santos et al. ( 2024 ) applied the concept of characteristic freezing time (tc) in an attempt to find a single variable for a better visualization of the performance of cryopreservation protocols; this variable can be calculated from the thermal history of the system (temperature vs. time curve) and can be defined as the time elapsed between the initial freezing temperature of the fluid and a reference temperature of −40 °C (Tf), since at this temperature, more than 80% of the total water content is in a frozen state (Santos et al. 2013 , 2024 ). In the present work, in order to analyze the freezing rates of the cryovials during cryopreservation of yeasts, this concept of tc was applied. There is scarce information related to the overall heat transfer coefficients that govern each protocol implemented for the cryopreservation of yeasts relevant for the brewing industry. In addition, the characteristic freezing times (that are representative of the reciprocal of the freezing rates) and the comparison of protocols for the cryopreservation of yeasts using cryovials filled with cells suspended in glycerol water mixture have yet not been reported. This information could be useful since the cryopreservation of yeast strains is time-consuming and several steps using devices increase processing times greatly. Additionally some procedures that use specific containers such as Mr.Frosty™ and CoolCell® could be expensive and hard to implement for some laboratories that maintain large microbial culture collections. Numerical simulations of the transient heat transfer partial differential equation using the finite element method are efficient when trying to predict temperature vs. time curves in different points of the systems that include several domains such as the liquid suspension of cells (LS) contained in the plastic cryoval (PC) and the headspace air (A); however, it is a challenging task since a phase change transition due to the transformation of water into ice is involved. The use of a numerical technique to describe heat transfer phenomena during the cryopreservation of yeast has yet not been reported, and it might help visualize the bottleneck of the freezing process by determining relevant heat transfer parameters and develop standardized procedures for yeast cryopreservation. This work aimed to (1) compare the cooling rates of two protocols: (A) direct placement of cryovials inside a cryobox in the Ultrafreezer and (B) cryovials placed in a device that retards the cooling process such as CoolCell® in an Ultrafreezer (UF), (2) to evaluate the characteristic freezing times (tc) and the cooling rate velocities during the cryopreservation of S. 
 eubayanus CRUB 1568  T , (3) to determine by numerical simulations using the finite element method the overall heat transfer coefficients of the two protocols, and (4) to correlate these parameters with the post-thaw viability and vitality of S. 
 eubayanus CRUB 1568  T . 
 Materials and methods 
 Yeast culture and cryopreservation protocols S. eubayanus CRUB 1568 T (CBS 12357) was obtained from the culture collection Colección de Recursos Microbianos de la Patagonia Andina (CReMPa), Río Negro, Argentina. This strain was inoculated into 100-mL conical flask containing 45 mL of yeast malt (YM) media (0.3 g yeast extract, 0.3 g malt, 0.5 g meat peptone, and 1 g glucose in 100 mL of distilled water), which was shaken at 20 °C and 180 rpm. The liquid culture was incubated overnight and afterwards, 1 mL of yeast suspension was transferred to a 250-mL conical flask containing 99 mL of YM media, obtaining a final volume of 100 mL. This culture was grown under the same conditions (20 °C and 180 rpm) until cells entered the early stationary phase. At this point (20 h of incubation), cells were harvested by centrifugation (10 min, 5000 rpm) in 15-mL falcons. The pellet was suspended in a 10 mL glycerol (99.5% v/v purity, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) dissolved in distilled water at a concentration of 10 w/w. Afterwards, 100 µL of the cell suspension was transferred to cryovials (Greiner Bio-One, Frickenhausen, Germany) containing 900 µL of glycerol (10% w/w) having a final cell concentration of 10 6 cells/mL. Cryovials were sealed and mixed as described by Shah et al. ( 2022 ), before freeze-thaw protocols. The performance of both protocols was assessed; in PROTOCOL A, cryovials are in contact with air at −80 °C in UF, and in PROTOCOL B, cryovials are in contact with the plastic material of the CoolCell® placed in inside the UF (Fig.  1 ). It must be remarked that in PROTOCOL B, the cryovial is not in contact with air but with a plastic material as can be observed in Fig.  1 . Fig. 1

 Experimental determination of the characteristic freezing time and cooling and warming rates The temperature vs. time curve for each protocol was measured using a type T-thermocouple (copper/copper-nickel) connected to a data-logger TESTO (Testo SE &amp; Co. KGaA, Lenzkirch, Germany), which recorded temperature every 10 s at the center and at the interface plastic/liquid of the cryovial. A second thermocouple was used to record the temperature in the external air inside the ultrafreezer. Figure  1 shows the photographs of the experimental setup using the cryovials, the thermocouples, and dataloggers for each protocol. The cooling rates for each protocol were calculated based on the initial straight-line slope (Fennema et al. 1973 ) of each stage during the freezing process. The characteristic freezing time (tc) was calculated for each protocol. An independent set of experiments using distilled water in the cryovials (that has known thermo-physical properties) submitted to the same protocols as the biological samples (described in Fig.  1 ) was conducted in order to determine the overall surface heat transfer coefficients. The thermal histories were recorded and used for the comparison of the numerical simulations with the experimental data. After 1 year of storage in an independent rack without risk of potential warming, cryovials were thawed in a controlled temperature water bath at 25 °C with mild stirring. Warming and cooling rates were obtained as described in Santos et al. ( 2024 ). 
 Viability and vitality After 1-year frozen storage in the ultrafreezer at −80 °C, cells were thawed using a controlled-temperature water bath at 25 °C with agitation. Viability was obtained by plating cells in YM media supplemented with 15 g L −1 agar after serial dilutions in physiological solution. Colony-forming units (CFU) were counted after 48–72 h. Vitality was assessed by measuring the colony diameter after 48 h using ImagePro Plus software. Percentage vitality was calculated by dividing the average colony diameter obtained in each protocol by the average colony diameter of the control treatment. In both cases, the data obtained were compared with the control treatment (cells from the same culture suspended in distilled water without any cryopreservation protocol) to determine viability and vitality. Statistical analysis (analysis of variance, determination of standard deviation (SD) and Tukey test) was performed by SigmaPlot 12.0 software. Each protocol was done in triplicate, and for each replica, 2 separate plates were analyzed. 
 Mathematical modeling of the freezing process during the cryopreservation protocols The cryovials used for simulation purposes were purchased from Greiner Bio-One (Greiner Bio-One, Frickenhausen, Germany) and have a 1-ml volume capacity. The geometrical dimensions of the cryovial were measured using a caliper. For the mathematical modeling and numerical simulation, the cryovial can be considered a solid of revolution having a symmetry plane at r  = 0; therefore, cylindrical coordinates were implemented. The geometry and domains were rendered within the engineering simulation environment of Comsol Multiphysics (COMSOL AB, Stockholm, Sweden, license number 2097640), and the domains can be seen in Fig.  2 .

The numerical model was solved using the COMSOL Multiphysics (COMSOL AB, Stockholm, Sweden, lic. #2097640) software incorporating the thermo-physical properties of each domain, overall surface heat transfer coefficients, and external air temperature. Numerical simulations and experimental measurements were carried out in a two-step process; first, the tested substance (water) was modeled in the cryovial since its temperature-dependent properties are fully documented in literature and were used as known input information in the model. The experiments with cryovials containing water that were submitted to the PROTOCOLS A and B allowed the rigorous determination of the overall surface heat transfer coefficients applying independent experiments. The advantage relies on the fact that water-ice thermo-physical properties during the temperature range of the freezing process are well known; therefore, the numerical simulations allowed the determination of the overall heat transfer coefficient by contrasting experimental and numerical temperature vs. time curves and finding the UA and UB that minimize the residual sum of squares (RSS). The second step consisted in introducing these calculated UA and UB values in the numerical model using as input information the glycerol + yeast suspension in order to simulate the freezing process and compare with the laboratory experiments. The agreement between experimental and numerical results allows the validation of the heat transfer model, confirming the accuracy of the UA and UB values.

 Spatial and time discretization A triangular mesh using Lagrange elements of order 2 was applied to discretize the three domains that constitute the cryovial system: plastic, the solution (distilled water or glycerol-water mixture 10% w/w), and the headspace air (see Fig.  5 ). The total number of elements that constituted the mesh for the three domains was 334 (polypropylene 119, solution 168, headspace air 47). The use of a finer mesh showed no significant effect on the accuracy of the solution. A fixed mesh was used because the phase change was modeled using a continuum approach, where the latent heat of melting was incorporated directly into the material properties via highly non-linear temperature-dependent functions for specific heat, thermal conductivity, and density; the phase transition that occurs over a discrete temperature interval was modeled using Heaviside and Gaussian functions. In this way, the model treated the interface as a property gradient rather than a geometric discontinuity, and the fluid domain remains geometrically fixed during the transition, eliminating the need for dynamic meshing. The time discretization scheme used was a backward Euler differentiation (minimum order 1 and maximum order 5) with a tuning step having a maximum of 0.1 s and a minimum initial starting value of 0.001 s. The absolute and relative tolerances for each integration step were 0.001 and 0.01, respectively. The numerical runs were tested for their computational speed using a PC Intel(R) Core(TM) i3 6300 with a processor speed of 3.80 GHz and a random access memory (RAM) of 4 GB having a maximum central processing unit (CPU) time of 4 min.  Warming rates The warming rates of cryovials (initial straight line slope) containing water and a water + glycerol mixture 10% w/w were 105.33 °C/min (standard deviation, s.d. = 17.63) and 104.46 °C/min (s.d. 14.92), respectively. The values found for both fluids were similar and correspond to high warming rates. This is important since a high warming rate helps to avoid the growth and recrystallization of small ice into larger, more damaging crystals which can occur during slow warming. Rapid warming melts the small crystals before they have time to grow. It has been reported that, for slowly cooled cells (where injury is due to solution effects), slow warming is often more damaging than rapid warming (Mazur 1970 ).

 Discussion The results of this study demonstrate that the direct freezing protocol (PROTOCOL A), characterized by a significantly faster cooling rate (tc = 10.9 min; UA = 18.04 W/m 2 K), is more suitable for the cryopreservation of S. eubayanus CRUB 1568ᵀ than the standard CoolCell® method (PROTOCOL B; tc = 27.57 min; UB = 4.76 W/m 2 K). PROTOCOL A yielded a significantly higher post-thaw viability of 71.7% compared with 51.2% for PROTOCOL B. These findings challenge the generalized assumption that a controlled cooling rate of 1 °C/min is universally optimal for all eukaryotic cells. This specific rate is widely regarded as the “gold standard” for the cryopreservation of human mammalian cells, particularly for peripheral blood lymphocytes (Farrant et al. 1972 ), mesenchymal stem cells (Linkova et al. 2022 ), and hematopoietic stem cells (Shu et al. 2010 ), where it is essential to maintain a delicate balance between avoiding lethal intracellular ice and minimizing osmotic injury (Hunt 2011 ). However, yeast cells possess distinct biophysical properties that shift this optimal window. Specifically, the higher hydraulic permeability and significantly higher surface-area-to-volume ratio of yeast (Dumont et al. 2003 , 2004 ) allow sufficient water efflux to reach osmotic equilibrium even at cooling rates higher than 1 °C/min. The experimental data and calculations confirm that the optimal cooling range for S. eubayanus is higher. Additionally, literature reports show that optimal cooling velocities for yeast are in the range of 3–10 °C/min (Morris et al. 1988 ; Mazur and Schmidt 1968 ), and Seki et al. ( 2009 ) showed an optimal of 7 °C/min for S. cerevisiae ; this value is in agreement with the results in this work which resulted in an average V1 = 6.75 °C/min (initial cooling period of the yeast + glycerol solution) and V3 = 6.86 °C/min (freezing period of the yeast + glycerol solution). Therefore, the lower viability observed in PROTOCOL B suggests that for S. eubayanus , the 1 °C/min rate is unnecessarily slow. At such slow rates, cells are likely subjected to prolonged “solution effects,” where excessive dehydration and exposure to concentrated solutes cause irreversible damage (Bank and Mazur 1973 ). The direct freezing protocol (PROTOCOL A) avoids lethal intracellular ice formation while minimizing the toxic solute exposure typical of slow-rate devices. From a practical and economic standpoint, this method offers a cost-effective and operationally simple alternative, eliminating the need for expensive cooling devices while ensuring higher cell survival and reduced handling time. While CFU-based viability is a practical and widely used endpoint (Mazur 1961 ; Meyer et al. 1975 ; Morris et al. 1988 ; Dumont et al. 2003 ; Gujjari et al. 2010 ; de Arruda Moura Pietrowski et al. 2015 ), it mainly reports the ability to regrow (often interpreted as preserved membrane integrity) and can miss sublethal effects or injuries that compromise function after thawing (e.g., osmotic/oxidative stress, membrane and organelle perturbations, and the energetic burden of repair). In this study, vitality, which was determined by colony diameter after 48 h, was reduced by approximately 30% in all treatments, regardless of the viability differences observed between protocols. This indicates that a fraction of the viable cells likely sustained sublethal injury that slows recovery and metabolism, so growth performance provides a more sensitive readout of post-thaw health. With that objective, our research group is currently standardizing yeast cryopreservation by combining viability with functional metrics, including lag phase duration, growth kinetics, and fermentation outputs (CO 2 production and ethanol yield). This approach aligns with recent evidence suggesting that high post-thaw recovery coincides with substantial proteome remodeling (Ramasamy et al. 2025 ), which emphasizes that optimal viability does not equate to minimal metabolic disturbance but rather depends on metabolic readiness and active injury-response pathways. An important aspect considered in the present work was the potential warming of the cryovials during their frozen storage; it is important to maintain them undisturbed once placed in the ultrafreezer for a 1-year period. In this study, they were not removed from the freezer or submitted to potential warming cycles since they were maintained in an independent rack. Several authors have reported risks associated with thawing yeast cultures (repeated freezing and thawing cycles). Cabrera et al. ( 2020 ) recommended taking precautions when sampling from a frozen stock to avoid thawing the entire batch, as cell viability decreases with each freeze–thaw cycle. Bond ( 2007 ) advised minimizing the time the straws remain out of liquid nitrogen during sample retrieval, and Meyer et al. ( 1975 ) performed three freeze–thaw cycles with Candida utilis and Lipomyces stokesii stored at − 60 °C and observed a reduction in both cell viability and fermentative capacity after each cycle. A mathematical model based on transient heat transfer partial differential equations with phase change transition that occurs during the cryopreservation of S. eubayanus CRUB 1568  T was developed and validated. The equations for the different domains (yeast suspension, plastic wall of the cryovial and surrounding air or insulated material according to the tested protocol) were numerically solved by the finite element method. The model was applied to determine overall surface heat transfer coefficients when two distinct cryopreservation protocols are applied: (A) direct freezing of cryovials placed in cryoboxes at − 80 °C, where heat transfer occurs mainly by air convection, and (B) freezing within a CoolCell® device, where heat is transferred by conduction through the plastic material. Numerical simulations reproduced the experimental temperature–time curves with high precision, enabling accurate estimation of the overall surface heat transfer coefficients. The values obtained were UA = 18.04 W/m 2 K for PROTOCOL A and UB = 4.76 W/m 2 K for PROTOCOL B. These values explain the observed differences in the characteristic freezing times (t𝚌), which was 10.9 min for PROTOCOL A in comparison to 27.6 min for PROTOCOL B, indicating that direct exposure to cold air enables more efficient thermal exchange than the contact with plastic insulation. The UA is consistent with air natural convection conditions around cryovials inside an ultrafreezer. The calculated Biot numbers indicated a nearly uniform temperature distribution within the cryovials for both protocols, confirming that the internal resistance to heat transfer is negligible compared to external resistance. The observed cooling rates for PROTOCOL A were V1 = 6.75 °C/min (initial cooling period of the yeast + glycerol solution) and V3 = 6.86 °C/min (freezing period of the yeast + glycerol solution); this PROTOCOL showed the best performance and aligned with the optimal range reported for yeasts, ensuring a balance between minimizing intracellular ice formation and avoiding excessive solute concentration effects. Consequently, post-thaw viability after 1 year of storage reached 71.7 ± 3.5% for PROTOCOL A, compared to 51.2 ± 3.6% for PROTOCOL B. From a mechanistic standpoint, the study demonstrates that the overall heat transfer coefficient (U) is a critical variable determining cryopreservation efficiency. By integrating experimental and numerical analyses, a relationship was established between heat-transfer performance, cooling rates, and biological survival. The results highlight that direct freezing of cryovials in cryoboxes not only enhances cell viability but also simplifies handling and reduces operational costs by eliminating intermediary devices which are expensive. In conclusion, direct freezing in cryoboxes represents a simpler, faster, and cost-effective method that ensures uniform temperature fields, higher survival, and reduced handling time compared with container-based systems. The results emphasize the relevance of mechanistic heat transfer modeling in defining reproducible, scalable cryogenic practices for microbial culture collections and industrial biotechnology.
