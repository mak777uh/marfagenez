 ABSTRACT 
 Coral reefs are declining globally, and novel solutions, such as cryopreservation, can help secure coral biodiversity and genetic diversity. Factors that affect successful cryopreservation of coral larvae include cryoprotectant type and concentration, membrane permeability, larval developmental stage, lipid content, volume, and freezing and warming rates. This study examined conduction‐dominated “cryomesh” technology across a range of coral species, distinguishing vitrification on cryomesh, 24 h swimming survival, and settlement competence as separate measures of success. Using a previously successful cryoprotectant solution, the exposure time, volume, and developmental stage most amenable to cryomesh cryopreservation were examined, and biophysical attributes were modeled to determine cooling and rewarming rates. Most species vitrified on the cryomesh and showed short‐term motility, but robust 24 h swimming survival was limited to Lobactis scutaria and Fungia fungites , the smallest species; other small‐volume species did not survive. To improve heat transfer, subsets of embryos were sheared during early development, yielding sub‐size larvae that did not swim post‐thaw, but the sheared, untreated controls settled and matured normally. These findings show that volume alone does not ensure survival. Additional next steps must include toxicity and permeation studies of other cryoprotectants and long‐term restoration potential evaluated by examining post‐thaw settlement success. 
 Cryomesh enables a low‐tech cryopreservation method, adding a valuable option to the coral reef conservation toolkit. The approach reveals how size, toxicity, and developmental stage govern larval survival, while modeling links cooling and rewarming rates to a theoretical upper volume limit. Shearing embryos reduces volume below this bound, offering a promising strategy for corals with larger‐sized larvae.

 Introduction 
 Coral reefs are some of the oldest, most diverse ecosystems on Earth and among the most valuable. They play a critical role in maintaining ocean health, nurture more than 25% of all ocean life, protect homes and cities from storms, and add billions of dollars yearly to the global economy through fisheries, new pharmaceuticals, tourism, and recreation [ 1 , 2 , 3 ]. The world's coral reefs, however, continue to degrade at unprecedented rates [ 4 , 5 ]. The overuse of fossil fuels is warming and acidifying the ocean [ 6 , 7 ], producing a reef crisis that could lead to many coral species facing extinction within decades [ 8 , 9 ]. Recent climate models estimate that if greenhouse gas emissions continue unabated, 95% or more of the world's shallow‐water reef‐building corals could die by the mid‐2030's [ 10 ]. Solutions are needed now to quickly ensure the integrity of global reef biodiversity and genetic diversity and to create robust genetic resources for future restoration. 
 Reducing global carbon emissions is essential for the survival of global coral reefs. However, the pace of climate change is outstripping the ability to save many coral species and habitats. Therefore, critical intervention methods are needed now to safeguard species and reefs, ensure opportunities for recovery, and sustain remaining biodiversity [ 11 ]. Novel solutions, such as cryopreservation, can help secure the biodiversity and genetic diversity of coral. Cryopreservation removes some cellular water via osmotic gradients and replaces it with cryoprotectant solutions. Then, the cells are cooled (either slowly or rapidly) to liquid nitrogen temperatures, where they can remain in stasis for decades. Cryoprotectants, such as dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), can permeate cell membranes to stabilize them during cooling and reduce lethal intracellular ice formation [ 12 , 13 , 14 ]. In addition to permeating cryoprotectants, non‐permeating cryoprotectants, such as trehalose, can pull water from cells osmotically, thereby concentrating permeating cryoprotectants within the cytoplasm and reducing the probability of intracellular ice formation [ 15 , 16 ]. These processes depend strongly on the biophysical properties of the plasma membrane that govern the permeability rates of both water and cryoprotectants across the membrane, as well as on the overall volume and geometry of the cell or tissue [ 17 , 18 ]. As the size of a cell or tissue increases, the surface‐to‐volume ratio decreases, and transport of water and cryoprotectants becomes increasingly limiting. Consequently, larger tissues and organisms are substantially more challenging to cryopreserve successfully because diffusion distances increase and the probability of damaging ice formation rises [ 19 , 20 ]. Successfully done, however, cryopreservation can maintain cells, tissues, and whole organisms in stasis, frozen and alive, for decades. 
 To date, institutions around the world have successfully cryopreserved coral sperm [ 21 , 22 ] and coral larvae [ 23 , 24 ] and have helped create coral biorepositories in the US (USDA's Animal Germplasm Program and Smithsonian Institution), Australia (Taronga Conservation Society), Curaçao (CARMABI Foundation), and Mexico (Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México). Additionally, practitioners have used cryopreserved sperm to produce “Assisted Gene Flow corals,” i.e., corals with parents from two geographically distinct regions, for both the Caribbean and the Great Barrier Reef [ 25 , 26 ]. 
 Cryopreservation of coral sperm has been possible for over 15 years [ 27 ], and there are now thousands of cryopreserved samples from over 55 species of reef‐building coral stored in biorepositories around the world [ 28 ]. Despite the value of these samples as genetic resources, the use of frozen sperm to produce new corals is limited by the availability of fresh oocytes. This, in turn, reduces the opportunities for using cryopreserved sperm to help diversify populations as natural populations are diminished. However, the ability to cryopreserve coral larvae is a crucial step toward offsetting the extinction threat to coral. Moreover, as banked larval resources increase, these frozen assets can produce coral recruits in the absence of reproductively active adult corals, thus enabling the deployment of new corals outside of limited spawning periods to reduce overall restoration costs long‐term. 
 Vitrification and laser‐warming experiments have demonstrated that cryopreservation of coral larvae is possible, with the successful recovery of live larvae from the gonochoric spawning coral Lobactis scutaria [ 23 ] and the brooding coral Stylophora pistillata [ 29 ]. While laser nano‐warming is a suitable proof‐of‐concept, this approach utilizes expensive equipment requiring specialist training, requires rigorous permitting in laboratories, and relies on manual handling of few larvae in small sample volumes. These factors limit both accessibility and sample throughput, making this technology difficult to scale up to the numbers required to support banking and restoration. 
 As the extinction threat to coral has become alarming, especially in less diverse ecosystems such as the Caribbean, small‐scale larval vitrification cannot keep pace with conservation needs. Therefore, there is a need to create more field‐hardy, scalable, and easily transferable methods to vitrify coral larvae. Vitrification uses a cocktail of concentrated cryoprotectants that causes the sample to form a glass, preventing lethal intracellular ice formation. Cryomesh technology, a method that uses metal mesh to rapidly conduct heat away from cells, has been used to vitrify Drosophila embryos [ 30 ], bovine oocytes [ 31 ], plant shoots [ 32 ], and coral larvae [ 24 ], generally with good post‐thaw survival at approximately 50–80%. Cryomesh technology can achieve extremely high cooling rates because the sample preparation process removes excess cryoprotectant from around the samples through the mesh pores, thereby minimizing the total thermal mass. A recent study evaluated the basic heat transfer principles behind the cryomesh approach and investigated critical design parameters to achieve a conduction‐dominated cryomesh [ 24 ]. The conduction‐dominated cryomesh dramatically increases the cooling rate from 2‐ to 10‐fold, resulting in a cooling rate of 0.24 to 1.2 × 10 5 °C/min in various biosystems. The rate of freezing and warming using cryomesh is related to both the volume of the sample [ 24 , 30 ] and the cryoprotectant permeability. 
 Our objective in this study was to apply conduction‐dominated cryomesh technology to larvae from a wide range of coral species to provide an initial guide for coral cryopreservation at this life stage. Because the volume of the larvae is a critical factor in determining whether the cryomesh can warm them rapidly enough, we started by measuring egg and larval volumes of a variety of Pacific and Caribbean coral species to determine whether there is a specific cutoff size for cryomesh cryopreservation. We developed a model of the ideal suite of constrained parameters that may lead to success for a particular coral species. In addition, we tested the toxicity of the vitrification solution (used in [ 23 ]) at different larval ages, conducted vitrification tests, and banked those species that survived the vitrification process. To build this framework, we examined a wide variety of larvae from the following species: Lobactis scutaria (Hawaiʻi), Pocillopora meandrina (Hawaiʻi), Pocillopora acuta (Hawaiʻi), Fungia fungites (Australia), Acropora spathulata (Australia), Dendrogyra cylindrus (Florida), Mussa angulosa (Florida), Colpophyllia natans (Florida), Pseudodiploria strigosa (Florida), Diploria labyrinthiformis (Mexico), Orbicella annularis (Mexico), and Orbicella faveolata (Curaçao and Mexico).  Methods 2.1 
 Coral Husbandry, Breeding, and Health 2.1.1 
 Hawaiʻi 
 Colonies of L. scutaria and P. meandrina were collected from patch reefs in Kāneʻohe Bay, Hawaiʻi under permit SAP 2024–45. Hawaiian species were kept in outdoor aquarium tanks with flow‐through seawater until they were ready to be used for spawning. Spawning events occurred around the full moon in July and September 2024 ( L. scutaria ); May 2025 ( P. meandrina ); July and August 2025 ( L. scutaria ). During spawning, L. scutaria individuals were transferred to bowls filled with 0.22 µm filtered seawater (FSW). Individuals were allowed to spawn either eggs or sperm into their respective bowls. Before fertilization occurred, eggs were collected from each individual female for volume analysis. Eggs were then fertilized with pooled sperm and left undisturbed for 1 h. Bowls were moved into a covered area at ∼26°C, and gentle water changes were performed to remove used sperm. Larvae were cleaned each morning with FSW before the start of experiments. For P. meandrina , two to three colonies (genotypes) were placed in separate bins and were allowed to spawn and fertilize in the same tanks. From these, pools of larvae (n = 12, where n is a cross between 2–3 colonies) were then reared in separate bowls as above but with LED lighting to ensure the symbionts in the larvae were active. 2.1.2 
 Australia 
 
 Fungia fungites , maintained long‐term at the Australian Institute of Marine Science (AIMS) National Sea Simulator facility (SeaSim), were isolated in three‐liter bowls prior to sunset, and they spawned at 7 PM. Their eggs and sperm were crossed as described for L. scutaria with seven sperm and egg donors (n = 3 males and n = 4 females). Afterward, the larvae were pooled and placed into a single 80‐L flow‐through rearing system with gentle flow and aeration using 0.2‐µm‐filtered seawater and a 60‐µm banjo filter on the outflow. Acropora spathulata was collected from Davies Reef under Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority permit #G23/ 49085.1 and with the free, prior, and informed consent of the Bindal Sea Country Traditional Use of Marine Resources Agreement committee. Colonies were collected in the week prior to coral spawning by AIMS divers and brought to the SeaSim facility at AIMS in Townsville, where they were maintained in flow‐through aquaria with shaded natural light at temperature profiles matching their natal reefs (∼27°C). 2.1.3 
 Florida 
 Colonies of D. cylindrus were collected in Florida under Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary permit FKNMS‐2015‐156. After collection, corals were transferred and loaned to the Florida Aquarium for long‐term holding. The colonies were held ex situ for several years under natural lighting conditions in greenhouses with recirculating tanks. Colonies were moved to an indoor spawning lab with LED lighting in the spring of 2024 and exposed to seasonal cues following procedures described in O'Neil et al. [ 33 ]. Spawn was collected and larvae produced in August 2024. Colonies were allowed to spawn in their respective tanks, and eggs and sperm were mixed in the water column and allowed to sit for approximately 20 min post‐spawn. Presumably fertilized eggs were then gently scooped or pipetted out of the tank into containers of filtered seawater for development. Once the embryos were at the 2‐cell stage of division, a subset of the dividing embryos was intentionally sheared to create smaller larvae. The cells were sheared apart by pouring the embryos from one bowl to another from approximately 1 m height. After shearing, all embryos were left to develop at ∼26.5°C until gastrulation. After gastrulation, larvae were cleaned each day by removing lipids from the surface of the water with plastic cling film and by picking out healthy larvae from the rearing containers with pipettes to move them to clean containers with filtered seawater. The sheared embryos were not suitable for experimentation for several days as they had membrane blebbing and irregular shapes. However, after about two days of healing time, when the larvae were 3 days (72 h) old, they exhibited normal larval morphology, swimming, and robustness. 
 Colonies of Mussa angulosa were collected under FKNMS permit FKNMS‐2017‐100 and/or FKNMS #2018‐139. After collection, corals were transferred and loaned to The Florida Aquarium for long‐term holding. The colonies were held ex situ for several years in recirculating tanks under natural lighting conditions in greenhouses. Spawn was collected and larvae produced in August 2024. The process from spawning to larval cleaning after gastrulation was the same as for D. cylindrus . 
 Colonies of Colpophyllia natans and Pseudodiploria strigosa were collected under FKNMS permit FKNMS‐2017‐100 and/or FKNMS #2018‐139. After collection, corals were transferred and loaned to The Florida Aquarium for long‐term holding. The colonies were held ex situ for several years under natural lighting conditions in greenhouses with recirculating tanks. For both C. natans and P. strigosa , colonies were moved to an indoor spawning lab with LED lighting in the fall of 2024, and spawn was collected and larvae produced in the fall of 2025. Colonies were allowed to spawn in their respective tanks, and the bundles of eggs and sperm were scooped out of the tank. Batch crosses were made by combining bundles from several colonies and allowing them to fertilize for approximately 30 min. After fertilization, the embryos were moved into rearing containers for development. Once the embryos were at the 2‐cell stage of division, a subset of the dividing embryos was sheared with gentle shaking on an orbital shaker at 58 RPM for 8 min. Sheared embryos were left to divide again into the 2‐cell stage, and the shearing process was repeated at 58 RPM for 12 min to create larvae at the equivalent of the 4‐cell division stage. After shearing, the process for larval development and cleaning was the same as for D. cylindrus . 2.1.4 
 Curaçao 
 Larvae of the mountainous star coral Orbicella faveolata (formerly Montastraea faveolata ) were reared from wild‐caught gamete bundles following annual mass spawning events. Divers monitored colonies from nights 5 to 8 after the September and October full moons from approximately 2h45m to 4h15m after sunset. Gamete bundles were collected using weighted polyester nets affixed to funnels and 50 mL conical tubes (BD Falcon). Gamete bundles were transferred to the laboratory and mixed in a 1L fat separator (Norpro), left to fertilize for 60 min, then rinsed 5 to 7 times with filtered seawater until the water ran clear. Filtered seawater was prepared from raw ocean seawater using sequential 10″ filter cartridges in polycarbonate housings with pore sizes 50, 20, 5, and 0.5 µm (H2O Distributors). Aliquots of the rinsed, fertilized eggs were transferred, using sterile Petri dish lids as scoops, into clear polystyrene clamshell deli containers (Dart Container Corporation), each containing approximately 800 mL of filtered seawater, and left to incubate overnight at ambient seawater temperature (27°C–28°C). Dead larvae and debris were removed by blotting with a laboratory wipe (Kimwipe) and by selective pipetting. Water changes were conducted every 1–2 days by transferring larvae by pipette into new containers. Collection and propagation work was conducted under the permits granted to CARMABI Foundation by the Government of Curaçao (Permit #2012/48584). 2.1.5 
 Mexico 
 Prior to the 2024 and 2025 spawning seasons, monitoring of sites and colonies suitable for gamete collection was conducted in the Puerto Morelos Reef National Park (PNAPM). Subsequently, and in accordance with the spawning prediction calendar for this region of the Caribbean, gametes of Diploria labyrinthiformis , Orbicella faveolata , and Orbicella annularis were collected as authorized under permits issued by the Dirección General de Ordenamiento Pesquero y Acuícola No. PPF/DGOPA‐070/20 and No. PPF/DGOPA‐051/25 for D. labyrinthiformis , and by the Dirección General de Vida Silvestre of SEMARNAT No. SPARN/DGVS/09459/24 and SBRA/DGVS/04385/25 for the Orbicella species protected under Mexican law (NOM‐059). Gamete collection was performed by SCUBA diving at night, using conical nets positioned vertically over the colonies a few minutes before gamete release. The gametes were concentrated in the collection tube located at the top of the net. After spawning finished, the tubes were collected, capped, and maintained during transfer to the laboratory for assisted fertilization under controlled temperature conditions (27°C ± 1°C) using seawater filtered to 1 µm and sterilized with UV‐C light (FSW). 
 In August 2024, gametes from three D. labyrinthiformis colonies were combined in equal proportions, whereas in September, gametes from two O. faveolata colonies were used. Once fertilization was confirmed, excess sperm was removed by three consecutive FSW washes of the embryos at 28°C. Daily 70% water exchanges were performed until the larval stage was reached. Larvae were maintained in FSW with daily exchanges throughout the rearing period. Four‐ and five‐day‐old D. labyrinthiformis larvae and two‐ and three‐day‐old O. faveolata larvae were used for measurements. 
 In July and August 2025, gametes from two and three colonies of D. labyrinthiformis , respectively, were pooled for assisted fertilization. In September 2025, gametes from four colonies of O. annularis were used for assisted fertilization and combined in equal proportions. Embryo and larval care were carried out following the procedures previously described. For larval measurement assays, O. annularis larvae aged 8 and 9 days were employed. 2.2 
 Volume Measurements for Coral Eggs and Larvae 
 Precise volume measurement is needed to inform successful cryopreservation. Eggs and larvae were imaged in different locations using different compound microscopes. The compound microscope used for imaging L. scutaria , P. meandrina , P. acuta , D. cylindrus , M. angulosa , C. natans , D. labyrinthiformis, O. annularis, O. faveolata , and P. strigosa was an Olympus CX43 (Model CX43RF) with a trinocular port and digital microscope camera (AmScope MU2003‐BI) or an Olympus digital camera (Model EP50). A Leica Orthoplan (model 957685) with a Canon EOSR7 was used to measure O. faveolata , and A. spathulata was imaged with an Olympus BH‐2 with a Science Supply View camera (model S01‐0801A). 
 The larvae were pipetted into 1‐µl wells made from a standard glass microscope slide covered with a double thickness of standard electrical tape (3 M Scotch Electrical tape). A 2‐mm biopsy punch was used to create a small flat well in the electrical tape. A single egg or larva was transferred in 0.4–1 µl of FSW using a 2‐µl pipette with a cut tip and placed into the well. A pipette tip was used to flatten the water level in the well to reduce any possible magnification error from a concave or convex water surface. The eggs or larvae were imaged using a compound microscope at a magnification power that produced the largest image possible while still being in focus, usually a 10–40× objective. Images were captured with ToupLite software ( https://sios.net.au/software/touplite ) or EPview 1.4 for the Olympus EP50 camera ( https://evidentscientific.com/en/downloads ). A micrometer (OMAX Model A36CALM1 or Euromex Model AE.1110) was included when the egg/larval images were taken to calibrate dimensions of the sample later during analysis. Eggs or larvae from each genotype or pool were collected and imaged on developmental days ranging from D0 (eggs, day of coral spawning) to D9, depending on species. Once all images were taken, volumes were calculated by measuring the eggs and larvae using ImageJ software (ImageJ 1.54 g http://imagej.org ). The shape of the eggs and larvae approximated an oblate spheroid; therefore, an ellipse was fitted to the image to acquire the semi‐minor and semi‐major axes of the planar image. Then a volume for each image was calculated using the equation for an oblate spheroid, V = 4/3 ´ π ´ (b) 2 ´ c (b = semi‐major axis, c = semi‐minor axis). 2.2.1 
 Shearing Larvae to Reduce Volume 
 Some of our early success with the cryomesh vitrification of coral larvae [ 24 ] was not readily transferable to species with larvae that had larger volumes, such as Pocillopora acuta and Diploria labyrinthiformis (data not shown). In August 2024, we held a workshop at The Florida Aquarium to test and standardize our cryomesh vitrification methods for coral larvae. It quickly became clear that the larger larvae were not amenable to vitrification; thus, we sheared subsequent cohorts of coral embryos during the early cell division stages to produce larvae with reduced overall volumes [ 34 ]. We applied this method to D. cylindrus embryos (prior to its listing as endangered under the U.S. Endangered Species Act) by shaking containers of larvae at the 2–8 cell stage or by pouring them from a height of approximately 1 m. Sheared D. cylindrus and C. natans larvae were settled and reared until maturity at The Florida Aquarium. These juvenile corals were held in recirculating tanks under natural lighting in greenhouses after settlement and treated with the same husbandry practices as all other recruits for the duration of their growth. 
 At a subsequent workshop at AIMS in 2025, embryos were dissociated using a combination of gentle shaking with an orbital shaker and pouring between two containers. Embryos at the 2‐cell stage were placed into square 2 L plastic containers on an orbital shaker and agitated at 59 RPM for 10 min, followed by pouring between containers from a height of approximately 20 cm. The dissociated embryos were allowed to divide further and were then dissociated again at the 2‐cell stage. At this point, blastomeres were equivalent in size to those of a normal 4‐cell stage embryo. This process was repeated one more time (equivalent to shearing embryos at the 8‐cell stage). 2.3 
 Modeling Vitrification and Rewarming Success Based on Volumes 
 A 1D numerical heat transfer model was developed in Excel to estimate achievable rewarming rates during vitrification‐based cryopreservation of coral larvae. The model incorporated conductive thermal resistances between the larvae, supporting substrate, and surrounding environment to simulate transient heat transfer during warming. Larval volumes used in the simulations were obtained from experimentally measured morphological data for each species and developmental stage. Using these experimentally derived geometries, the model was used to predict theoretical cooling and rewarming rates under different external conditions and evaluate whether the estimated critical cooling and warming threshold for suppressing devitrification could be achieved. 2.4 
 Symbiont Reduction and Feeding Coral Larvae 
 Some coral species transfer symbionts from the parent to the egg ( P. meandrina ) or larvae ( P. acuta ) during maturation [ 35 ]. However, the presence of photosynthetic symbionts (Symbiodiniaceae spp.) in the tissue causes complications during vitrification because the symbionts have different water and cryoprotectant permeabilities than the coral tissue [ 36 , 37 ]. Because of this, larval symbiont ‘bleaching’ protocols were created to preserve the health of the larvae during vitrification (methods in [ 38 ]), with the understanding that coral symbionts could then be re‐introduced later after thawing. Briefly, the larvae were bleached using a menthol solution (99%, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) suspended in ethanol (190 proof, Decon Labs, Inc., King of Prussia, PA) paired with a constant 24‐h light period (no dark cycle) under fluorescent lights (∼350 PAR) at 26°C. A 200 mg/mL stock solution of menthol in ethanol was prepared (2 g menthol in 10 mL ethanol) and stored at 4°C until use. Larvae were placed in 2‐L aquaria, and 500 µl of the stock solution was added per 1 L of FSW (final concentration 0.1 mg/mL) with aeration for approximately 5 h at 26°C. After 5 h, they were transferred to a new tank with fresh FSW and aeration and maintained under the same lights. This was repeated until the larvae were visibly bleached, usually 2–3 days. Once the larvae were visibly bleached, they were returned to a 12 h day/night cycle and fed daily with commercially available amino acid supplements (Aquaforest Amino Mix or Brightwell Aquatics CoralAmino), adding 15 drops/L for 15 min. 2.5 
 Toxicity and Vitrification Testing 
 In previous studies, Daly et al. [ 23 ] (using laser‐warming) and Guo et al. [ 24 ] (using conduction‐dominated cryomesh) both successfully cryopreserved and revived L. scutaria larvae with a vitrification solution that was comprised of 10% 1,2‐propanediol (Sigma–Aldrich, 398039), 5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma–Aldrich, D8418), and 1 (m) trehalose (Food‐grade trehalose dihydrate, Swanson, Catalog #SWU478) in 1X Phosphate‐Buffered Saline (PBS; Sigma Aldrich, P3813). Therefore, we tested the larval response of many coral species to this cryoprotectant mixture of propanediol, DMSO, and trehalose in phosphate buffer solution – hereafter “PDT” – to determine how long each could tolerate the solution without damage (e.g., disruption of membranes and/or swimming/survival rates). Some experiments were stand‐alone toxicity trials while others were paired with a vitrification trial. Toxicity and vitrification of L. scutaria larvae have been studied previously – they can withstand exposure to PDT for up to 120 s – therefore, the overarching goal for this species in these experiments was to determine the optimal equilibration and rehydration time, while for all other species, the main goal was to assess toxicity exposure limits and post‐thaw responses. 
 Larvae were pipetted onto a 316 stainless steel woven cryomesh (400 × 400 openings per inch, 0.035 mm aperture, Inoxia Brand, McMaster‐Carr Catalog #9319T189) that was attached to a 3D‐printed PLA frame [ 39 ], blotted on lab‐grade wipes to remove FSW, and placed into PDT for dehydration for the specified experimental time. Upon removal, the cryomesh was again blotted to remove PDT, followed by an equal rehydration time in 0.5 M trehalose in FSW, and then recovered in FSW and analyzed the next day. Dehydration and rehydration steps were performed in shallow Petri dishes by lowering the cryomesh onto an aliquot of the treatment solution. For recovery in FSW, each cryomesh was placed into a separate well of a polystyrene 6‐well cell culture plate. Care was taken to ensure that larvae on the cryomesh remained separate and did not overlap each other, which would potentially affect membrane exposure to cryoprotectant and rehydration solutions, and impede heat transfer in vitrification treatments. Dehydration and rehydration times tested were 30, 60, 90, and 120 s. This broad range of dehydration and rehydration times was chosen to help identify whether post‐thaw survival could be increased by either reducing cryoprotectant exposure time to minimize potential toxicity (e.g., 30 s), or by increasing exposure time to potentially increase dehydration and cryoprotectant permeation (e.g., 90 and 120 s), compared to the 60 s exposure time used in previous studies. Survival was determined by counting larvae at 24 h that were both swimming (moving) in a directional, progressive motion and morphologically similar to the control larvae; smaller and irregularly shaped fragments that were stationary or spinning in a circular motion were excluded. Lobactis scutaria larvae were tested for toxicity and vitrification at developmental days D3–D5, P. meandrina larvae were tested at D2–D4, D. cylindrus larvae were tested at D2–D3, and F. fungites larvae were tested on D5. Diploria labyrinthiformis larvae were assessed for toxicity and vitrification on D4, whereas sheared larvae were assessed on Days 4 and 5. Orbicella annularis larvae were tested between Days 6 and 9, and O. faveolata larvae on D5. 
 To test for successful larval vitrification and warming, cryomeshes loaded with larvae were 1) blotted on lab‐grade task wipes to remove FSW, 2) gently placed in PDT for dehydration with long forceps, 3) removed and blotted to remove excess PDT from the cryomesh and frame, 4) gripped with long forceps and vertically plunged into liquid nitrogen until vitrified (i.e., until LN2 stopped boiling), 5) quickly removed from liquid nitrogen and plunged vertically into a container of 0.5 (M) trehalose in FSW for warming, 6) transferred to a larger container of ambient temperature (∼26°C) 0.5 (M) trehalose in FSW, and 7) transferred to FSW overnight for recovery. Note that all toxicity exposure testing followed the steps above, omitting only the liquid nitrogen and warming steps. For full description of cryomesh construction and cryomesh handling, see the experimental section in Guo et al. [ 24 ]. 
 Toxicity and vitrification tests with Fungia fungites were conducted early in the development of the cryomesh freezing method during a limited research trip, and there were very few larvae produced by this species during that cycle; therefore, less extensive testing was done on this species overall. However, like L. scutaria , in the laser warming process [ 23 ], the F. fungites larvae were exposed to three toxicity and vitrification trials with 60:60, 60:120, or 120:60 s dehydration‐to‐rehydration times. Approximately 100–200 larvae were loaded onto each cryomesh, placed in PDT, either exposed to liquid nitrogen (vitrified) or not (toxicity exposure), rehydrated in 0.5 m trehalose in FSW, and then transferred to fresh FSW. Experimental wells with larvae from all toxicity and vitrification trials were video recorded at 24 h post‐treatment. As above, the number of intact progressive swimming larvae was counted, excluding irregularly shaped fragments. 2.6 
 Testing Increased Warming Temperature 
 We noticed in July 2025 that cryopreserved, post‐thaw L. scutaria larval treatment replicates appeared to have more, smaller larvae than the untreated controls. We hypothesized that some of the larvae may have cracked during warming, with the resulting larval pieces surviving and continuing to develop. To test this, two cryomeshes, each with ∼200 D5 L. scutaria larvae, were vitrified on stainless steel cryomesh with PDT (60s dehydration and rehydration; see previous methods) and warmed at two temperatures, 26°C and 50°C in 0.5 (M) trehalose in FSW. The 26°C treatment was warmed and rehydrated in 0.5 (M) trehalose in FSW (26°C) as above. For the other treatment, the 0.5 (M) trehalose in FSW was heated to 50°C. The vitrified larvae on the cryomesh were quickly plunged into the 50°C trehalose for less than one second, and then immediately moved into ambient (26°C) trehalose for the remainder of the rehydration period. Volumes for most of the larvae in the two treatments above and the untreated controls were measured and their volume distributions plotted (26°C: n = 221; 50°C: n = 239; control D5 larvae: n = 99). In examining the videos from the F. fungites from 2022, they also appeared to show this same phenomenon of producing smaller, fragmented swimming larvae, but volume distribution analysis was not done on this cohort. 2.7 
 Banking Coral Larvae 
 Cryomeshes with approximately 200 vitrified larvae each were placed into a “cryobox”: a VWR CryoPro cardboard sample box (CAT#82007‐158) with a 3D‐printed insert designed to hold 80 cryomeshes [ 39 ] (Figure S1 ), which fits into standard rack systems in cryobanks. The first iteration of this cryobox used in Australia only held 10 cryomeshes (not shown), but the more recent version holds 80 cryomeshes (Figure S1 ). The 3D‐printed inserts were designed to hold the cryomeshes with the ability to maintain some liquid nitrogen within the cryobox to help maintain temperature during handling and transfer into a permanent bank. 
 The vitrification of samples and loading into the cryobox were done in a liquid nitrogen‐safe container so that both the cryomesh sample and the cryobox remained fully immersed in liquid nitrogen throughout handling. Cryomeshes were vitrified in a strict vertical position to minimize the Leidenfrost effect [ 40 ]. Once boiling stopped, the cryomesh was maintained continuously under liquid nitrogen and transferred into a slot in the 3D‐printed insert so that it was not exposed to the air at any point. When the cryobox was full, the VWR cardboard sample box lid was labeled, placed in liquid nitrogen until temperature equilibration, secured to the cryobox, and then rapidly moved into temporary liquid nitrogen storage until shipment to a biorepository could be arranged. 
 Cryomesh is designed to facilitate a high heat‐transfer rate, so it is very sensitive to temperature change, and therefore it needs to be maintained constantly at cryogenic temperatures (i.e., below –150°C). Even though a vapor‐phase liquid nitrogen dewar can be used for permanent storage, because of their temperature sensitivity, we strongly advise against it. If kept in vapor phase for storage, the cryomeshes must be moved to liquid phase nitrogen during handling and transfer of samples to minimize transient warming from the surroundings. 2.8 
 Statistical Analysis 2.8.1 
 Volume 
 To examine changes in volume, we used the nonparametric Kruskal‐Wallis test with Dunn's post hoc multiple comparisons (separately per species) for L. scutaria , P. meandrina , D. cylindrus , D. labyrinthiformis, O. annularis , and sheared C. natans larvae. For larval volumes of M. angulosa , C. natans , P. strigosa , O. faveolata , and sheared P. strigosa , we used a one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc normality assumptions, and confirmed the results with Shapiro‐Wilk tests and diagnostic plots. Similarly, for both the sheared D. cylindrus and sheared M. angulosa larval volumes, ANOVA normality assumptions were verified with Welch's correction for unequal variances and Dunnett's T3 post hoc comparisons. Survival rates of settled normal‐size versus sheared larvae from both D. cylindrus and C. natans were analyzed with Fisher's Exact Test. 2.8.2 
 Toxicity Exposure 
 Percent survival for L. scutaria and D. cylindrus toxicity was analyzed as a function of development day and exposure time using a beta‐binomial generalized model with a logit link (initial binomial models showed substantial overdispersion), with the response specified as the number of individual larvae that survived or died per trial. Interaction and main effects were analyzed using Type II Wald chi‐square tests with Tukey‐adjusted post hoc pairwise comparisons, and model assumptions for both were assessed and verified using simulation‐based residual diagnostics. Similarly, P. meandrina toxicity percent survival was also analyzed with a beta‐binomial generalized linear model (logit link) using the number of survived or died larvae per trial as the response, exposure time as a fixed effect, verification of model assumptions, and post hoc comparison. The F. fungites toxicity survival was analyzed with a one‐way ANOVA, but due to the low sample size, violation of assumptions could not be assessed and limits inferences. 2.8.3 
 Cryopreservation Post‐Thaw Survival and Volume 
 Post‐thaw percent survival of cryopreserved L. scutaria larvae was analyzed as a function of two developmental days and three exposure times with a beta‐binomial generalized linear model with logit link, response as the number of larvae that survived versus died, and interaction, main effects, and post hoc tests as above. Additionally, percent survival of day five L. scutaria larvae (survived/died counts) was also analyzed with a beta‐binomial generalized linear model (logit link) using exposure time and warming temperature as predictors. As above, beta‐binomial models were selected due to overdispersion in the initial binomial models, and further model assumptions were verified with simulation‐based residual diagnostics and plots for both. The F. fungites post‐thaw survival was analyzed with a one‐way ANOVA, but as above, the low sample size limits model verification and interpretation (more on this below). Post‐thaw volumes for the D5 L. scutaria control larvae and 26°C and 50°C warmed larvae were analyzed with a Kruskal‐Wallis test with Dunn's post hoc multiple comparisons analysis. 
 GraphPad Prism software (version 11.0.2; San Diego, CA) was used for volume analyses, figures, and general descriptive statistics. Percent survival analyses for toxicity and cryopreservation were conducted in R (version 4.6.1; R Core Team, 2026) [ 41 ]. Beta‐binomial models were fit using the glmmTMB package (version 1.1.14). Model fit and residual diagnostics were assessed using the DHARMa package (version 0.5.0), including simulation‐based tests for overdispersion and outliers. Type II Wald chi‐square tests for fixed effects were conducted using the car package (version 3.1.5). Estimated marginal means and Tukey‐adjusted pairwise comparisons were obtained using the emmeans package (version 2.0.4), and compact letter displays summarizing groups of statistically indistinguishable levels were generated using the multcomp (version 1.4.31) and multcompView (version 0.1.12) packages. 3 
 Results 
 Cryomesh cryopreservation had been successfully demonstrated in coral larvae in only a single species [ 24 ]. We hypothesized that a key element of success is the amount of heat transfer possible using cryomesh, determined largely by the volume of the tissue, but certainly, the physiological response to the vitrification medium (toxicity) and ability to vitrify (no visible ice formation) on the mesh were important as well. In this study, we examined the larvae of 12 species. Here, we present data for four species with approximately the same larval volumes, each with different biological aspects and outcomes, to illustrate some of the important elements leading to successful cryomesh vitrification and post‐thaw survival. These species are: 1) two species of coral ( L. scutaria and F. fungites ) with aposymbiotic larvae (lacking photosynthetic symbionts) that successfully vitrified, warmed, and survived (i.e., progressive forward movement with intact morphology), 2) one coral species ( P. meandrina ) whose larvae have symbionts and which did vitrify but did not survive post‐thaw warming, and 3) one coral species ( D. cylindrus ) whose aposymbiotic larvae were sheared to a smaller size, below the target volume for vitrification, that also vitrified but did not successfully survive after warming. The remaining data for other species can be found in the Supporting Information files. 3.1

 Cryopreservation Post‐Thaw Survival and Volume 
 Both L. scutaria and F. fungites larvae survived vitrification, demonstrating normal, directional progressive movement and morphology at 24 h post‐thaw. Mean post‐thaw survival of D4 F. fungites larvae used three dehydration/rehydration combinations: 60 s:60 s, ∼50% survival; 120 s:60 s, ∼72% survival; and 60 s:120 s, ∼30% survival. A one‐way ANOVA did not find any significant difference among the three groups (n = 6 trials; F 2,3  = 2.259, p = 0.2521), but, again, the number of available larvae for these experiments was very limited (Table S3 and Figure  9B ). The small number of larvae that were available for this F. fungites cohort limited the number of trials needed to provide robust statistical analysis, yet those that were available survived the toxicity and cryopreservation process. Therefore, these data presented here should be considered primarily as an overview of a species that survived the entire process rather than the nuanced differences among dehydration and rehydration times. Additional testing is needed to optimize methods for a protocol.

 Modeling Vitrification and Rewarming Success Based on Volume 
 Based on the measured volume, we developed a thermal model to predict the cryomesh cooling in LN 2 or rewarming in rewarming solution (Figure  12A , see details in Supporting Information ). There are two processes of heat release: 1) convection heat transfer, which transfers the heat of the cryomesh and biosystem to liquid nitrogen (vitrification) or to the rewarming solution (rewarming), and 2) conduction heat transfer inside and between the cryomesh and biosystem. To describe the relative contributions to heat transfer, we analyzed a simplified thermal resistance model, which can be used to describe transient heat transfer in a model system. This model describes the heat flux ( q" ) during cooling, which will have a linear relation with the heat loss rate and thus cooling rate. The analysis primarily focused on rewarming rather than cooling because insufficient rewarming rates can lead to devitrification and intracellular ice formation during thawing, which is often one of the major limitations in vitrification‐based cryopreservation of larger and more complex biological systems [ 43 ]. The heat transfer analysis demonstrates that larval size is a dominant parameter governing achievable cooling and rewarming rates during vitrification‐based cryopreservation (Figure  12B and Figure S8 ).  The previous successful cryopreservation of F. fungites is used as a threshold for cooling (7.5 × 10 4 °C/min) and rewarming rate (1.4 × 10 5 °C/min), shown as a red dashed line (Figure  12 ). Larval species and development stages with a theoretical rewarming rate exceeding the threshold can be rewarmed without devitrification. Across all tested coral species (Figure  12B,C ), smaller larvae of L. scutaria (Figure S9A,B ) and P. meandrina (Figure S9C,D ) consistently exhibited substantially higher rewarming rates around 1.86 × 10 5 °C/min and 1.58 × 10 5 °C/min, respectively, as the lowest theoretical rewarming rate. In contrast, larger larvae showed a reduced rewarming rate compared with smaller larvae (Figure  12B ), which had a rewarming rate below the threshold. Reduced rewarming rate is due to increased thermal mass and longer characteristic heat diffusion distances (i.e., L 
 s and L 
 s,c ), consistent with the thermal resistance‐based predictions of the heat transfer model. 
 The model also demonstrated that mechanically sheared larvae generally achieved higher theoretical rewarming rates than non‐sheared larvae at equivalent developmental stages, due to their reduced effective volume and thermal resistance ( R 
 b ). Based on the heat transfer model, the sheared larvae with smaller volumes ( C. natans, D. cylindrus, and P. strigosa ) can achieve a rewarming rate that exceeds the threshold (Figure  12C and Figure S10 ). The theoretical rewarming rate of D. cylindrus at Day 1 increases from 8.41 × 10 4 °C/min to 1.47 × 10 5 °C/min at Day 1 – sheared, which is a 74% increase in rewarming rate and is beyond the threshold of the rewarming (Figure S10C ). When the temperature of the rewarming solution increases from 24°C–26°C to 50°C, the theoretical rewarming rate is also increased (Figure  12D ). 
 For larvae with a theoretical rewarming rate above the threshold, such as L. scutaria and P. meandrina , elevated warming temperatures may also reduce the required cryoprotective agent (CPA) concentration, thereby minimizing CPA‐associated toxicity while maintaining vitrification stability [ 44 ]. For species with a rewarming rate close to the threshold, increasing the temperature of the rewarming solution can raise it above the threshold. D. cylindrus at Day 4 sheared has an average rewarming rate of 1.28 × 10 5 °C/min at 24°C–26°C, while the average rewarming rate is 1.44 × 10 5 °C/min at 50°C, which is a 12.5% increase, and the rate is just above the threshold (Figure S10C,D ). A higher‐temperature rewarming solution can increase the rewarming rate by increasing the temperature gradient in Equation ( 1 ), thereby improving the heat flux rate. However, a few species of coral larvae with large volumes cannot reach the rewarming rate threshold even with a higher‐temperature rewarming solution or shearing embryos due to their large thermal mass (Figure S11 ), requiring other rewarming methods, such as laser rewarming [ 23 , 29 ], to further increase the rewarming rate. Overall, the heat transfer model results demonstrate that optimizing larval volume and external rewarming conditions are essential for scalable cryopreservation of coral larvae with different volumes. 3.5

 Discussion 
 In the marine ecosystem, cryopreservation is a nascent conservation tool that can help augment endangered coral populations. The first proof‐of‐concept studies demonstrated that cryopreserved coral sperm could produce coral larvae that successfully settled, signifying competency in a laboratory‐reared coral [ 45 ]. Subsequently, cryopreserved sperm from the brain coral Diploria labyrinthiformis were used to create coral larvae, and the developing larvae were settled and outplanted onto the Puerto Morelos Reef National Park [ 46 ], establishing that cryopreservation can be an active conservation tool. The work described here is another example of using cryopreservation to help reef populations by securing the genetic diversity of the endangered D. cylindrus , thereby reducing the extinction threat for this coral. Although we banked whole larvae of D. cylindrus , as methods advanced, we could thaw them differently to help ensure survival. Here we demonstrated that cryopreserved coral larvae showed normal morphology (though reduced in size) and swimming behavior post‐thaw, were competent to settle, and we also put larvae into a biorepository. Future experiments should include tracking post‐thaw settlement and growth of coral recruits in both an aquarium setting, as was done for the control (untreated) sheared larvae (Table  2 and Figure  5 ), and on the reef to monitor growth and maturation. 
 A global network of coral biorepositories could serve as an insurance policy for the ocean [ 28 ]. Because biodiversity is distributed among nations, such a system must respect national sovereignty while encouraging international cooperation. Regional facilities across the world's oceans could collect and store genetic material from local coral species, with redundant backups to ensure long‐term security. Table  3 shows the wide variety of locations where coral larvae were banked during these studies and supports the idea of having regional or nodal cryorepositories. With expanded funding, research, and coordination, conservationists could secure much of the remaining genetic diversity of reef‐building corals within years. There are more than 1000 species of Scleractinian coral; we cannot save them all. Difficult decisions about priorities will be necessary, but preserving as much genetic diversity as possible would make future restoration still feasible. 
 One of the main challenges for coral cryopreservation is having sufficient, high‐quality larvae that can withstand the stresses of the whole process. Reefs that have experienced multiple warming and subsequent bleaching events (and pressures from localized stressors) may not have reproductive material that is sufficiently robust to withstand the larval vitrification process. Larval quality and quantity may also be influenced by insufficient genetic diversity of parental colonies from which to collect gametes, desynchronized spawning that reduces the genotypes represented in the pool, poor fertilization success due to stress and disease, and population extirpation due to multiple stressors. While starting with robust, healthy larvae is undoubtedly important, other factors that support successful cryopreservation of coral larvae are larval volume and developmental stage, the concentration and type of cryoprotectant used, membrane permeability to water and cryoprotectants, toxicity to the cryoprotectants, the amount of fat in the larvae, symbiont status and menthol/light bleaching stress, husbandry differences, and the warming temperatures used. 
 A central result of the work presented here was that the small volume of the larvae alone did not predict success. For example, P. meandrina had small larvae and vitrified but did not survive to 24 h, and sheared D. cylindrus achieved smaller volumes that did not yield 24 h post‐thaw movement either. Across these studies, we assumed that the vitrification solution, PDT, permeated coral cells and tissues uniformly across all species. We made this assumption based on two factors: 1) PDT had already demonstrated early success in two fungiid species resulting in 24 h+ swimming, and 2) a relatively uniform freezing protocol using 10% DMSO successfully cryopreserves most species of coral sperm. However, this was likely not a reasonable assumption. When we looked more carefully, even though PDT produced swimming larvae in those two fungiid species, many of those larvae were fractured, suggesting that sub‐lethal ice formed, cracking but not killing them. This could have been caused by insufficient amounts of PDT entering the larvae, an inadequate warming rate, and/or devitrification in the rehydration medium; using a full‐strength vitrification solution instead of 0.5  m trehalose in FSW for the rapid warming step might offset the potential for devitrification. 
 We did test different exposure times to PDT to account for different larval volumes, but nevertheless, few of the larger‐volume species tested swam at 24 h. This may indicate that the equilibration times used were insufficient to achieve effective dehydration and cryoprotectant permeability in these larger larvae, but may also suggest that there are species differences in permeability that reduce the effectiveness of PDT in achieving vitrification. Moreover, larvae with symbionts had to be bleached, which added an additional stress, and husbandry differences or suboptimal larval rearing conditions may induce stress and reduce physiological fitness. This is particularly true for P. meandrina , which was difficult to maintain in larval culture. Finally, developmental age may also present a barrier. Lobactis scutaria were permeable to DMSO and PG on developmental D2‐D4, but by D5 they showed reduced permeability to those cryoprotectants [ 23 ]. Essential next steps should include toxicity measurements encompassing a suite of cryoprotectants in varying concentrations and exposure times, accompanied by shrink‐swell experiments to determine permeability rate; if successful vitrification and warming are achieved, those vitrified larvae need to settle and growth should be tracked. 
 This work is meant to serve as a helpful guide for the cryopreservation of other larval coral species. The first step in this process is to measure egg volume, which provides a sufficient approximation of larval volume and can be used to determine whether embryo shearing is required to bring that volume within the range required for cryomesh warming. For new species, this should be followed by exposure of larvae to a panel of cryoprotectants at a range of concentrations (e.g., 0.5–7  m ) to determine which of those may be suitable for use in a vitrification solution – more than likely, a suite of non‐toxic cryoprotectants forming a vitrification cocktail will be needed. Cryoprotectants showing minimal toxicity can then be tested in shrink‐swell experiments to determine the exposure times required to achieve cellular dehydration and penetration. Combinations of cryoprotectants with minimal toxicity can then be tested at a range of dehydration and rehydration times to assess vitrification solution toxicity, and finally, vitrification experiments can proceed with the refined vitrification solutions and exposure timings. Both toxicity and vitrification experiments should include post‐treatment assessment of larval morphology and swimming behavior, and if possible, the surviving larvae can also be settled and grown out to monitor the effects of cryoprotectant exposure on development during early life stages (Figure  13 ). As evidenced by the present study, this approach is not a guarantee of success but will generate a thorough understanding of the cryobiology of the target species and provide a basis for continued experimentation and protocol refinement. FIGURE 13 
 Flowchart of steps for coral larvae cryomesh vitrification. Knowing the egg/larval volume will help determine if a species is too large for cryomesh vitrification and if reducing the size (via shearing) is needed. Testing individual cryoprotectant (CPA) toxicity and shrink‐swell timing will guide which CPAs and concentrations should be explored for a vitrification solution. Testing the toxicity and vitrification success will refine the solution and protocol, and surviving larvae can then be assessed for morphology and monitored for any possible long‐term effects. 
 One of the challenging issues with these vitrification data was the fracturing of the coral larvae after vitrification that produced smaller, and possibly more, larvae than the untreated controls. While we did actively exclude small and irregular fragments, it is possible that the post‐thaw percent survival numbers may have been skewed by this, but it is likely consistent across all vitrification treatments. To better understand the effect of larval fracturing on survival numbers, we carefully counted and measured the volumes of hundreds of untreated controls and post‐thaw swimming larvae after vitrification. This yielded a robust distribution of the volumes produced by the cryopreservation processes and demonstrated that brief warming in a 50°C solution produced a median volume distribution closer to the controls than the 26°C warming (Figure  11 ). However, there was still a nearly 50% decrease in volume, and it did not address how to accurately assess post‐thaw survival numbers from vitrification. A future experimental goal to improve our overall vitrification process would aim for post‐thaw volumes that more closely match the control volume distribution. 
 In some species, successful vitrification was observed (i.e., no ice formed during freezing), and larvae showed movement at 2 h but then demonstrated no swimming at 24 h. The reasons for this are difficult to determine but might indicate that warming was not fast enough to prevent the formation of lethal ice crystals (i.e., devitrification). To investigate this, the embryos from some species with larger coral larvae were dissociated during early development to generate larvae with a reduced volume to improve heat transfer during warming. Cryopreservation of these smaller larvae did not result in swimming larvae at 24 h, indicating that other factors are involved in addition to the relationship between larval size and heat transfer. One possible important factor may be the amount of lipids in the larval tissues, since lipids play a role in the formation of intracellular ice [ 47 ]. Supporting this view were a handful of much larger O. faveolata larvae that were cryopreserved at 30+ days of age (i.e., when lipid reserves were depleted) and had fractured pieces swimming after 24 h post‐thaw. Further investigations are required to elucidate the roles of lipid content and larval age in the recovery of larvae from cryopreservation using cryomesh technology. 
 Importantly, reducing the volume of the coral larvae was not an impediment to their growth or settlement. Untreated D. cylindrus larvae that were sheared at the 2‐ to 8‐cell stage were able to settle, develop, and grow into normal mature coral (Figure  5 ). This suggests that the production of smaller larvae or pieces of swimming larvae, either by shearing or as a result of vitrification and warming, will not be an issue for future normal coral development and growth. Despite this, a better understanding of the impacts of shearing on the genetic distribution of the resulting larval culture, and whether there is more potential for chimerism due to the increased proportion of larvae with a high level of relatedness [ 48 ], would need to be determined before this approach could be implemented in a conservation setting. Finally, because some of these data were collected during a training workshop and our methods were not fully developed, there was some variability in the approaches taken to collect the data. The use of embryo dissociation to generate larvae for cryopreservation requires further work to standardize and improve the efficiency of the current methods but, nevertheless, may be key to the future success of larval cryopreservation and biobanking. 
 Currently, successful cryopreservation for a particular larval volume resides below the hatched line in Figure  1 . Differences in larval volume were observed across development days in some species, some of which was likely due to sample size while for others it may have been due to standing variation. However, in general, the egg volume provided a reasonable proxy for the expected size of the larvae. Therefore, when assessing future species for cryopreservation, the egg can be assessed quickly and easily as soon as the coral spawns, and further steps can be taken to shear the larvae early in development if needed. In some of the sheared larval batches, an increase in the measured larval size was observed at D3 and D4. The specific reason for this is unknown but may have been related to developmental changes such as gastrulation, fusion of sheared larvae during rearing that produced larger volumes, or sampling effects due to preferential selection of smaller larvae at earlier stages. Variability in the size of sheared D. cylindrus larvae, as well as the apparently higher lipid content of this species compared to others tested, may have been factors in the failure of cryopreservation in this treatment. Thus, care must be taken to ensure the use of uniformly sized sheared larvae on a particular date and, if possible, waiting until later developmental dates when lipid reserves may be depleted, potentially leading to increased cryopreservation success (as in O. faveolata ). 
 The heat transfer modeling results highlight the critical role of larval size and geometry in cryomesh vitrification‐based cryopreservation. Larger larvae exhibited lower theoretical rewarming rates due to increased thermal resistance, increasing the risk of devitrification during rewarming. In contrast, mechanically sheared larvae achieved substantially higher rewarming rates because of their reduced effective volume and smaller thermal mass. The model also demonstrated that increasing the external rewarming temperature significantly improved rewarming rate across all species and developmental stages. Overall, these findings emphasize that optimizing larval size and heat transfer conditions are essential for scalable cryopreservation of coral larvae, potentially enabling reduced CPA concentrations and lower toxicity. 
 It is essential to focus on specific critical parameters to improve the achievable cooling rate, such as using materials with higher thermal conductivity for the cryomesh. Additionally, thermal mass can be reduced by using thinner wire diameters and minimizing the nitrogen vapor barrier through vertical plunging to allow the release of the vapor barrier from the cryomesh. Increased cooling rates enable the use of lower cryoprotectant concentrations, lowering the associated toxicity, which can help facilitate the cryopreservation of biosystems that have not yet been preserved due to susceptibility to cryoprotectants, such as coral larvae [ 24 ]. With the improved cooling rate and vertical plunge, uniform cooling was achieved for larger cryomeshes (e.g., 15 × 4 cm) by using thermally conductive materials and optimized cryomesh design [ 24 ]. The scale‐up achieved by conduction‐dominated cryomesh paves the way to cryopreserve a wide range of organisms in greater quantities. 5 
 Conclusions 
 A roadmap to cryopreserve coral larvae was presented using conduction‐mediated cryomesh technology that is easy to make and use under field conditions. The volumes of eggs and embryos from many species of coral were measured as one factor that may help determine the efficacy of heat transfer on the cryomesh. Only coral larvae from two fungiid species that were aposymbiotic and had a volume of ∼0.002 mm 3 demonstrated post‐thaw swimming at 24 h. Small volume larvae with symbionts ( P. meandrina ) were cryopreserved with little success, possibly because of the stress of the bleaching process to remove the symbionts, the husbandry techniques to rear the larvae, poor cryoprotectant permeability or cryoprotectant toxicity. Heat transfer modeling further demonstrated that reduced larval volume significantly increased achievable rewarming rates by shortening conductive heat transfer distances, supporting the improved cryopreservation performance observed in smaller or mechanically sheared larvae. Cryopreservation of D. cylindrus larvae sheared at the 2‐ to 8‐cell stage to produce smaller larval fragments ∼0.002 mm 3 did not produce post‐thaw swimming larvae for potentially many reasons; however, these smaller untreated fragments were competent to settle and mature into normal coral. Therefore, as this cryopreservation process advances, reducing larval size by embryonic dissociation to improve heat transfer would not be an impediment to producing competent larvae with the potential to use these smaller post‐thaw larvae in restoration efforts. 
 Next steps to improve this cryopreservation process should include examining the permeability and toxicity of different cryoprotectants, conducting shrink‐swell experiments to understand water and cryoprotectant permeability and toxicity across many species and larval sizes, improving husbandry to allow the larvae or fragments to use up much of their fats without impacting their overall health and survival, and using a wide variety of improved heat transfer techniques and increased warming to improve overall vitrification and ultimately conservation success.
