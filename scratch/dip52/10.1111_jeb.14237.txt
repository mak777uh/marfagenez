Abstract 
10 
The continued existence of sex despite many costs it entails still lacks an adequate 
11 
explanation. Previous experimental studies demonstrated that sex enhances the rate of 
12 
adaptation in changing environments. To the best of our knowledge, no experimental study 
13 
that investigated the effects of sex on adaptation has manipulated the rate of environmental 
14 
change, which is negatively correlated with the probability of population survival. Since the 
15 
patterns of adaptation (adaptive walk) depend on the rate of environmental change, the 
16 
overall effects of sex may also be altered by this factor. 
17 
To investigate the interplay of sex and the rate of environmental deterioration, we 
18 
carried out a long-term selection experiment with a unicellular alga (Chlamydomonas 
19 
reinhardtii), by manipulating mode of reproduction (asexual or sexual populations) and the 
20 
rate of environmental deterioration (an increase of salt concentration). We monitored both 
21 
the population size and extinction dynamics and estimated the probability of evolutionary 
22 
rescue.

1. Introduction 
38 
Despite the fact that the great majority of extant eukaryotes are sexual, sex still 
39 
remains one of the most intriguing puzzles in evolutionary biology (Otto and Lenormand, 
40 
2002). The reason for this reflects in the numerous disadvantages and costs of sex (Lewis, 
41 
1987; Lehtonen et al., 2012). These costs can be significant and hence the continued 
42 
existence of sex implies large potential benefits. Unexpected difficulty to provide an 
43 
explanation for such a widespread phenomenon brought sex the title of “the Queen of 
44 
problems in evolutionary biology” (Bell, 1982). 
45 
Most contemporary hypotheses explaining the maintenance of sex by natural 
46 
selection are based on the idea that sex increases genetic variability of the progeny 
47 
(Kondrashov, 1993). This effect, in turn, increases the response of selection and thus, 
48 
facilitates the rate of adaptation to novel environments (Weismann, 1889; Fisher, 1930;

Muller, 1932; Burt, 2000). The advantageous effect of sex based on this concept is 
50 
manifested through faster accumulation of mutations beneficial in changing conditions in 
51 
sexual populations, which increases the rate of adaptation relative to asexual populations. In 
52 
asexual populations, which may fix novel mutations at a slower rate (one at the time), 
53 
adaptation proceeds at a lower rate or can be limited by competition of individuals carrying 
54 
different mutations (termed clonal interference) (Muller 1932; Gerrish and Lenski, 1998). 
55 
A substantial body of experimental evidence indicates that sex increases the rate of 
56 
adaptation to changing environments (Hartfield and Keightley, 2012). The advantageous 
57 
effect of sex is manifested through faster assembling of mutations beneficial in novel 
58 
environments (Kaltz and Bell, 2002), reduction of clonal interference in large populations 
59 
(Colegrave, 2002), an increase in fitness of the progeny (Becks and Agrawal, 2012) and 
60 
more efficient adaptation to the presence of pathogenic species (Morran et al., 2009). 
61 
Furthermore, sex increases the rate of adaptation in a deteriorating environment (Goddard et 
62 
al., 2005) and the probability of survival in conditions lethal for the ancestral population 
63 
(Lachapelle and Bell, 2012), termed evolutionary rescue (Gomulkiewicz and Holt, 1995). 
64 
 
65 
While the benefits of sex in novel and deteriorating environments are relatively well 
66 
understood, to the best of our knowledge, no experimental studies which investigated the 
67 
effects of sex have manipulated the rate of environmental change, which is negatively 
68 
correlated with the probability of population survival in stressful environments (Perron et al., 
69 
2007; Bell and Gonzales, 2011; Ramsayer et al., 2013; Lindsey et al., 2013). Adaptation 
70 
occurs through sequential fixation of beneficial mutations (adaptive walk) (Collins et al., 
71 
2007), a process which patterns depend on the rate of environmental change (Collins and de 
72 
Meaux, 2009). Since sex directly impacts the adaptive walk through accumulation of 
73 
beneficial mutations, the overall effects of it may be altered by different rates of 
74 
environmental change.

The higher rates of environmental deterioration imply a larger initial maladaptedness 
77 
of a population and adaptation may occur through strong selection, which causes a 
78 
demographic pressure manifested through higher proportion of selective deaths (Haldane, 
79 
1957; Burger and Lynch, 1995). Furthermore, adaptation to the higher rates of environmental 
80 
change occurs through fixation of few mutations of larger fitness effects (Collins and de 
81 
Meaux, 2009), which are less frequent in the distribution of beneficial mutations (Kassen and 
82 
Bataillon, 2006; Eyre-Walker and Keightley, 2007). Consequently, if population size 
83 
substantially declines, the mutations which increase the fitness under the higher rates of 
84 
environmental deterioration may appear in disadvantageous genetic background (with 
85 
deleterious alleles) due to genetic drift (Otto and Lenormand, 2002). As a result, adaptation 
86 
of an asexual population may become impeded and the population may face extinction. The 
87 
potential beneficial effect of sex under these conditions is the release of these mutations 
88 
from disadvantageous genetic background, which may restore the adaptive walk of the 
89 
population. However, since demographic cost of adaptation imposes the limit for the rate of 
90 
environmental change that a population can sustain (Lynch and Lande, 1993), the beneficial 
91 
effect of sex may diminish if the magnitude of environmental change outweighs the 
92 
reproductive capacity of a species to compensate for the demographic loss. 
93 
Adaptation to a gradual rate of environmental change occurs through fixation of 
94 
mutations of smaller fitness effects, which are more frequent in the distribution of beneficial 
95 
mutations (Eyre-Walker and Keightley, 2007). In addition, a population subjected to the 
96 
gradual rate of environmental deterioration will likely suffer a less pronounced population size 
97 
decline than a population subjected to the higher rate of environmental change and hence 
98 
the supply of beneficial mutations will be proportionally higher. However, due to weaker 
99 
selection operating under the gradual environmental change, these mutations may not reach 
100 
fixation, or otherwise fix in less predictable intervals (a process termed periodic fixation) 
101 
(Collins and de Meaux, 2009). If two or more mutations, which are beneficial under the 
102 
gradual rates of environmental deterioration, segregate in an asexual population, adaptation

104

105

106

107 
In order to investigate the interplay of sex and the rate of environmental deterioration 
108 
on adaptation to changing conditions, we carried out a selection experiment by subjecting the 
109 
experimental populations of a unicellular alga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii to the environment 
110 
which progressively deteriorated and ultimately became lethal for the ancestral populations. 
111 
We manipulated both their mode of reproduction and the rate of environmental deterioration 
112 
in a fully factorial design and monitored the extinction dynamics and the occurrence of 
113 
evolutionary rescue events within the treatment groups.  
114 
We predicted that the sign of the effect of sex (slower extinction rate and a 
115 
higher probability of evolutionary rescue of the sexual populations) will not depend on the 
116 
rate of environmental deterioration, despite the potential differences in the patterns of the 
117 
effects of sex under different rates of environmental change. Furthermore, we predicted that 
118 
the asexual populations will adapt less efficiently under the higher rates of environmental 
119 
change, hence the magnitude of the effect of sex (the relative advantage of sexual 
120 
populations) will be positively correlated with the rate of environmental deterioration.

2.2.   Selection experiment 
140 
Treatments 
141 
 
142 
The experimental populations were subjected to a deteriorating environment, which 
143 
comprised an increase of NaCl (hereafter referred to as salt) level in the medium in regular 
144 
intervals, at three different rates. The salt was chosen as a stressor since previous 
145 
experiments (Lachapelle and Bell, 2012; Lachapelle et al., 2015) demonstrated the 
146 
detrimental effect of elevated salt concentration on C. reinhardtii populations, manifested 
147 
through osmotic and ionic disbalance in cells (Lachapelle et al., 2015).  
148 
We manipulated the life cycle of C. reinhardtii by shifting between a stage during 
149 
which all the populations were reproducing mitotically (hereafter referred to as the asexual 
150 
stage) and a stage of sexual reproduction. The asexual stage consisted of 4 growth cycles 
151 
(3-4 days each), during which the populations underwent approximately 4-6 rounds of mitotic 
152 
divisions per growth cycle (9-12 doublings of cells). During this stage, salt level remained 
153 
constant. After completion of the asexual stage, a sexual cycle has been initiated in all the 
154 
populations, but completed only in the sexual populations, due to presence of both mating 
155 
types (see „Sexual cycle‟ section below for details). After completion of each sexual cycle,

Cultivation and Transfer Procedure  
184 
All experimental populations were cultivated in 24-well microtiter plates in Bold‟s 
185 
basal medium (2 ml per culture), widely used for algal cultivation (Harris, 2009), under 
186 
standard conditions (26°C, 100 μE illumination, constantly shaken at 180 rpm (3-mm rotation 
187 
diameter), covered with sterile breathable membranes to prevent uneven evaporation across 
188 
the plates and cross-contamination). A serial passage was performed after completion of 
189 
each growth cycle by transferring 5 % of each population to a fresh medium.  
190 
Sexual Cycle   
191 
After completion of the asexual stage, sexual populations were allowed to undergo a 
192 
sexual cycle, using the following protocol. All the populations were centrifuged at 5000 rpm 
193 
for 10 minutes and re-suspended in nitrogen-free medium to initiate gametogenesis. The 
194 
populations were then incubated in standard conditions (without shaking) under bright light 
195 
for 24 hours to allow for mating of gametes and formation of zygotes. After given period, the 
196 
microtiter plates containing the experimental populations were wrapped in aluminium foil and 
197 
incubated in the darkness for additional 4-5 days to allow the zygotes to mature. Since sub-
198 
lethal stress may affect the mutation rate, the effects of sex may be confounded with the 
199 
effects of mutations (Goho and Bell, 2000; Colegrave et al., 2002). Hence, the nitrogen 
200 
starvation treatment was also applied to the asexual populations, but since they were made 
201 
up of single mating types, no mating took place. After incubation in the darkness, the 
202 
microtiter plates with mature zygotes were placed in a freezer for 4 hours at -20˚C in order to 
203 
eliminate unmated gametes. The zygotes develop the additional layers of cell wall during 
204 
maturation in the darkness, thereby acquiring resistance to stressful conditions (Harris, 
205 
2009). The pilot experiments carried out in our laboratory revealed that this characteristic 
206 
enables the zygotes to withstand low temperature. Since unmated gametes lack resistance 
207 
to freezing, this procedure was omitted for the asexual and facultative sexual groups, and the 
208 
transfer of both the produced zygotes and unmated gametes to the next asexual stage was

244

315

Population size dynamics  
317 
 
318 
To estimate the effect of mode of reproduction and the rate of environmental 
319 
deterioration on population size dynamics, we regularly monitored population size of 
320 
experimental populations by measuring the optical density of the cultures after every second 
321 
growth cycle. The measurements were carried out up to and including the 20th growth cycle. 
322 
We estimated that experimental populations of C. reinhardtii underwent approximately 100 
323 
generations of cell divisions during this interval of time.  
324 
Mode of reproduction significantly affected the rate of decline of mean population size 
325 
(two-way ANOVA; F2,201 = 102.26, P < 0.00001). The highest decline of mean population size 
326 
was recorded in the asexual group, having been by 42 % and 36 % higher than that of the 
327 
obligate sexual and facultative sexual group, respectively (Figure 1A). Post-hoc Tukey‟s HSD 
328 
analysis revealed statistically significant difference (P < 0.00001) between the asexual group 
329 
and both the obligate sexual and facultative sexual group. No significant difference between 
330 
the sexual groups of populations was detected (P = 0.07).  
331 
 
332 
The rate of environmental deterioration was positively correlated with the rate of 
333 
decline of mean population size (two-way ANOVA; F2,201 = 102.99, P < 0.00001) (Figure 1B). 
334 
The decline of population size was lowest in the treatment group subjected to the gradual 
335 
rate of salt increase, and significantly lower than that of the treatment groups subjected to  
336 
the moderate rate and high rate of environmental deterioration (by 39 % and 47 %, 
337 
respectively)  (Tukey‟s HSD, P < 0.00001). The treatment group subjected to the moderate 
338 
rate of salt increase had significantly lower decline of population size than that of the 
339 
treatment group subjected to the high rate of salt increase (by 12 %) (Tukey‟s HSD, P = 
340 
0.001).

360 
 
361

362

363 
growth cycle to record the potential extinction events. An experimental population was 
364

5. 
Discussion 
507 
The objective of this study was to investigate the interplay of mode of reproduction 
508 
and the rate of environmental deterioration on extinction dynamics and the probability of 
509 
evolutionary rescue of C. reinhardtii experimental populations, in environment deteriorating at 
510 
different rates. The main prediction of the experiment was an increase of relative advantage 
511 
of sexual populations under higher rates of environmental deterioration. 
512 
Overall, sexual mode of reproduction was beneficial. Sex significantly reduced both 
513 
the rate of population size decline and the extinction rate with respect to salt concentration, 
514 
and increased the probability of evolutionary rescue. The sexual groups combined had 39% 
515 
lower decline of population size relative to the asexual group. The obligate sexual group had 
516 
significantly slower extinction rate than that of the asexual group, as reflected in higher 
517 
number of surviving populations during exposure to salt concentrations between 12 g/l 
518 
(under which first extinctions had occurred) and 30 g/l. Finally, the obligate sexual group had 
519 
the highest proportion of evolutionary rescue events and the fastest growth rate of the 
520 
rescued populations. 
521 
We detected the interaction between mode of reproduction and the rate of 
522 
environmental deterioration on both the rate of population size decline and the extinction rate 
523 
(with respect to both time in growth cycles and salt concentration). Under the moderate rate 
524 
of environmental deterioration, the obligate sexual group had the lowest population size 
525 
decline and significantly slower extinction rate (with respect to time in growth cycles) than 
526 
that of the asexual group. The facultative sexual group had the slowest extinction rate (with 
527 
respect to salt concentration) under the high rate of environmental deterioration. 
528 
This experiment demonstrated that the sign of the effect of sex remains constant 
529 
irrespective of the rate of environmental deterioration (the advantage of at least one or both 
530 
sexual groups relative to the asexual group), as predicted. Furthermore, we detected that the 
531 
relative advantage of sex (slower extinction rate) increased with the rate of environmental

542

543

545

546

547

548

549

550

551

552

553

554

555

595

596

600

601

602

603

604

605

606

607

608

609

610

611
