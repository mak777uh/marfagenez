Abstract 1 
Refining the energetic costs of cellular maintenance is essential for predicting microbial growth 2 
and survival in the environment.  Here, we evaluate a simple batch culture method to quantify 3 
energy partitioning between growth and maintenance using microcalorimetry and thermodynamic 4 
modeling.  The constants derived from the batch culture system were comparable to those that 5 
have been reported from meta-analyses of data derived from chemostat studies.  The model 6 
accurately predicted temperature dependent biomass yield and the upper temperature limit of 7 
growth for Desulfovibrio alaskensis G20, suggesting the method may have broad application.  An 8 
Arrhenius temperature dependence for the specific energy consumption rate, inferred from 9 
substrate consumption and heat evolution, was observed over the entire viable temperature 10 
range.  By combining this relationship for specific energy consumption rate and observed specific 11 
growth rates, the model describes an increase in non-growth associated maintenance at higher 12 
temperatures and the corresponding decrease in energy available for growth.  This analytical and 13 
thermodynamic formulation suggests that simply monitoring heat evolution in batch culture could 14 
be a useful complement to the recognized limitations of estimating maintenance using 15 
extrapolation to zero growth in chemostats. 16

Introduction 18 
Processes governed by the activities of microorganisms in environmental and engineered 19 
systems are determined by their capacity to extract sufficient energy and nutrients for both 20 
synthesis of new cellular material (growth) and preservation of cellular viability.  It has been 21 
generally assumed that organisms adapt to suboptimal environmental conditions by increasing 22 
their investment in maintaining viability at the cost of reduced growth  (Tijhuis, Van Loosdrecht, & 23 
Heijnen, 1993; Turkarslan et al., 2017; Varela, Baez, & Agosin, 2004).  Therefore, cellular viability 24 
is bounded by the physical and chemical conditions where the energetic costs needed to remain 25 
viable do not exceed a critical threshold of energy recovery.  That range of conditions permissive 26 
to viability in the absence of biotic competition, as represented by the pure culture in 27 
 
 
3 
 
microbiology, define an organism’s fundamental niche.  Thus, an overarching goal in microbial 1 
ecology and biotechnology has been to develop a predictive understanding of how individual cells 2 
and systems of interacting cells balance the partitioning of biologically available energy between 3 
growth and viability as environmental conditions vary (Hoehler & Jorgensen, 2013). 4 
Microbial nutrient consumption and growth has been described using a variety of models detailing 5 
growth rate, biomass yield on a substrate, decay rate, and substrate costs to maintain viability 6 
(hereafter maintenance costs) (Beeftink, van der Heijden, & Heijnen, 1990; Marr, Nilson, & Clark, 7 
1963; Pirt, 1965, 1982).  While numerous studies have evaluated the utility of these models 8 
(LaRowe, Dale, Amend, & Van Cappellen, 2012; van Bodegom, 2007; Wang & Post, 2012), the 9 
Pirt model (Pirt, 1965) is used here due to its simplicity and application across a variety of isolates 10 
and conditions.   11

Briefly, the Pirt model describes microbial substrate consumption (qs) as increasing with the 14 
specific growth rate (µ) while the growth associated biomass yield (Y; hereafter growth 15 
associated cost, GAC) and maintenance costs (m; hereafter non-growth associated maintenance, 16 
NGAM) remain constant. Furthermore, this model has been widely applied to traditional methods 17 
for physiological estimation, such as growth rates quantified using chemostats (Harder, 1997; 18 
Muller et al., 2021) and “zero growth” metabolic states established in retentostats (Lin, 19 
Westerhoff, & Röling, 2009; Müller & Babel, 1996).  This breadth of applications has generated a 20 
data set against which to benchmark new approaches for parameter estimation (Tijhuis et al., 21 
1993).   22 
Here, we propose a simple experimental approach to infer energy partitioning between growth 23 
and maintenance for microorganisms growing under conventional conditions in batch culture 24 
(Figure 1).   We used microcalorimetric measurements of heat evolution during early batch 25 
 
 
4 
 
culture growth to quantify energy expenditures of a sulfate-reducing microorganism growing 1 
under increasing thermal stress.  Heat evolution aggregates all growth and non-growth processes 2 
contributing to Gibbs energy dissipation (Gustafsson, 1991).  In addition, we quantified both the 3 
heat evolution and the changes in chemical and biological state associated with microbial activity 4 
(von Stockar & Liu, 1999) to assess not only enthalpy measured as heat but also the contribution 5 
of metabolic product and biomass formation to an increase in system entropy.  These data were 6 
then used to model energy partitioning as a function of thermal stress for Desulfovibrio alaskensis 7 
G20, a well-characterized sulfate-reducing microorganism (Wall, Rapp-Giles, & Rousset, 1993).   8 
The model accurately predicted the impact of thermal stress on yield and the maximum growth 9 
temperature of the organism.  In addition, we compared our observations and parameters to 10 
those reported previously (Figure 2) and found close agreement between both the range and 11 
trends (Calabrese, Chakrawal, Manzoni, & Van Cappellen, 2021; Smeaton & Van Cappellen, 12 
2018).   Given the consistency between modeled and quantified growth, and agreement with 13 
parameters determined using independent methods, we anticipate this simple experimental 14 
approach could complement current chemostat-based models by 1) incorporating 15 
microcalorimetric measurement of heat evolution to provide a real-time assessment of metabolic 16 
activity, 2) demonstrating an Arrhenius dependence of specific energy consumption rate over the 17 
entire physiological temperature range, 3) defining the temperature dimension of the fundamental 18 
niche using maximum growth rates as opposed to the inferred maxima of chemostat-based 19 
approaches, and 4) predicting yield and growth rate behavior at lower growth rates than can be 20 
imposed by nutrient availability in chemostats. 21

General experimental approach.  Desulfovibrio alaskensis G20 was grown in precultures and 24

Strain, medium, culturing and microcalorimetry.  D. alaskensis G20 was maintained as freezer 5 
stock originating from Meyer et al. (Meyer et al., 2013).  Cultures of D. alaskensis G20 were 6 
grown at 37 °C in a CCMA-based medium (Hillesland & Stahl, 2010) with the following 7 
composition: 30 mM Na2SO4 (electron acceptor), 30 mM sodium DL-lactate (electron donor, 8 
limiting nutrient), 39 mM NaCl, 27 mM MgCl2·6H2O, 0.95 mM CaCl2·2H2O, 9.3 mM NH4Cl, 1.3 9 
mM KCl, 1.1 mM of K2HPO4 and 25 mM NaHCO3.  The medium included 1 ml l-1 of a trace 10 
element solution, 1 ml l-1 of a vitamin solution, 1 mM sodium sulfide as reductant and 4.0 µM 11 
sodium resazurin as redox indicator as described by Hillesland and Stahl (Hillesland & Stahl, 12 
2010).  The growth experiments were conducted at 20, 30, 35, 37, 40, 45 and 50 °C and pH ~7.2. 13

The TAM III Nanocalorimeter (TA instruments) was operated as described elsewhere (Turkarslan 15 
et al., 2017).  D. alaskensis G20 was grown at 37 °C and 300 rpm shaking on an orbital table to 16 
early stationary phase.  All subsequent manipulations were done in a glovebox flushed with a N2-17

chemical speciation of the media were calculated with PHREEQC and the enthalpies of the 23 
relevant reactions were within the error of the experimental replicates.  The remainder of the 24 
seeding culture was used for analyte sampling (metabolites, cell counts, and sulfide 25 
measurement, as described below).  The seeding culture was further incubated without shaking 26

Analytical methods.  Dissolved hydrogen sulfide was measured as described by Cline (Cline, 4 
1969).  Sulfate consumption was measured with a ready-made photometric assay (No. 984648) 5

861, Thermo Fisher Scientific).  Lactate and acetate were measured on a Dionex ICS 5000+ 7

min at maximum speed in a tabletop centrifuge (Centrifuge 5414D, Eppendorf) before 1:10 9

were separated on an ion-exclusion column IonPac ICE AS6 (Thermo Fisher Dionex) with 0.5 11 
mM heptafluorobutyric acid (MP Biomedicals) as eluent at 30 °C.  The isocratic flow was set to 1 12 
ml min-1, the injection loop had a delay volume of 125 µl which was flushed with sample once.  13 
Ion suppression was performed with 5 mM tetrabutyl ammonium hydroxide (Thermo Fisher 14

quantified within Chromeleon 7.2.2.6668 (Thermo Fisher Dionex). 18

Determination of Gibbs energy consumption.  Microbial metabolism can be represented by 13 
simplified chemical equations to determine Gibbs energy consumption during growth.  Two 14

growth implicitly with only the catabolic reaction (Reaction 1, lactate oxidation with sulfate 16

Heijnen & Van Dijken, 1992).  Growth represented with only the catabolic equation is described 20 
below while the combined anabolic and catabolic approach is described in the supplementary 21 
material.  Growth was conducted under lactate limited conditions to minimize possible 22 
accumulation of reduced intermediates, including hydrogen and formate, and constrain the overall 23 
catabolic reaction. 24

Arrhenius temperature dependence of specific lactate consumption, specific heat evolution, and 8 
specific energy consumption rate.  Most biotic and abiotic rates follow the Arrhenius relationship 9 
for temperature; therefore, it follows that the rate limiting process in a cell would similarly follow 10 
that relationship.  Previous work has proposed an Arrhenius relationship for growth rate up to the 11

Sagemann et al., 1998).  Furthermore, the specific energy consumption rate is related to the 17

of a maximum growth temperature.  D. alaskensis G20 displayed an increase in growth rate from 21 
20 to 40 °C, with a sharp decrease at 45 °C (Figure 4B).  Growth did not occur at 50 °C and was 22 
not observed following transfer to a lower temperature.  No growth was detected 15 days after 23 
transfer from 50 °C to 37 °C, indicating loss of viability.  The highest growth rate was recorded at 24

Discussion  12 
Adaptive energy partitioning by an organism governs both the ability of that organism to respond 13 
to stresses and to sustain viability.  Previous reports of growth rate dependence on temperature 14 
have shown a similar Arrhenius temperature dependence up to the maximum growth rate for an 15 
organism (Knoblauch & Jørgensen, 1999; Price & Sowers, 2004; Sagemann et al., 1998).  Here 16 
we develop a general model formulation that describes and predicts the increase in maintenance 17 
energy demand as our model organism adapts to higher growth temperatures (Figure 4A).  The 18 
model infers an increase in maintenance costs with temperature that accurately predicted the 19 
temperature at which viability is lost as the maintenance demand overwhelms the capacity for 20 
adaptive response (Figure 4C).  Although previous studies suggesting that maintenance 21 
requirements increase with an Arrhenius relationship are generally in line with our analysis, those 22 
studies reported a much lower temperature dependence (ENGAM = 69-110 kJ mole-1) than our 23 
determination of 234 kJ mole-1.   However, the nonlinear response of maintenance energy costs 24 
to temperature makes determination and subsequent comparisons of ENGAM numerically and 25 
technically difficult beyond “order of magnitude” (see Supplementary Table S7).  By further 26 
developing a model of energy partitioning, these analyses thus provide an improved analytical 27 
 
 
15 
 
framework for defining the temperature dimension of an organism’s fundamental niche, here 1 
predicting a maximum growth temperature of 47 °C for Desulfovibrio alaskensis G20.  2 
Furthermore, batch culturing directly determines the boundary conditions reaching maximum 3 
growth rates whereas chemostats only approximate this condition due to washout (Figure 5). 4 
The model presented here combined the principles of temperature-dependent kinetics and 5 
microbial energy partitioning to describe batch growth using principles established for chemostat 6 
growth (Heijnen & Van Dijken, 1992; Tijhuis et al., 1993).  While growth conditions differ between 7 
chemostat and batch cultures, the energy requirements and responses to temperature reported 8 
here are similar to those reported for chemostat analyses of other organisms.  For example, the 9 
Gibbs energy consumption calculated by the empirical formula of Heijnen and Van Dijken 10 
(Heijnen & Van Dijken, 1992) and employed in the model of Tijhuis et al. (Tijhuis et al., 1993) for 11 
chemostat growth (there, Gibbs energy dissipated is originally abbreviated as (𝐷𝑆 01/𝑟𝑎𝑥)𝑔𝑟 in 12 
formula 1a and interpreted as the energy required for growth (Tijhuis et al., 1993)) is in the same 13 
order of magnitude as the energy consumption requirements for growth (GAC) calculated by our 14 
batch-based approach (347 kJ mole-1 vs 372 kJ mole-1, Supplementary Table S7).  Meta-15 
analyses presented by Tijhuis et al. 1993 and Calabrese et al. 2021 aggregated and normalized 16 
data derived from a variety of organisms and metabolisms.  Metabolic types were normalized by 17 
Calabrese et al. by collapsing the energy requirements to a “thermodynamic efficiency” 18 
comparable across metabolisms and organisms.  However, Tijhuis et al. also suggested that a 19 
more refined analysis should consider a separate relationship for cultures grown aerobically and 20 
anaerobically, since the electron donor and acceptor can change the energy requirements of a 21 
cell.  Although our analysis of a specific metabolic type indicated only a minimal impact of 22 
anabolic processes on the maintenance term, additional observations are warranted for 23 
organisms and metabolisms that invest a larger fraction of energy in anabolic processes.  24 
The principle of using heat evolved per biomass formed to characterize microbial yield in a 25 
defined growth environment extends classical yield-based analyses of microbial growth, since it 26 
 
 
16 
 
relies on the direct measurement of heat evolution and biomass formation.  As such, we and 1 
others have demonstrated the utility of this proxy to evaluate the impact of different environmental 2 
stresses on yield, such as metal toxicity (Gibbons et al., 2011; McTee et al., 2013), nutrient 3 
limitation (Robador, LaRowe, Finkel, Amend, & Nealson, 2018), osmotic stress (Zhou et al., 4 
2017), and simulated environmental dynamics (Turkarslan et al., 2017).  In particular, heat 5 
evolved per biomass was successfully used to demonstrate the fitness trade-offs associated with 6 
maintaining regulation in a varying resource environment (Turkarslan et al., 2017) as well as the 7 
reduction of maintenance costs of D. vulgaris Hildenborough adapted to higher salinities through 8 
experimental evolution (Zhou et al., 2017).  All these experiments illustrate that in comparison to 9 
more favorable conditions, additional energy expenditures are required under stressed conditions 10 
and that the accompanying heat production reflects the underlying adaptive metabolism, instead 11 
of heat being solely proportional to the number of actively growing cells (Robador et al., 2018). 12 
We acknowledge that although our modeled and quantified growth and energy expenditures are 13 
consistent, our model assumes that growth costs are independent of temperature and that 14 
maintenance costs vary with an Arrhenius relationship.  These assumptions and the 15 
generalization of method require further testing, including response to different classes of stress 16 
(e.g., salinity and pH) and further comparisons to alternative approaches used to infer 17 
maintenance costs and energy partitioning as shown in Figure 2 (Calabrese et al., 2021; van 18 
Bodegom, 2007).  However, considering the relatively simple experimental format, we anticipate 19 
this approach could be of general utility for advancing predictive modeling of biotic and abiotic 20 
factors controlling the distribution and activity of microbes in the environment. 21
