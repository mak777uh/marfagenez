Abstract Septation and cell separation occur as distinct events during cell division in many Gram-positive bacteria. The process first involves synthesis of a complete, multilayered peptidoglycan (PG) septum dividing the cell, which is subsequently hydrolyzed to facilitate physical separation. Using fluorescent D-amino acids and high-resolution microscopy, we identify a previously unrecognized, post-septational wave of transpeptidation that crosslinks septal PG during cell separation in Bacillus subtilis . Notably, this activity does not involve new PG synthesis, but instead remodels pre-existing septal PG. The transpeptidase PBPH plays a key role in this process, and its activity and localization at the separating septum depend on PG hydrolysis by the endopeptidase LytF. Disruption of this interplay impairs cell separation. Our findings reveal a mechanism whereby the coordinated activities of PG hydrolysis and transpeptidation ensure successful cytokinesis. This work expands the current model of cell division by identifying post-septational transpeptidation as a key step in septal resolution and pole formation. Introduction Cell division is a fundamental biological process that enables the propagation of life. In bacteria, this involves two key events: the formation of the septum, which partitions the mother cell, and cell separation, which splits the mother cell into two daughter cells. Division begins with the recruitment of FtsZ, a tubulin homolog, which polymerizes into the Z ring at the midcell 1 . The Z ring then coordinates the assembly of the divisome, a protein complex responsible for synthesizing septal peptidoglycan (PG), the primary structural component of the septum and the bacterial cell wall 2 , 3 . Following septation, newly synthesized septal PG is cleaved by PG hydrolysis 4 , 5 , physically separating the daughter cells. The specifics of septation and cell separation vary among bacterial species. In most Gram-negative bacteria like Escherichia coli , septal synthesis and hydrolysis are spatiotemporally intertwined: as septation proceeds inwards, hydrolases simultaneously cleave septal PG at the periphery to drive inward constriction (Supplementary Fig. 1A ) 6 . In contrast, many Gram-positive species like Bacillus subtilis perform septation and cell separation sequentially 7 . Once septation is complete, daughter cells are compartmentalized by their membranes but remain connected by a flat septal plate (Supplementary Fig. 1B ). Septal PG hydrolysis then occurs in a distinct, regulated step to transform the flat septal plate into the hemispheric poles of the daughter cells. Notably, this process can be delayed or bypassed depending on environmental conditions 7 , with cells existing either as short motile cells or as part of nonmotile chains 8 . Cells can persist as chains for multiple generations when grown in rich medium before separation occurs 8 , reflecting the modular nature of septation and cell separation in this species. When transitioning from chains to short rods, cells must tightly regulate septal PG hydrolysis to preserve structural integrity and prevent lysis during the final stage of cell division. At the molecular level, PG is composed of glycan strands crosslinked with peptide side-chains, forming a meshwork that confers the cell its shape and mechanical strength. Crosslinking of the peptide side-chains (also called transpeptidation) is essential for cell wall integrity and is catalyzed by the activity of PG transpeptidase enzymes. Conversely, PG hydrolysis involves enzymatic breakdown of PG through cleavage of different bonds within the PG meshwork, which is important for cell elongation as well as for cell separation. The spatiotemporal patterns of PG synthesis and hydrolysis are tightly regulated during cell division to achieve septation and cell separation. In B. subtilis , the transition from chained to individual cells is regulated by alternative sigma factor SigD, which drives the expression of several PG hydrolases necessary for cell separation, including LytC, LytD, and LytF 5 . Deletion of sigD or simultaneous loss of lytC, lytD , and lytF results in cell chaining due to severely impaired separation 5 , 9 , 10 . Despite this understanding, the spatiotemporal dynamics of PG remodeling during cell separation remain poorly understood in B. subtilis and other Gram-positive bacteria. Using sequential fluorescent D-amino acid (FDAA) labeling and high-resolution three-dimensional structured illumination microscopy (3D-SIM), our previous work enabled visualization of dynamics of septum synthesis in B. subtilis 11 . FDAA labeling revealed an inward progression of transpeptidation activity during septation, demonstrating that septum synthesis proceeds from the cell periphery towards the center, coordinated by FtsZ treadmilling. In this study, we build on that approach to investigate cell separation and report a second, temporally distinct wave of transpeptidation that occurs after septation is complete, coinciding with hydrolysis-driven cell separation in this species. This wave of post-septational transpeptidation initiates at the septal periphery and proceeds inwards, and does not require new PG precursor insertion, implying that it remodels existing septal PG. However, it requires the hydrolysis of septal PG by the major cell separation-specific PG hydrolase LytF, after dispersal of FtsZ from the midcell. We identify the transpeptidase PBPH as a key enzyme for post-septational transpeptidation and show that its sustained localization at the separating septum requires PG hydrolysis by LytF. Together, these findings reveal a temporally and spatially coordinated mechanism of PG hydrolysis and post-septational transpeptidation during cell separation and pole maturation in B. subtilis , with implications for other Gram-positive bacteria that undergo two-stage cell division. Results Bacillus subtilis undergoes a two-step transpeptidation process during cell division During cell separation in B. subtilis , septal PG is cleaved and remodeled into two hemispheric poles. Since septal PG is rich in uncrosslinked pentapeptide side-chains 12 , we hypothesized that crosslinking of these peptides may contribute to pole maturation. To test this hypothesis, we used FDAAs, which enable direct spatial visualization of PG crosslinking activity through incorporation into peptide side chains during transpeptidation 13 – 16 . We first treated B. subtilis cells with HADA for 60 min to label the entire PG, including the septum, of a growing cell population. We then washed the cells to remove excess HADA, and pulsed the cells with TADA for 2 min, to label newly crosslinked PG. Sequential addition of differently colored FDAAs enables virtual time-lapse microscopy 16 (Fig. 1A ), with each FDAA pinpointing the location and intensity of transpeptidation during its labeling period. To visualize the FDAA-labeled septum, we utilized three-dimensional structured illumination microscopy (3D-SIM). This high-resolution technique allows us to distinguish between cells undergoing septal PG synthesis (with an incomplete septum) and those undergoing cell separation (with a complete septum). Analysis of cells at different division stages identified two distinct FDAA labeling patterns (Fig. 1B ). In cells undergoing septal PG synthesis, TADA appeared as a ring-shaped structure within the pre-labeled HADA ring, consistent with inward septational transpeptidation (Fig. 1B , top panel) 11 . In contrast, cells at early stages of cell separation exhibited a ring-like pattern of HADA at the periphery, and diminished HADA signal between the peripheral HADA ring and the fully formed, HADA labeled septum (Fig. 1B , middle panel). The peripheral HADA ring represents a second wave of transpeptidation originating at the periphery. The inward loss of HADA signal is likely due to PG hydrolysis, which removes the HADA labeled peptides during cell separation. Supporting this, the loss of HADA signal was not observed in a Δ sigD mutant (Fig. 1B , bottom panel), a strain deficient in a key regulator of cell separation that forms long chains due to impaired separation, as discussed later. Interestingly, the second, short-pulse of TADA showed a ring-like pattern of labeling coinciding with the region of HADA loss, indicating that the second wave of transpeptidation moves inward, accompanying PG hydrolysis during cell separation (Fig. 1B , middle panel). Treatment with the transpeptidase inhibitor ampicillin abolished TADA incorporation in both septating and separating cells (Fig. 1C, D ), confirming that FDAA labeling reflects active transpeptidation. Henceforth, we will refer to the transpeptidation during septum formation as septational transpeptidation and that following septal PG completion as post-septational transpeptidation. Post-septational transpeptidation proceeds in an inward wave following septum completion The ring-shaped TADA signal in cells with complete septa (Fig. 1B , middle panel) suggests that post-septational transpeptidation initiates at the septal periphery and proceeds inwards during cell separation. To further analyze post-septational transpeptidation, we sequentially labeled cells with three FDAAs: HADA (blue, faux colored cyan, 60 min), BADA (green, faux colored yellow, 2 min), and TADA (red, 1 min). Figure 1E (i) shows a cell in the process of septum formation, in which the first probe (HADA) formed a ring matching the septal diameter, while the second (BADA) and third (TADA) probes show ring-like patterns of decreasing diameters. This pattern reflects the previously established inward pattern of septal PG synthesis 11 . In contrast, cells with a nearly completed septum presented a different labeling pattern. In Fig. 1E (ii) the first probe (HADA) labeled most of the septal disc with a central gap. Meanwhile, the second probe BADA filled the center, reflecting inward transpeptidation and completion of septation. Finally, the third probe TADA shows faint labeling in the center, reflecting residual septational transpeptidation, but more prominently, it shows a ring of staining at the outermost edge of the septum, marking the onset of post-septational transpeptidation. Figure 1E (iii) shows a cell further along in the process of constriction. Here, HADA staining at the center of the septum reflects the last stage of septational transpeptidation, while BADA labeling at the outermost edge corresponds to initiation of post-septational transpeptidation, followed by TADA labeling in a ring of smaller diameter than the BADA ring, reflecting the inward wave of post-septational transpeptidation. Finally, Fig. 1E (iv) shows a deeply constricted cell with a faint and blurry HADA signal at the incipient polar hemispheres, while BADA is visible as a ring within the constriction site, and TADA is at the center, reflecting inward post-septational transpeptidation in a cell at the end of the separation process. Altogether, these data indicate that B. subtilis undergoes a two-step, inward-directed transpeptidation process during cell division, first during septum synthesis and secondly during cell separation into the two daughter cells. To quantify the inward dynamics of septational and post-septational transpeptidation at the population level, we plotted TADA ring diameters relative to cell length as a proxy for cell cycle progression. As shown in Fig. 1F , TADA ring diameters decreased with increasing cell length, both during septal PG formation and cell separation, confirming the inward progression of transpeptidation for both processes. However, the two distributions were clearly distinct, confirming that post-septational transpeptidation occurs at later stages of the cell cycle (i.e. in longer cells). Thus, we conclude that post-septational transpeptidation occurs predominantly in longer cells, at later stages of the cell cycle coinciding with cell separation. New PG synthesis is not required for post-septational transpeptidation Post-septational transpeptidation during cell separation might result either from crosslinking of preexisting pentapeptides within the septal PG, or from incorporation of newly synthesized PG at the separating septum. To distinguish between these possibilities, we labeled sites of new PG synthesis by tracking lipid II incorporation, as lipid II is a necessary precursor for glycosyltransferase-mediated glycan strand extension during new PG synthesis. For this, we used EDA-DA, a clickable alkyne-conjugated analog of D-alanine-D-alanine, which gets incorporated into lipid II in the cytoplasm 17 – 19 . These labeled PG precursors are then incorporated into the cell wall during new PG synthesis and can be visualized by covalent linkage to fluorophores via click-chemistry. For this experiment, we treated cells with HADA for 90 min to label the entire PG, followed by a 3 min pulse of EDA-DA. As expected, EDA-DA appeared as a smaller ring within a wider HADA ring during septal PG formation (Fig. 2A , top), consistent with new, inward PG insertion at the septum. In contrast, no EDA-DA signal was detected at complete septa undergoing separation, where only post-septational transpeptidation occurs during cell separation (Fig. 2A, bottom; 2B ). This result was confirmed by quantifying EDA-DA incorporation across cell lengths. Instead, post-septational transpeptidation during cell separation must result from crosslinking of pentapeptides already present in septal PG 12 . PG hydrolysis by the endopeptidase LytF is required for post-septational transpeptidation PG hydrolysis is a key event that drives cell separation in B. subtilis . Thus, we hypothesized that PG hydrolases involved in cell separation may regulate post-septational transpeptidation, especially given the spatiotemporal overlap between the two processes. B. subtilis encodes around forty PG hydrolases 20 – 23 , but prior studies have identified LytC (an amidase), LytD (a glucosaminidase), and LytF (a D,L-endopeptidase) as the key PG hydrolases necessary for cell separation 5 , 24 . Deletion of lytCDF or the sigma factor sigD which controls their expression, results in long chains of unseparated cells 5 . Among these, LytF has been shown to carry out the majority of cell separating activity during vegetative growth, while LytC and LytD play secondary roles 5 . Deletion of lytC or lytD causes a chaining phenotype only in a Δ lytF background, indicating hierarchical roles of PG hydrolases in septal PG separation, with LytF playing a primary role 5 . When labeled with HADA for 60 min, Δ sigD and Δ lytCDF showed fully formed septa as well as ring-like HADA signals marking ongoing septal PG synthesis, as expected for septal transpeptidation (Fig. 3A ). To test whether post-septational transpeptidation also occurs in unseparated but fully-formed septa within these chains, we treated these HADA labeled cells to a subsequent pulse of TADA for 2 min. Notably, the second TADA pulse was only observed at sites of ongoing septal PG synthesis, with no TADA signal detected at completed HADA-labeled septa (Fig. 3B ). These findings indicate that PG hydrolysis by the Lyt enzymes is required for post-septational transpeptidation during cell separation.  ≤ 0.0001. We next tested which of the three Lyt hydrolases was most important for post-septational transpeptidation. Previous studies have shown that overexpression of lytF , but not lytC or lytD , can fully rescue the chaining phenotype of Δ sigD 5 . We overexpressed lytC , lytD , or lytF in Δ sigD mutants and assessed post-septational transpeptidation using HADA labeling for 60 min followed by a 2 min TADA pulse. Ring-like TADA signals at the periphery of completed, HADA labeled septa were used as a readout for post-septational transpeptidation. Both Δ sigD and Δ lytCDF showed significantly reduced post-septational transpeptidation relative to WT (Fig. 3C , Supplementary Fig. 3 ). Although we observed low level TADA signal in these mutants (average intensity ~1) rather than values approaching zero, this likely reflects either cellular autofluorescence after background subtraction, minor HADA-bleed through or low residual post-septal transpeptidation activity. Overexpressing lytC or lytD in Δ sigD slightly increased TADA labeling, but not to WT levels. In contrast, LytF overexpression resulted in a nearly two-fold increase in post-septational transpeptidation relative to WT (Fig. 3C ). These results suggest that post-septational transpeptidation relies on PG hydrolysis, with LytF being the primary hydrolase promoting transpeptidation during cell separation. To assess whether LytF catalytic activity is required for post-septational transpeptidation, we constructed a catalytically inactive mutant of LytF (LytF C400A) which lacks the conserved cysteine essential for PG hydrolysis 25 . This mutant showed cell separation defects and chaining similar to the Δ lytF strain (Supplementary Fig. 2 ). We conducted short pulses of TADA in WT, Δ lytF, lytF C400A, and Δ sigD cells. While WT cells displayed strong TADA incorporation at separating septa, lytF C400A showed much lower transpeptidation at these sites, similar to Δ lytF and Δ sigD cells (Fig. 3D , pink arrowheads; Fig. 3E ). Together, these results reinforce the model that LytF-dependent PG hydrolysis is essential for triggering post-septal transpeptidation. LytF localizes to the septum after septation and FtsZ dispersal Given the importance of LytF in facilitating post-septational transpeptidation during cell separation, we examined its localization during septation and separation. To define the timing of divisome disassembly and the onset of cell separation, we tracked FtsZ-YFP (a divisome marker) and stained cells with FM4-64 to label the membrane and monitor its invagination during separation. Initially, FtsZ-YFP appeared at the future site of septation (Supplementary Fig. 4A , 0 min, white arrowhead). By 20 min, nascent membrane invagination was visible at the septum as two FM4-64 foci. FtsZ-YFP remained at the division site throughout septum synthesis (Supplementary Fig. 4A , 0–40 min, white arrowheads) but dispersed after septum completion (Supplementary Fig. 4A , 60 min). Subsequently, FtsZ reappeared at the next incipient division site (Supplementary Fig. 4A , open white arrowheads). Notably, FtsZ was absent from sites undergoing cell separation marked by complete FM4-64 labeling and visible constriction in phase contrast (Supplementary Fig. 4A , pink arrowheads). Demographs summarize FtsZ-YFP localization at the population level in FM4-64-labeled cells (Supplementary Fig. 4B ). In the shortest cells, FtsZ-YFP was localized to the midcell, whereas in slightly longer cells with bright FM4-64 signal in the middle, FtsZ-YFP was dispersed from the midcell but localized to the incipient sites of the next division. This pattern repeated itself again in cells that were further elongated, again showing FtsZ-YFP dispersal once septum synthesis was complete. Additionally, fluorescence intensity as a function of cell length showed a peak in FtsZ-YFP intensity during septation, with no enrichment at separating septa (Supplementary Fig. 4C ). Next, we explored LytF localization dynamics using a C-terminal msfGFP fusion (LytF-msfGFP). We verified the functionality of this fusion by expressing it in the ∆ sigD mutant, in which it partially restored cell separation (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B ). We then conducted timelapse microscopy to elucidate LytF localization at the septum. We tracked the formation of two new septa by FM4-64 labeling (Fig. 4A , white arrowheads, 0-40 min). By 60 min, faint LytF-msfGFP signals appeared at fully formed septa as indicated by FM4-64 labeling, and progressively intensified over time (Fig. 4A , white arrowheads, green channel, 60–120 min). At another septum where separation was further along, LytF remained at the nascent poles for an extended period, even after complete separation, consistent with previous studies (Fig. 4A , 0-120 min, pink arrowheads). These findings indicate that LytF-msfGFP is recruited to the septum after its completion and persists at the nascent poles throughout cell separation (Fig. 4A , pink arrowheads). This pattern was also evident at the population level as represented in demographs of LytF-msfGFP cells stained with FM4-64 (Fig. 4B ). Consistently, fluorescence intensity measurements of LytF-msfGFP revealed a peak in fluorescence at completed septa during cell separation, and enrichment at the cell poles following separation (Fig. 4C ). The distinct localization patterns of FtsZ and LytF suggest a lack of temporal overlap between the two proteins at the septum. PBPH plays a key role in post-septational transpeptidation To identify the transpeptidase(s) responsible for post-septational transpeptidation, we focused on D,D-transpeptidases that function in vegetative B. subtilis 26 . B. subtilis encodes ten PG synthases called penicillin binding proteins (PBPs), all with D,D-transpeptidase domains 27 , 28 . Among these, four Class A PBPs (PBPs 1, 4, 2 C and 2D) possess both glycosyltransferase and D,D-transpeptidase domains, while six class B PBPs (PBPs 2A, H, 2B, 3, 4B, and SpoVD) possess only D,D-transpeptidase domains. We excluded PBP2D, SpoVD, and PBP4B, which are expressed only during sporulation 29 , 30 , as well as PBP2B, which localizes with FtsZ during septal PG formation, but does not stay at the septum during cell separation 11 , 31 . L,D-transpeptidases were also excluded due to lack of L,D-transpeptidase activity in vegetative cells 26 . To determine which of the remaining PBPs are responsible for post-septational transpeptidation, we measured cell lengths in their deletion mutants. Deletion of pbpF (PBP2C), pbpD (PBP4), pbpA (PBP2A), or pbpC (PBP3) had no clear phenotype. In contrast, deletion of ponA (PBP1) and pbpH (PBPH) caused a significant increase in cell lengths (Fig. 5A , blue arrows, and 5B). These mutants exhibited heterogenous lengths with some cells or chains up to 65 µm long, while others resembled WT in length. HADA labeling revealed that longer Δ pbpH cells displayed a chaining phenotype, whereas Δ ponA cells showed much fewer septa, consistent with its known role in septum synthesis 32 (Supplementary Fig. 6A ). We next investigated post-septational transpeptidation in the D,D-transpeptidase deletion mutants using two-step FDAA labeling and 3D-SIM. Mutants were first treated with a long HADA pulse to label the entire cell wall, followed by a short TADA pulse to label new transpeptidation. Deletion of ponA or pbpH significantly reduced peripheral TADA labeling, whereas deletion of pbpA , pbpF , pbpC , or pbpD showed no significant change (Fig. 5C ). In Δ ponA cells, both HADA and TADA labeling appeared patchy, with distorted septa and uneven HADA labeling throughout the cell body compared to WT (Supplementary Fig. 6B ), consistent with the essential role of PBP1 in PG synthesis during various stages of the cell cycle, including elongation, division, and sporulation 32 , 33 . Although Δ ponA cells showed reduced post-septational transpeptidation (Supplementary Fig. 6B ), the pleiotropic defects associated with loss of PBP1 make it challenging to define its specific role in cell separation. In contrast, FDAA labeling in Δ pbpH was uniform and resembled that of WT, with clearly defined septa at different stages of cell division (Fig. 5D ). In elongated ∆ pbpH cells, TADA labeling was detectable in septa still undergoing septation, but absent from the outer periphery of completed septa, indicating the absence of post-septational transpeptidation associated with cell separation (Fig. 5D ). This observation mirrors the absence of peripheral post-septational transpeptidation in Δ sigD and Δ lytCDF mutants, which exhibit significant cell separation defects (Fig. 3A ). Together, these findings indicate that PBPH contributes to post-septational transpeptidation. Furthermore, the separation defect observed in Δ pbpH highlights its potential involvement alongside PG hydrolysis in promoting cell separation. PG hydrolysis by LytF is required for PBPH localization and post-septational transpeptidation To investigate PBPH localization during cell separation, we constructed an inducible mNeonGreen-PBPH fusion at the amyE locus. This fusion was validated for functionality by its ability to rescue viability of a double deletion mutant lacking the coessential genes pbpA and pbpH , when expressed ectopically 34 (Supplementary Fig. 7 ). We then used this construct to examine PBPH localization in mutants defective in cell separation including Δ lytF, lytF C400A, and Δ sigD . In WT cells, PBPH localized both to actively forming septa (Fig. 6A , white arrowhead) and to sites of cell separation (Fig. 6A , pink arrowhead). However, in Δ lytF, lytF C400A, and Δ sigD c ells, PBPH was mostly present at sites of septum synthesis (Fig. 6A , white arrowheads) and not at sites of cell separation (Fig. 6A , pink arrowheads, and Fig. 6B ). A msfGFP tagged version of LytF C400A localized normally to the septum, indicating that the lack of PBPH at sites of cell separation was not due to mislocalization of LytF C400A (Supplementary Fig. 5C ). These results demonstrate that LytF, and specifically its PG hydrolysis activity, is required for sustained PBPH localization at the separating septum. Finally, we tested whether PBPH itself plays a reciprocal role in LytF localization, given its early arrival at the septum during septum synthesis. However, we found that LytF localization was unchanged in the absence of PBPH (Supplementary Fig. 8 ). Similarly, since PBP1 also localizes to the septum throughout septation and cell separation 32 , and may contribute to post-septational transpeptidation, we tested its role in LytF localization. However, LytF localization remained unchanged in the absence of PBP1 ( ∆ponA ) (Supplementary Fig. 8 ). These results suggest that neither PBPH nor PBP1 is required to recruit or maintain LytF at the septum. Together, these results indicate that PBPH plays a crucial role in mediating post-septational transpeptidation during cell separation and that its continued presence at the septum depends on LytF-mediated PG hydrolysis. This suggests that septal splitting by LytF promotes PBPH activity, potentially enabling pentapeptide crosslinking of PG during nascent cell pole formation. Discussion Two-step transpeptidation in B. subtilis cell division Our study reveals a previously unknown, second wave of transpeptidation in septal PG during B. subtilis cell separation, highlighting a potential mechanism for the dynamic remodeling of the flat septal plate of the mother cell into the hemispheric poles of the nascent daughter cells. Using FDAA labeling and 3D-SIM microscopy, we show that B. subtilis cells exhibit post-septational transpeptidation alongside PG hydrolysis during cell separation (Fig. 1 ), a previously unreported finding in bacterial cell division. This activity does not require new PG insertion via transglycosylation (Fig. 2 ), indicating that post-septational transpeptidation remodels and crosslinks existing PG to reinforce the nascent daughter cell poles. These findings contrast with Gram-negative bacteria like E. coli , in which septal PG synthesis and splitting are tightly coupled 10 , 35 . In E. coli , the divisome, scaffolded by FtsZ, coordinates both PG synthesis and the recruitment of PG hydrolases, with hydrolysis being necessary for efficient septal synthesis 36 . This co-regulation ensures that PG synthesis, PG hydrolysis, outer-membrane constriction, and cell separation happen in concert, preserving the integrity of the cell-envelope during the division process. However, in B. subtilis , FtsZ disperses following septation 11 . Septal splitting is instead mediated by specific PG hydrolases, whose roles we explore in this study to understand the interplay between PG hydrolysis and post-septational transpeptidation during cell separation. The coupling of PG hydrolysis and post-septational transpeptidation The PG hydrolases LytC, LytD and LytF, and their transcriptional regulator SigD, are essential for cell separation in B. subtilis ; mutants lacking these hydrolases, or sigD , form chains of unseparated cells divided by membranes but connected by intact septa 5 . In Δ sigD and Δ lytCDF mutants, two-step FDAA labeling showed that post-septational transpeptidation was absent at fully formed septa in chains of unseparated cells, indicating that PG hydrolysis is essential for post-septational transpeptidation (Fig. 3A ). However, overexpression of LytF in the Δ sigD strain, which rescues cell separation 5 , restored post-septational transpeptidation, even increasing its levels compared to WT (Fig. 3B ). Together, these results indicate that post-septational transpeptidation depends on PG hydrolysis, particularly by LytF, with increased PG hydrolysis enhancing post-septational transpeptidation. LytF, a periplasmic D,L-endopeptidase 24 , localizes to septa after septation is complete. While its recruitment cues remain unclear, LytF does not colocalize with FtsZ, arriving at the septum after FtsZ disperses (Fig. 4A, B ). However, transient interactions between FtsZ and LytF may aid LytF recruitment prior to FtsZ dispersal. Alternatively, other components of the divisome may facilitate LytF localization, although neither PBPH nor PBP1, which are present at the separating septum, are required for LytF localization (Supplementary Fig. 8 ). Finally, distinct features of the septal PG may also contribute to LytF localization. Lipoteichoic acid and wall teichoic acids, anionic polymers that decorate the Gram-positive cell wall, appear to restrict LytF to the septum, likely due to their reduced abundance at newly formed septa 37 . Consistent with this, mutants lacking these polymers throughout the cell wall show LytF mislocalization to the lateral cell surface 38 , 39 . Another key question is how LytF activity is regulated to ensure uniform PG thickness at the pole during cell separation. Post-translational endopeptidase inhibitors like IseA may control the activity of LytF 40 . Alternatively, regulation may occur through association with other protein complexes. In E. coli , the ABC transporter-like complex FtsEX, consisting of the ATPase FtsE and the membrane protein FtsX, regulates amidase activity during cell division, via the activator EnvC 35 . FtsEX also regulates PG hydrolases in other bacteria, including B. subtilis 41 – 43 , in which it controls the activity of endopeptidase CwlO, important for lateral PG expansion 42 . Whether FtsEX also regulates LytF activity, and whether it plays a role in the coordination of post-septational transpeptidation, remains to be determined. The role of the transpeptidase PBPH in cell separation Our findings reveal that the D,D-transpeptidase PBPH plays a key role in post-septational transpeptidation during cell separation (Fig. 5C, D ). Furthermore, the ∆ pbpH mutant displayed a chaining phenotype indicative of impaired septal PG hydrolysis (Fig. 5A, B , Fig. S5 ), underscoring the interdependence of PG hydrolysis and transpeptidation during cell separation. However, LytF localization remained unaffected in ∆pbpH cells (Supplementary Fig. 8 ). Notably, the chaining phenotype of ∆ pbpH was less severe than in ∆ sigD or ∆ lytCDF (Figs. 3 , 5A, B), suggesting that additional PBPs may contribute to cell separation. Indeed, the ∆ ponA mutant (lacking PBP1) also exhibited elongated cells with reduced post-septational transpeptidation (Fig. 5A–C ). However, interpretation of these results is complicated by the overall reduction in FDAA labeling across the cell wall in the absence of PBP1, given its fundamental role in cell wall biogenesis 32 . Nonetheless, PBP1 remains localized at the septum during cell separation 44 , and its misregulation during the cell cycle has been linked to polar bulging under conditions of stress 45 . Thus, the potential role of PBP1 in pole maturation warrants further investigation. Here, we show that PBPH localizes to the septum throughout division, including during cell separation 44 . Importantly, septal PBPH localization is significantly reduced in Δ lytF cells and in cells expressing a catalytically inactive LytF (LytF C400A), demonstrating that the hydrolytic activity of LytF, and not merely its presence, is required for PBPH localization and post-septational transpeptidation (Fig. 6 ). However, other factors may also influence PBPH localization; PBPH and its homolog PBP2A are key components of the elongasome complex 34 and prior studies suggest that their localization is modulated by the availability of the PG precursor lipid II during growth 46 . While lipid II is not incorporated during cell separation (Fig. 2 ), unprocessed pentapeptides, which are transpeptidase substrates enriched at the septum 12 , may help retain PBPH at the septum and promote its activity during cell separation. Significance of post-septational transpeptidation during bacterial cell separation B. subtilis possesses dense septal PG enriched in uncrosslinked pentapeptides 12 , 47 . Hydrolysis of this relatively flexible structure may allow the septal plate to stretch and reshape into the hemispherical poles of the daughter cells. However, septal PG undergoes a significant shift in mechanical pressure during this process. Whereas the flat septal plate experiences uniform internal pressure from both sides of the dividing cell prior to cell separation, the nascent daughter cell poles experience much higher turgor pressure upon exposure to the environment 48 . Concurrent transpeptidation alongside PG hydrolysis would likely increase PG crosslinking in the nascent daughter cell poles, helping them become robust enough to withstand this increased turgor pressure. The septation first strategy of cell separation is widespread among Gram-positive bacteria 49 . Thus, it would be interesting to explore whether post-septational transpeptidation is similarly employed for pole maturation in other species and whether variations in shapes and separation mechanisms influence this process. B. subtilis and several other firmicutes, such as Listeria monocytogenes and Streptococcus mutans , undergo gradual, enzymatically controlled cell separation after the septum formation is complete 49 . Interestingly, many actinobacteria and some firmicutes like Staphylococcus aureus , undergo fast daughter cell separation driven by mechanical cracking 49 , 50 . This process is enabled by a distinct septal PG architecture, in which transmission electron microscopy reveals a central, low electron density region nestled between two high-density PG layers that develop into the daughter cells poles 51 , 52 . Consequently, the daughter cells are connected by a narrow peripheral ring, reducing the need for extensive PG hydrolysis like B. subtilis and allowing mechanical force such as turgor pressure to drive quick separation. In S. aureus , the bifunctional PG hydrolase Atl which has both amidase and glucosaminidase activities, digests peripheral PG connecting daughter cells 53 . Deletion of atl results in unseparated clump of cells impaired in host colonization 54 . Similarly, in many actinobacteria, including Corynebacteria and Mycobacteria, hydrolysis of peripheral PG connecting daughter cells is carried out by RipA family endopeptidases 55 , 56 . It remains to be determined whether these bacteria also rely on post-septal transpeptidation to fortify new poles, and how such activity might be coordinated with PG hydrolysis given the distinct mechanisms of separation within bacteria. Interestingly, in S. aureus cells, transpeptidation appears important for cell separation, as active site mutations in the transpeptidase PBP1 have been shown to impair cell separation 57 , suggesting that post-septational transpeptidation may be a widespread feature among Gram-positive bacteria. In summary, cell separation in B. subtilis involves a second, post-septational wave of transpeptidation, dependent on PG hydrolysis by LytF (Fig. 7 ). We identify PBPH as the key transpeptidase enzyme responsible, localizing to the septum during PG hydrolysis. We propose that the interplay between PG hydrolysis and PBPH-mediated transpeptidation enables proper cell separation and nascent pole maturation in B. subtilis . Methods Bacterial strains and growth conditions All experiments were performed in B. subtilis 3610 or its genetically competent derivative, 3610 comIQ12L . Cells were streaked from −80 °C freezer stocks onto LB agar plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C. A single colony was then inoculated into 5 ml liquid LB in a glass tube and grown at 37 °C with shaking (Innova 44 R shaker) until the cultures reached an OD 600 of 0.4. The culture was then diluted 20-fold with fresh LB medium (to an OD 600 of 0.02) and returned to the shaker. Unless otherwise specified, experiments were conducted when cultures again reached an OD 600 of 0.4. When required, antibiotics were used at the following concentrations: for E. coli , ampicillin (100 μg/ml); for B. subtilis , kanamycin (10 μg/ml); chloramphenicol (10 μg/ml); tetracycline (5 μg/ml); spectinomycin, (100 μg/ml), and erythromycin (1 μg/ml) with lincomycin (25 μg/ml) for erm (macrolide-lincomycin-streptogramin B (MLS) resistance). Cloning, transformation, and strain construction DNA fragments were amplified by PCR and assembled using either classic cloning or Gibson assembly according to the manufacturer’s protocol (New England Biolabs, USA). Assembled plasmids were transformed into high-efficiency E. coli NEB5α competent cells. After verification of inserts by Sanger sequencing, plasmids were transformed into the desired background of genetically competent B. subtilis 3610 comI Q12L cells. Competent B. subtilis cells were prepared by growing strains of interest in minimal competence medium supplemented with 12 mM MgSO 4 to an OD 600 of 0.7-0.8. Approximately 500 ng of plasmid DNA was added to 1 ml of culture, followed by 1 h incubation at 37 °C with shaking. Cells were pelleted, most of the supernatant was removed, and the cells were plated onto LB plates containing the appropriate antibiotic for selection. A single amino acid substitution in lytF (C400A) was introduced at the native locus using a previously described CRISPR-dCas9-based genome editing protocol 58 . Briefly, a 20-bp guide RNA fragment generated by annealing oligonucleotides 264/265 and a repair fragment carrying the desired mutation were cloned into pJOE8999 using flanking BsaI and SfiI sites, respectively. The repair fragment was generated by amplifying ~1.5 kb regions upstream and downstream of the mutation site, incorporating the desired mutation within the overlapping region, using oligonucleotide pairs 258/266 and 262/267. The resulting plasmid was transformed into high-efficiency E. coli NEB5α cells and selected on LB agar containing 30 µg/ml kanamycin. Successful cloning was confirmed by PCR, and the presence of the mutation was verified by Sanger sequencing. The confirmed plasmid was then transformed into the recipient B. subtilis strain at 30 °C. Transformants were selected on LB plates containing 15 µg/ml kanamycin and 0.2% mannose to induce dCas9 expression from the plasmid. Positive colonies were patched on LB agar and incubated at 42 °C (non-permissive temperature) for three consecutive days to cure the plasmid. Loss of the plasmid was confirmed by the inability of isolates to grow on kanamycin-containing plates, and the presence of the lytF C400A mutation was verified by PCR amplification followed by Sanger sequencing. Strains carrying lytF-msfGFP, lytF C400A-msfGFP or mNeonGreen-PBPH under IPTG-inducible control at the amyE locus were generated using the pDR111 plasmid 59 . The C-terminal msfGFP fusion to LytF and LytF C400A was connected via a TGSGGHGTGSTGSGSS linker. lytF and msfGFP were amplified using primer pairs 420/421 and 316/351, respectively, and fused by overlap PCR prior to cloning into pDR111. The lytF C400A–msfGFP construct was generated in the same manner, except that lytF C400A was amplified from a DNA template carrying the point mutation. The N-terminal mNeonGreen fusion to PBPH was linked through a LEGSGQGPGSGQGSG peptide linker. mNeonGreen and pbpH were amplified with oligonucleotide pairs 437/438 and 439/440, respectively. Gibson assembly was then used to combine pDR111 with the mNeonGreen and pbpH fragments, followed by transformation into competent E. coli cells. Synthesis of fluorescent D-amino acids (FDAA) HADA (7-hydroxycoumarin-3-carboxylic acid-D-alanine), BADA (BODIPY FL-D-alanine), and TADA (TAMRA-D-alanine) were synthesized as reported previously 13 . A simplified protocol for HADA is as follows: 7-hydroxycoumarin-3-carboxylic acid was dissolved in dimethylformamide (DMF) in a round bottom flask and activated with 1 equivalent of carbonyldiimidazole (CDI). The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature (RT) for 2 h. Then, 2 equivalents of N α -Boc-D-2,3-diaminopropionic acid were added, and the reaction was allowed to proceed overnight under an argon atmosphere. The solvent was removed under vacuum and the product was extracted with ethyl acetate (EtOAc) and 0.33 N HCl in H 2 O. The resulting material was treated with trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and dichloromethane (DCM) in a 1:1 ratio for 30 min with vigorous stirring at RT. The resulting product was purified by reverse-phase HPLC using a 10-90% methyl cyanide (MeCN)/H 2 O gradient. Synthesis of TADA and BADA followed a similar procedure, using 5/6-TAMRA-COOH and BODIPY-FL-COOH as the repective fluorophores. Coupling was carried out using N,N,N’,N’-Tetramethyl-O-(N-succinimidyl)uronium (TSTU) and N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) as the coupling reagents. FDAA labeling FDAA stock solutions were prepared in DMSO at 100 mM and stored at −20 °C. The labeling conditions, including incubation intervals and the sequence of colors used, are described in the corresponding figure panels and legends. For single color labeling (e.g. Fig. 3A , left side panel), the FDAA stock solution was directly added to exponential phase cell cultures at a final concentration of 0.5 to 1 mM. Cells were incubated at 37 °C with shaking for 60 to 90 min, depending on culture density. To fix the cells, 100% ethanol was added directly to the culture to a final concentration of 70%, followed by incubation on ice for 60 min. Fixed cells were pelleted by centrifugation (6000 g for 1 min) and resuspended in PBS. To remove residual FDAA, cells were washed twice with PBS (9000 g for 3 min) and finally resuspended in a small volume of PBS to achieve a high density for microscopy. Labeled cells were kept on ice until imaging. For sequential FDAA labeling, cells were incubated with the first FDAA to the final concentration of 1 mM, pelleted (6000 g, 1 min) and resuspended in pre-warmed fresh culture medium containing the second FDAA (1 to 2 mM). Cultures were then incubated at 37 °C with shaking for the desired duration. Fixation and washing were performed as described above. For three-color labeling, the same procedure was repeated with a third FDAA. For shorter labeling times, higher FDAA concentrations were used to ensure adequate signal intensity. Ampicillin treatment of B. subtilis Ampicillin stock solutions were prepared in double-distilled water (ddH 2 O) at a concentration of 1 mg/ml. Data analysis Classification of septation versus cell separation Cells undergoing septal PG synthesis or cell separation were classified manually based on their morphology observed in 3D-SIM, FM4-64 epifluorescence, and phase images. In 3D-SIM experiments, septating cells were defined by a ring-shaped HADA signal upon long pulse HADA labeling, whereas separating cells were defined by the presence of a fully formed HADA-labeled septal disc. Cells in the separation phase showed varying degrees of membrane constriction at the division site, along with a completed septal PG disc, while those undergoing septation lacked constriction or a fully formed septum. For FtsZ and LytF localization experiments, the cell separation stage was defined by the presence of membrane pinching, as observed in phase-contrast and epifluorescence images of FM4-64. For quantification of mNeonGreen-PBPH fluorescence, division sites showing early-stage membrane pinching, indicative of completed septation and initiation for separation, were selected based on FM4-64 and phase-contrast images. FDAA intensity quantification (3D-SIM) FDAA intensity at cell division sites (Figs. 1 – 3 , and 5 ) was quantified from 3D-SIM images. After image reconstruction, division sites were cropped, rotated at 90°, and saved without further processing. Fluorescence intensity measurements were processed using a custom MATLAB script reported previously 11 . Briefly, the script first subtracted background intensity and then identified FDAA labeled regions based on the intensity difference from the surrounding background. A user defined slope of intensity change across continuous pixels was used as the threshold for region detection. The script then calculated the mean fluorescence intensity of all pixels within the identified regions. For Fig. 2B intensity analysis was instead performed on two-dimensional epifluorescence projection images using the FIJI plugin MicrobeJ. Cell division sites were selected manually, and mean intensity values were obtained for each site. A minimum of 50 cells per condition was analyzed for all quantitative comparisons, unless otherwise noted. Fluorescence intensities in the figures are represented as arbitrary units (arb. units) and represent background subtracted values obtained from microscopic images acquired on the same instruments and under identical imaging conditions (same microscope, laser power, exposure time, and camera gain). Cell length measurements Cell lengths were measured using phase-contrast images analyzed with the FIJI plugin MicrobeJ. For automated measurements in MicrobeJ, the following thresholds were applied: length ≥2 µm; width ≥0.5 µm. For filamentous strains such as Δ sigD and Δ lytCDF , which often formed chains too long to be fully captured in a single image, cell lengths were measured manually using FIJI. This variability in chain lengths contributed to the higher standard deviation observed in these measurements. Curve fitting for ring constriction speed Transpeptidation ring diameters and cell lengths (Fig. 1F and 2C ) were measured manually from 3D-SIM images using FIJI. XY plots were generated in GraphPad Prism 8, and linear regression was applied. The absolute value of the regression slope was reported as the ring constriction speed. FtsZ and LytF localization dynamics in FM4-64 labeled cells To analyze protein localization dynamics at the population level, demographs were generated using the MicrobeJ plugin in FIJI. The following cell selection thresholds were applied: length ≥ 1.3 µm; width = 0.3–1.1 µm. Cells were sorted by length. Fluorescence intensity values for FM4-64, YFP-FtsZ, LytF-msfGFP, and HaloTag-FtsZ (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 4 ) were measured using MicrobeJ, and the corresponding intensity profiles were plotted in GraphPad Prism 10. mNeonGreen-PBPH intensity measurements The fluorescence intensity of mNeonGreen-PBPH at sites of cell separation was measured in WT, ∆ lytF , lytF C400A, and ∆ sigD using FIJI. Stages of septation and cell separation were tracked using FM4-64 labeling and phase-contrast imaging. Division sites showing completed septa and/or initiation of cell separation were selected for quantification. Areas of measurement were selected manually using the freehand selection tool in FIJI. Intensity measurements generated in FIJI were exported to GraphPad Prism 10 to plot the fluorescence intensities in the different strains. TADA intensity measurements in WT, ∆ lytF , lytF C400A and ∆ sigD The intensity of TADA labeling at division sites in WT, Δ lytF , lytF C400A, and Δ sigD was quantified in FIJI. Regions corresponding to separation sites (as determined by membrane constriction in phase-contrast images) were manually selected. Measured fluorescence intensity values were imported into GraphPad Prism 10 for graphical representation.
