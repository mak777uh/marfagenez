## Experimental Setup 
 2 at a flow rate of 500 mL/min was used as an inert purge
gas to prevent oxidation by ambient air, in accordance with the operating
requirements of the Rigaku thermogravimetric instrument. The purge
gas was introduced from the rear of the furnace system and was not
directed as a high-velocity jet onto the sample surface. The same
gas-flow condition was consistently applied to all experiments. Biomass
and OC were weighed separately and manually mixed using a mortar and
pestle prior to each run to promote a uniform distribution of the
two solid components. Subsequently, 5 mg of biomass was tested either
alone as the biomass-only control or physically mixed with 2.5, 5,
or 10 mg of Badar stone, corresponding to OC-to-biomass mass ratios
of 0.5, 1, and 2, respectively. Comparative experiments using synthetic
Fe 2 O 3 were conducted at an OC-to-biomass mass
ratio of 1. The biomass loading of 5 mg was selected to maintain a
shallow, reproducible sample bed and to ensure that all biomass and
OC mixtures remained within the crucible’s practical volumetric
capacity, particularly for low-bulk-density feedstocks such as sawdust.
Prior to each TGA experiment, the separately weighed biomass and OC
were manually mixed using a mortar and pestle to promote a uniform
distribution of the two solid components. The mixture was then transferred
directly into the TGA sample holder. The same mixing procedure was
consistently applied to all experimental conditions. The sample was
first heated from room temperature to 383 at 20 K/min and held for
10 min to remove moisture, followed by heating to 1300 K at the same
rate. Because no external reducing gas was supplied during these runs,
the measured mass loss reflects biomass pyrolysis and the subsequent
interaction of pyrolysis-derived gases and char with the OC under
TGA conditions. Therefore, the present nonisothermal TGA experiments
are interpreted as a comparative kinetic screening tool rather than
as a direct representation of reactor-scale chemical looping behavior.
In addition, selected experimental conditions were independently repeated
to evaluate the run-to-run repeatability of the TG and DTG profiles.
The repeated experiments were selected to cover lignocellulosic and
microalgal biomass samples, biomass-only controls, different Badar
stone loadings, and synthetic Fe2O3 comparisons. For each repeated
experiment, the biomass and OC were separately weighed, manually mixed,
and transferred to the TGA sample holder following the same preparation
and operating procedures. Repeatability was quantitatively assessed
using pairwise RMSE values calculated from the normalized TG profiles.
Representative TG and DTG overlays and the corresponding repeatability
metrics are provided in Table S.1 of the Supporting Information . The replicate set was
designed to evaluate the technical repeatability of representative
experimental conditions rather than to provide statistical replication
for every biomass and OC combination. Because parameter-specific fitting
uncertainty was not independently determined, small differences of
only a few kJ/mol among the fitted apparent activation energies are
considered comparable and are not interpreted as evidence of distinctly
different reaction mechanisms. 2.3.

are approximately −90, −30, and −10 mg/K, respectively;
however, in RH, the very high ash content enhances the distinction
of the two hemicellulose-related peaks, making them more visible in
the DTG profile. Lignocellulosic-based biomass exhibited overlapping
peaks due to the combined degradation of all three components, while
lignin contributed to a wider thermal span up to 850 K, characterized
by β-O-4 bond cleavage near 630 K. At elevated temperatures (990–1100 K), additional mass loss
was observed in the lignocellulosic-based biomass–OC mixtures.
Under the present TGA conditions, this behavior is more cautiously
interpreted as being associated with interactions between biomass-derived
char and pyrolysis-derived gases with the OC, rather than being assigned
exclusively to a specific iron oxide reduction pathway. This interpretation
is consistent with previous observations for lignin–Fe 2 O 3 blends.
,
 These results indicate that microalgal-based
biomass exhibits more complex devolatilization behavior than lignocellulosic
biomass due to the coexistence of nitrogenous and lipid-rich compounds.
Unlike other microalgae, ME has a deep mass reduction and similar
to protein- and carbohydrate-rich biomasses such as defatted Chlorella , because its thermolytic degradation occurs predominantly
between 500–600 K, where intense volatilization of intracellular
organics (proteins, polysaccharides, and residual lipids) takes place,
leading to rapid devolatilization and release of gases such as CO 2 , CO, CH 4 , and light hydrocarbons. A 1:1 Fe 2 O 3 ratio showed higher reduction
performance than an equivalent Badar stone ratio and exhibited behavior
comparable to the 2:1 Badar mixture, highlighting both the catalytic
advantage and the inherent limitations of pure Fe 2 O 3 .

Similarly, Robles et al. found that for
forest residue biochar, reduction proceeds from Fe 2 O 3 →Fe 3 O 4 at 837–923 K, Fe 3 O 4 →FeO at 923–1004 K, and FeO→Fe
above 1004 K. Wang et al. conducted sawdust–Fe 2 O 3 reduction experiments, showing that the stepwise

FeO→Fe at 1123–1173 K. However, in the present study,
the LTZ, MTZ, and HTZ are used as empirical fitting windows to separate
distinct regions of the measured TG and DTG profiles and should not
be interpreted as mechanistically exclusive reaction regimes or as
one-to-one assignments of individual iron oxide reduction steps. In
the LTZ and much of the MTZ, biomass devolatilization provides the
dominant contribution to the observed net mass loss, as indicated
by the major DTG peaks and the relatively limited separation among
different OC loadings. Nevertheless, simultaneous oxygen carrier reduction,
carbon deposition, secondary reactions involving pyrolysis-derived
species, char formation, and other overlapping processes cannot be
excluded. Therefore, the fitted parameters represent apparent descriptors
of the net thermogravimetric response within each temperature window
rather than intrinsic kinetic parameters of a single rate-controlling
mechanism.

quantified, its contribution cannot be separated from the other processes
based on TG data alone. Therefore, the fitted kinetic parameters should

rather than as isolated kinetic constants of individual reactions.
