 Radio Frequency (RF) heating is a promising alternative for conventional heating to inactivate pathogens during food processing, but a knowledge gap exists concerning RF effects on bacteria in relation to different food microstructural aspects. This study focusses on RF inactivation of Listeria monocytogenes and Salmonella Typhimurium in differently-structured food matrices, tackling (i) indirect effects of RF compared to conventional heating in different matrices, and (ii) food matrix rheology influence on inactivation. RF and conventional thermal inactivation experiments were conducted in a low-viscosity and high-viscosity liquid, and an aqueous gel. Indirect RF effects (inactivation rate k higher for RF than for conventional heating) at higher temperatures occurred in the aqueous gel for L. monocytogenes , and in the liquids for S . Typhimurium. The effect of increasing liquid matrix viscosity on RF inactivation was similar to conventional heating for both microorganisms; increasing viscosity resulting in smaller k . The effect of the presence of a gelled matrix on RF inactivation was similar to conventional heating for S . Typhimurium, i.e., k higher for high-viscosity liquid than for aqueous gel. For L.monocytogenes , the decrease in k in the aqueous gel was lower than for conventional heating, indicating possible RF-specific inactivation in gelled systems.

## Introduction 
 Recent studies have identified Radio Frequency (RF) heating of foods as an alternative to conventional heating processes for inactivating pathogenic microorganisms, mainly due to the fast and volumetric heating mechanism 1 , 2 . RF heating is a dielectric heating method in which foods are exposed to electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths between 1 and 300 MHz, although only 13.56, 27.12, and 40.68 MHz are permitted for industrial, scientific and medical applications 3 . RF heating involves lower frequencies compared to the most well-known dielectric heating method, i.e., microwave heating, with permitted wavelengths of 915 and 2450 MHz 4 . When exposing foods to RF energy, an alternating electrical field is applied, resulting in heating through molecular friction, governed by a combination of two mechanisms, i.e., ionic depolarisation (dominating mechanism) and bipolar rotation 3 , 5 . The low frequencies related to RF heating entail a rather large penetration depth, causing RF heating treatments to be generally identified as a volumetric heating method that can be applied to bulk products and can provide a faster heat treatment in foods compared to conventional thermal processes 2 , 6 . 
 Most of the current literature on RF heating inactivation focusses on acquiring uniform heating profiles in food products and the effect on food quality 7 , 8 . There is, however, still a major knowledge gap concerning the inactivation mechanisms of RF heating, mainly pertaining to (i) the (indirect) effects of RF energy on the bacterial cells and (ii) the (protective) effects of different food microstructural aspects on RF heating inactivation. On the one hand, the (indirect) effects of RF heating at 27.12 MHz on microorganisms have been investigated to some extent, with no clear consensus reached thus far. For example, Kou et al. 9 reported the absence of non-thermal effects of RF heating on Escherichia coli in apple juice and mashed potatoes, similar to Cui et al. 10 for Staphylococcus aureus , E. coli and Saccharomyces cerevisiae in saline solution, while Jeong et al. 11 reported that RF heating treatments produced a significantly greater reduction of three Salmonella enterica serovars in raw shelled almonds. On the other hand, no research has been conducted on the effect of food microstructural factors on RF heating inactivation. Food microstructural factors (e.g., the presence of a gelled food matrix) have been reported to cause protective effects for bacterial cells against conventional thermal inactivation 12 , 13 . The research gap on this topic is caused in part by the almost exclusive conduction of RF heating inactivation experiments in real food products. While this approach is useful to assess whether RF treatments are suitable to assure the microbiological safety of certain products, the presence of many different food components and background microflora in combination with variability between food products does not allow the isolation the effect of RF heating on the bacterial cells, making it difficult to study inactivation mechanisms. Moreover, the effect of food microstructure on RF heating inactivation cannot be investigated by comparing different real foods, since compositional and physicochemical differences among the differently-structured products inevitably affect microbial kinetics 14 . 
 In the current study, the influence of food microstructure on RF heating inactivation of Listeria monocytogenes (Gram-positive) and Salmonella Typhimurium (Gram-negative) was investigated by (i) assessing possible indirect effects of RF heating compared to conventional heating, and (ii) identifying the influence of food matrix rheology (i.e., viscosity and presence of a gelled matrix) on RF heating inactivation kinetics. RF heating and conventional thermal (i.e., water bath) inactivation experiments were conducted for fish-based food model systems varying in microstructural complexity, i.e., (i) a low-viscosity liquid “liquid”, (ii) a high-viscosity liquid “xanthan”, and (iii) an “aqueous gel”. A specific mathematical modelling approach was used to effectively compare inactivation kinetics and sublethal injury between RF and conventional heat treatments. 
## Methods 
## Microorganisms and preculture conditions 
 Bacteria were cultured by the methods as described in Espitia et al. 15 and Verheyen et al. 13 . Listeria monocytogenes LMG 23,774 and Salmonella Typhimurium LMG 14,933 strains were acquired from the BCCM/LMG bacteria collection of Ghent University (Belgium). The culture method was the same for both microorganisms, with their respective optimal nutrient media and temperature. Brain Heart Infusion Broth (BHI, VWR International, Leuven, Belgium) and Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB, VWR, International, Leuven, Belgium) were used as liquid culture media for L. monocytogenes and S. Typhimurium, respectively. Stock cultures were stored at -80 °C in 80% (v/v) of the respective culture medium and 20% (v/v) glycerol (Acros Organics, Geel, Belgium). Before experiments, a purity plate was prepared for each microorganism by spreading a loopful of stock culture onto agar plates, prepared by addition of 1.4% (w/v) bacteriological agar (VWR International, Leuven, Belgium) to the respective liquid medium. Purity plates were stored for 24 h at 30 and 37 °C for L. monocytogenes and S. Typhimurium, respectively. For each microorganism, one colony from the purity plate was transferred into an Erlenmeyer flask containing 20 mL of the respective liquid culture medium. The Erlenmeyer flasks were incubated for 24 h at 30 and 37 °C for L. monocytogenes and S. Typhimurium, respectively. Afterwards, 20 µL was transferred to an Erlenmeyer flask with fresh liquid medium and the same incubation conditions were applied. This resulted in stationary phase cultures with inoculum levels of approximately 10 9 CFU/mL. 
## Model system preparation and inoculation 
 Model systems representing the microstructural range of common processed fish-based food products were prepared based on the protocol of Verheyen et al. 14 . Physicochemical, rheological, and compositional properties of the model systems are described in Table  1 . The model systems were designed to effectively isolate microstructural aspects, i.e., differences in composition, pH, and a w were minimised among the different systems. The liquid and xanthan systems were non-Newtonian liquid systems, while the aqueous gel was a strong gel. The rheology of the liquid and xanthan systems was characterised by their power law model viscosity parameters (i.e., the consistency index K and the flow behaviour index n ), with xanthan being the most viscous system. The rheology of the aqueous gel was characterised by the storage modulus ( G ’, a measure of elastic properties) and the loss modulus ( G ”, a measure of viscous properties) 14 .

 Model systems were inoculated as described in Verheyen et al. 13 . In brief, model systems in their liquid state (i.e., before the chemical gelation step for the aqueous gel), 30 g per batch, were homogeneously inoculated with stationary phase L. monocytogenes or S. Typhimurium cultures to an approximate cell density of 10 8 -10 9 CFU/mL. At the end of the model system preparation procedure, the model systems were divided over small glass vials (4 mL, 45 × 14.7 mm, BGB Analytic Benelux B.V., Harderwijk, the Netherlands). Model system volumes of 3.00 mL (liquid, xanthan) and 2.25 mL (aqueous gel) were used, selected to acquire stable electromagnetic oscillations for all model systems. Vials containing inoculated model systems were stored at 10 °C for approximately 16 h prior to the RF heating inactivation experiments to allow the cells to adapt to their environment. 
## Radio frequency heating inactivation experiments 
 RF heating experiments were conducted in a free running oscillator RF set-up, built according to the flyback topology design using an air-core transformer, as described in detail by Kozak et al. 16 . In brief, The RF set-up was of the free-running oscillator type, and operated at a frequency of 27.0 ± 0.5 MHz with an input power of approximately 12–13 W (3–4 W without sample load). The setup consisted of a single copper disk electrode (diameter of 16.25 mm), with the samples in glass vials placed in a polystyrene holder on top of this electrode. A step-up transformer (4 turns for the primary coil, 675 turns for the secondary coil) was wound on a glass tube (15 mm diameter). A variable capacitor (25–150 pF) was connected in parallel to the primary coil and the emitter of the NPN transistor. For more details on other parts of the RF setup (e.g., power supply, cooling fan), Kozak et al. 16 can be consulted. 
 RF heating experiments and the associated temperature measurements were conducted as described in Espitia et al. 15 . In brief, glass vials containing liquid or gelled samples were closed by a polypropylene screw cap lid with a glass capillary inserted through the middle, the latter being used to insert an optical fibre temperature sensor in the sample without contaminating the sample. When the vials were closed, the glass capillary touched the bottom of the glass vial. In order to stop the inactivation after the RF heating treatment, the samples were stored in ice-cooled water for 10 min and afterwards stored at 4 °C for up to 3 h prior to microbiological analysis. For each model system-microorganism combination, 31–40 sample points were taken from 6 to 12 independent biological replicates, and 10–15 unique time points were conducted (at least) in duplicate, i.e., coming from two independent biological replicates. 
## Conventional thermal inactivation experiments 
 Conventional thermal inactivation experiments were conducted in water baths. In order to ensure data comparability, three different target water bath time-temperature profiles for the internal temperature of the model systems were constructed based on the conducted RF heating treatments: minimum, average, and maximum. There were always two biological replicates per microorganism-model system combination, divided over the temperature profiles, providing an accurate representation of the time-temperature profiles encountered during the RF heating treatments. 
## Microbiological analysis 
 Sample processing was conducted based on Verheyen et al. 13 . Due to the complexity of the microstructures in the model systems, samples were processed differently before analysis. For the liquid and xanthan systems, serial dilutions were directly prepared from the samples using an aqueous solution containing 0.90% (w/v) NaCl (Sigma Aldrich, Diegem, Belgium). For the aqueous gel, the model systems were removed from the glass vials and transferred into sterile stomacher bags. Vials were rinsed with 3.38 mL of Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS, 0.20 g/L KCl, 0.20 g/L KH 2 PO 4 , 8.00 g/L NaCl, 1.15 g/L Na 2 HPO 4 , Sigma Aldrich, Diegem, Belgium) and drained into a stomacher bag. In order to initiate gel breakdown, 3.38 mL of McIlvaine buffer (28.39 g/L Na 2 HPO 4 , Sigma Aldrich, Diegem, Belgium, 19.12 g/L citric acid, Acros Organics, Geel, Belgium) was added to the stomacher bag. The resulting mixture was homogenised for 10 min (basic masticator, Led techno, Heusden-Zolder, Belgium), after which serial decimal solutions were prepared. Serial dilutions were plated on nonselective and selective agar plates. Brain Heart Infusion agar (BHIA) and Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) were used as nonselective media, while Palcam agar (VWR, Leuven, Belgium) and Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate agar (XLD, VWR, Leuven, Belgium) agar were used as selective media, for L. monocytogenes and S. Typhimurium, respectively. Drops of 20 µL were plated for dilutions − 1 to -6, while drops of 100 µL were plated for the undiluted sample, and spread out using an L-shaped spreader. Prior to enumeration, agar plates were incubated for 30–48 h at 30–37 °C, for L. monocytogenes and S. Typhimurium, respectively. Obtained cell counts were considered significant when at least 10 colonies per drop of 100 µL were detected for the undiluted sample, resulting in a detection limit (DL) of 2.0 and 2.6 log (CFU/mL) for liquid/xanthan and aqueous gel, respectively. The higher DL for gelled systems was due to the extra dilution step caused by the addition of PBS and McIlvaine buffer to the samples. Undiluted samples with colony counts below the DL were taken into account for the model fit as such. Undiluted samples for which no colonies were detected were assumed to be equal to the average of the DL and zero, i.e., 0.85 log (CFU/mL) for liquid and xanthan, and 1.44 log (CFU/mL) for the aqueous gel. In both cases, results were critically evaluated. 
## Temperature measurements 
 Temperature measurements were conducted as described in Espitia et al. 15 and Tonti et al. 17 . During RF and water bath experiments, the internal temperature (at the bottom of the glass vial) of the model systems was recorded using a calibrated TS3 GaAs-based optic fibre temperature sensor (inserted in the glass capillary) connected to a Bench Top FOTEMP-PLUS signal conditioner (Weidmann Technologies Deutschland GmbH, Dresden, Germany). Data were processed using the FOTEMP-Assistant software (Optocon, AG, Dresden, Germany). 
 The temperature distribution in the samples during RF and water bath experiments was assessed in separate experiments. These experiments were conducted according to the average temperature profile for RF and water bath experiments. As illustrated in Fig.  1 , three glass capillaries were inserted through the sample caps to measure the temperature evolution at three positions, i.e., bottom, middle and top. Since the insertion of three capillaries in the aqueous gel model system negatively affected the structural integrity of the aqueous gel model system during extended RF heating, results were only reported up until a maximum of 20 min for all model systems.

## Mathematical modelling of inactivation kinetics 
 The variation in temperature profiles is a problem inherent to RF heating, influenced by several factors. These include poor heat uniformity at high heating rates 18 , heat distribution affected by the electrode gap 19 , sample orientation and geometry 20 , as well as faster heating at the edges compared to the interior of the sample 21 . Therefore, a specifically designed mathematical modelling approach was used to take each individual RF heating or water bath treatment (i.e., initial cell density, final cell density, temperature profile) into account separately to estimate a general set of inactivation kinetics parameters in function of temperature for RF and conventional heating, respectively. This methodology is described in detail in Tonti et al. 17 . The comparison of these two sets of inactivation kinetics parameters among the different model systems formed the basis for the investigation of possible indirect effects of RF heating on microorganisms. Since the aim of the current study was not to design RF heating processes for specific applications, but rather to gain insights of general effects of RF heating on microbial inactivation, the selected approach was deemed suitable.

## Statistical analysis 
 For the model parameters, significant differences were determined as described in Verheyen et al. 13 using an analysis of variance (ANOVA, single variance) test at a 95.0% confidence level ( α  = 0.05). Fisher’s Least Significant Difference test was used to distinguish which means were significantly different from others. Standardised skewness and standardised kurtosis were used to assess if datasets came from normal distributions. For TICC values, two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests ( α  = 0.05) were conducted to assess whether the different TICC values obtained by the Monte Carlo simulations for two different conditions were from the same continuous distribution. Test statistics were regarded as significant when P  ≤ 0.05. The analyses were performed using MatLab version R2020b (TheMathworks Inc., Natick, USA). 
## Results 
## Temperature distribution 
 Figure  2 illustrates the temperature distribution inside the model systems during RF and conventional heating, expressed in terms of temperature difference of the top and middle measurement point as compared to the bottom measurement point reference. The variability in temperature profiles was significantly larger for the RF treatments than for the conventional treatments, as indicated by the larger standard deviations for the former. Moreover, the temperature distribution was significantly more homogeneous for the conventional heating than for the RF heating, probably due to the small experimental scale in this study, imposing limited heat transfer limitations for conventional heating. 
 In the following sections, the differences in temperature distributions among the different model systems and the two heating methods will be taken into account for each specific condition in order to accurately interpret the results.

 Figures  3 , 4 and 5 illustrate the temperature profiles and remaining cell densities following the RF heating and conventional thermal treatments of L. monocytogenes and S. Figure  6 provides the inactivation rate k in function of treatment temperature for all conditions, with a statistical analysis among different conditions having been conducted at different temperature values (Supplementary Material S1) to assess the significance of the observations. The estimated values for z’ are 29.9 ± 6.7, 17.1 ± 12.7, and 18.9 ± 5.6 °C for RF heating of L. monocytogenes in the liquid, xanthan and aqueous gel, respectively; similarly 30.6 ± 7.6, 32.5 ± 5.5, and 54.5 ± 19.0 °C, for conventional heating of L. monocytogenes ; 21.9 ± 4.4, 11.7 ± 3.4, and 24.2 ± 5.67 °C for RF heating of S . Typhimurium; and 23.1 ± 3.5, 46.1 ± 16.0, and 31.5 ± 6.0 °C for conventional heating of S . Typhimurium. The Mean Squared Errors (MSE) of the model fits were 0.81, 4.28, and 1.60 for RF heating of L. monocytogenes in the liquid, xanthan and aqueous gel, respectively; similarly 1.04, 0.31, and 1.29, for conventional heating of L. monocytogenes ; 1.77, 2.90, and 0.66 for RF heating of S . Typhimurium; and 0.68, 0.94, and 0.89 for conventional heating of S . Typhimurium. The values for z’ and MSE are also summarised in Supplementary Material S2.

## Comparison between RF and conventional heating 
 In order to identify indirect effects of RF heating, inactivation kinetics and sublethal injury were compared between RF and conventional heating, separately in each model system. 
 Liquid . For L. monocytogenes , no significant differences in k were observed between the RF and conventional treatments (starting above approximately 59 °C), indicating the absence of indirect RF effects on the cells. Since TICC was higher for RF than for conventional treatments, with the difference being limited, additional RF-specific injury was inflicted on the cells, although not high enough to cause more inactivation. For S . Typhimurium, k was significantly higher for RF heating than for conventional treatments above approximately 54 °C, while no significant differences were observed at lower temperatures. There was hence a critical temperature above which inactivation occurred faster for RF than for conventional heating. Additionally, TICC was higher for RF heating than for conventional heating, indicating that an amount of RF-specific injury was also inflicted on S . Typhimurium, in this case also resulting in lethal damage. Since the temperature distributions in the liquid system were rather similar between the RF and conventional treatments (Fig.  2 a, b), these observations were representative. In the liquid, indirect effects of RF at higher temperatures were hence only present for S . Typhimurium. 
 Xanthan . For L. monocytogenes , k was the highest for conventional treatments at temperatures below 73 °C, while no significant differences were observed at higher temperatures. TICC was higher for RF heating than for conventional heating, indicating RF-specific sublethal injury. The differences at lower temperatures were possibly caused by the differences in temperature distribution between RF and conventional heating of the xanthan system (Fig.  2 c, d). Since the temperature for RF heating was overall lower than for conventional heating at the middle and top position, observed differences may have been artefacts of the experimental method. For S . Typhimurium, k was higher for the conventional treatments than for the RF treatments below 67 °C (probably due to the temperature distribution differences as discussed above), while the opposite relation was present at higher temperatures. TICC for RF heating was higher than for conventional heating. For S . Typhimurium, similar conclusions to the liquid system were hence drawn for the xanthan system. In xanthan, indirect effects of RF at higher temperatures were hence, similar to the liquid, only present for S . Typhimurium. 
 Aqueous gel . For L. monocytogenes , k was significantly higher for conventional treatments than for RF treatments below 63 °C, while the opposite relation was observed at higher temperatures. TICC was higher for conventional heating than for RF heating, meaning that most of this injury was probably inflicted at the lower temperature part of the treatments. The temperature at the middle and top positions was higher for RF than for conventional heating at lower temperatures, and similar at higher temperatures (Fig.  2 e, f). Therefore, the observed trends are strengthened for all temperature ranges. For S . Typhimurium, k was higher for the conventional treatments than for RF heating at all temperatures. TICC was higher for RF heating than for conventional heating, possibly due to the previous relation being independent of temperature and a significant amount of cells hence rather being sublethally injured than completely inactivated. For the lower temperatures, this trend is strengthened by the temperature distribution measurements, with temperatures at the middle and top position even being higher for RF than for conventional heating. In the aqueous gel, indirect RF effects were hence only present for L. monocytogenes . 
## Comparison between different model systems 
 Due to the minimal compositional and physicochemical differences among the different systems 14 . More specifically, the model systems were standardised regarding pH, water activity, and protein, gelling agent (i.e., sodium alginate) and NaCl concentration. Concerning pH and water activity, model system composition and preparation protocols were varied to obtain the absence of significant differences among the different model systems. Concurrently, compositional differences among model systems were maintained below 1% w/w of the total model system mass. Therefore, the used model systems allowed, for the effect on the RF heating inactivation of L. monocytogenes and S . Typhimurium, the isolation of two microstructural aspects related to the rheology of the food matrix, i.e., (i) liquid food matrix viscosity (liquid vs. xanthan) and (ii) the presence/absence of a gelled food matrix (xanthan vs. aqueous gel). Microstructural effects occurring for RF heating were also compared to those occurring for conventional heating. 
 Liquid vs. xanthan . For RF heating, k was significantly lower in the xanthan system than in the liquid system at all temperatures for both microorganisms. Furthermore, TICC was higher in xanthan than in the liquid, indicating that cells in xanthan were rather being sublethally injured than completely inactivated. While Fig.  2 (a, c) illustrates that the middle and top temperatures in the xanthan system were approximately 2–3 °C lower than in the liquid, the difference in k in Fig.  6 was sufficiently high to confirm the observed trend. For the conventional treatments, k was significantly lower in the xanthan than in the liquid system for both microorganisms. TICC was higher in the liquid, indicating that the inactivation effect on the cells was, at least in part, different than for RF heating. Since the middle and top temperatures were similar for the liquid and xanthan systems for conventional heating (Fig.  2 b, d) with the differences in k still being significant, the trends discussed above are representative. 
 Xanthan vs. aqueous gel . For RF heating, there were no significant differences in k between the xanthan and aqueous gel system for L. monocytogenes at any temperature. However, this finding should be interpreted critically, since Fig.  2 (c, e) illustrates that the top and middle temperatures in the aqueous gel were approximately 3–4 °C lower than in the xanthan system, meaning that k could in reality have been higher in the aqueous gel system. TICC was lower in the aqueous gel than in the xanthan system, together with the often limited inactivation observed in Fig.  4 (a), confirming this hypothesis. For S . Typhimurium, no significant differences were observed between the xanthan system and the aqueous gel below approximately 62 °C (except below 55 °C, where the value in the aqueous gel was higher due to the low value in the xanthan system), while k was higher in the xanthan system at higher temperatures. TICC was again lower in the aqueous gel than in the xanthan system. S . Typhimurium was hence more resistant to RF heating in a gelled matrix. The observation of this phenomenon was strengthened by the aforementioned temperature distribution differences between the xanthan and aqueous gel systems. For conventional heating, k was significantly higher in the xanthan system than in the aqueous gel at all temperatures for L. monocytogenes , with temperature distributions being similar in both systems (Fig.  2 d, f). TICC was higher in the aqueous gel than in the xanthan system. While a behaviour similar to the RF heating treatments was hence observed for L. monocytogenes , there were possible effects related to RF at higher temperatures. The decreasing top and bottom temperatures in the aqueous gel at longer treatment times (and related higher treatment temperatures) indicate the possibility of an only limited protective effect of a gelled matrix on L. monocytogenes RF heating inactivation compared to conventional heating. This assumption is strengthened by the higher TICC in the xanthan matrix than in the aqueous gel. For S . Typhimurium during conventional heating, there were no significant differences in k between the xanthan and aqueous gel system at temperatures above approximately 68 °C, while k was higher in the xanthan systems at lower temperatures. TICC was higher in the aqueous gel than in the xanthan system, although differences were rather limited and TICC was close to zero. 
## Discussion 
 RF processing has several applications in the food industry, such as control of insect pests 7 , thawing 19 , baking 21 , and pasteurization 1 , 6 . However, RF pasteurization is particularly challenging, as RF heating can be easily influenced not only by sample composition but also by the target microorganism 15 , 17 . Therefore, the current study aimed to assess, for L. monocytogenes and S . Typhimurium, (i) possible indirect effects of RF heating on microorganisms and (ii) the influence of food matrix rheology on RF inactivation. For this purpose, food model systems with minimal complexity were used in order to more effectively study fundamental effects. For the same reason, a lab-scale RF systems was used for the RF treatments, in order to more easily control treatment parameters. In future studies, the general findings of the current study can be validated at a more applied scale, using specific food products and larger-scale RF setups. 
## Indirect RF heating effects on microorganisms 
 A microorganism and structure-dependent indirect effect of RF heating was observed, since inactivation occurred faster for RF heating than for conventional treatments at high temperatures under some conditions. For L. monocytogenes , possible indirect effects were only observed in the more structurally complex aqueous gel. For S . Typhimurium, these indirect were only observed in less complex structured systems (i.e., low-viscosity liquid system and high-viscosity xanthan system). A microorganism and/or structure-dependency is also a possible explanation for the contradicting findings of concerning indirect RF effects of Kou et al. 9 (no indirect RF effects on E. coli in apple juice and mashed potatoes), Cui et al. 10 (no indirect effects on S. aureus , E. coli and S. cerevisiae in saline solution), and Jeon et al. 11 (indirect effects on Salmonella serovars in raw shelled almonds). Since food matrices and target microorganisms were not constant over the three aforementioned studies and the current study, accurate comparisons could, however, not be made. 
 The authors propose two possible hypotheses for the indirect effects of RF observed in the current study, i.e., (i) heat developing at a faster rate within microorganisms than in the surrounding food matrix, causing cells to be thermally inactivated at lower measured (macroscopic) temperatures 28 , and (ii) electromagnetic waves causing a synergistic additional effect (e.g., DNA or membrane damage) on the bacterial cells at high temperatures. 
 For the first hypothesis (heat developing at a faster rate within microorganisms than in the surrounding food matrix), the absorption of electromagnetic energy by microorganisms exposed to RF is dependent on their dielectric properties (i.e., dielectric constant, representing the ability to store electromagnetic energy; and dielectric loss factor, representing the ability to dissipate electromagnetic energy and convert it to heat), which are strongly dependent on their Gram-type and related membrane properties 15 , 29 . Both the dielectric constant and loss factor are higher for Gram-positive than for Gram-negative bacteria, due to the large amount of protein in the peptidoglycan layer 15 , 30 . When exposed to RF energy in the food matrix, Gram-positive bacteria will therefore both store more electromagnetic energy and dissipate more heat than Gram-negative bacteria. In the aqueous gel, the indirect effects of RF observed for L. monocytogenes were hence possibly caused by this additional generated heat not easily being dissipated over the sample bulk due to the absence of convectional heat transfer, causing the cells to experience higher temperatures than macroscopically measured. While this hypothesis also justifies the absence of indirect RF effects on the Gram-negative S . Typhimurium in the aqueous gel, it does not explain the observed indirect effects in liquid matrices for this microorganism. Possible explanations are related to the variation of cell dielectric properties over the different cell components (i.e., membrane, cytoplasm, nucleic acids), and to the influence of the surrounding medium on the cell state, in turn influencing the cell response to electromagnetic fields 31 . 
 For the second hypothesis (electromagnetic waves causing a synergistic additional effect), research is limited since the majority of previous studies on RF heating inactivation solely focussed on differences in surviving cell populations on a macroscopic level, meaning that possible additional effects were not taken into account. Cui et al. 10 compared RF (27.12 MHz) and conventional thermal heating effects on S. aureus in liquid broth. They found that cell membranes, morphological structures (e.g., shrunk and wrinkled cell membranes), intracellular structures, intracellular proteins (i.e., α-helix), and gene expression were damaged/altered by RF heating. However, the effect was only significantly different compared to conventional heating for the gene expression, with a lower number of differentially and specifically expressed genes for RF-treated cells. At a lower frequency of 20 kHz, Ukuku et al. 32 , 33 found that RF energy treatments caused non-thermal damage to E. coli by disruption of the bacterial cell surface hydrophobicity and loss of negative ions, in turn leading to injury, leakage of cellular materials, and death. While these findings at 20 kHz give some indication of possible cell damage mechanisms at higher frequencies, results cannot be directly extrapolated. At a frequency of 13.56 MHz, Wust et al. 34 reported non-thermal membrane/cellular injury to cancer cells as ion fluxes adversely affected the proliferation and clonogenicity of the cells. While the latter study belongs to a different research domain, findings again indicate the presence of non-thermal effects on biological materials, further confirming the possibility of achieving positive results in the bacterial inactivation domain as well. In general, due to the limited amount of studies conducted at RF frequencies (i.e., 13.56, 27.12, 40.68 MHz), more research towards to the effects of RF exposure on microorganisms in foods is necessary. These studies should be conducted on different types of microorganisms and in different (food) matrices. Apart from the damage types assessed by Cui et al. 10 , other possible effects to be assessed in future studies include DNA damage and a more complete gene expression assessment. 
## Effect of food matrix rheology on RF inactivation: effect of food matrix viscosity and effect of a gelled food matrix 
 First, the effect of food matrix viscosity is discussed. For RF heating, cells were more resistant to RF heating in high-viscosity matrices than in low-viscosity matrices. This influence of food matrix viscosity on RF heating inactivation was similar to conventional heating. A possible explanation is the limited oxygen availability in the high-viscosity xanthan system compared to the low-viscosity liquid system. During the 16 h between model system inoculation and inactivation experiment (see Model system preparation and inoculation), cells in the xanthan system were possibly exposed to more stressing conditions compared to the liquid system due to the limited oxygen and nutrient availability and the local accumulation of acidic metabolites, both caused by high-viscosity-related diffusion limitations. This stressing environment may have caused cross-protective effects against subsequent thermal treatments, becoming apparent through decreased thermal inactivation and increased sublethal injury (i.e., TICC) in the higher viscosity xanthan system, and this for both RF and conventional heating 12 , 35 . This cross-protection can consist of phenomena such as reducing protein synthesis and metabolic activity, causing incomplete diffusion through multilayered structures, and altering colony physiology 36 . It should be noted that the heating rate in xanthan was lower than in the liquid. Slow heating rates may enhance the thermotolerance of bacteria 37 , 38 . However, in the current study, the absolute difference in the heating rates in the liquid and xanthan (both decreasing from 5 to 1 °C/min over the course of the treatments) was never larger than 1 °C/min. Consequently, critical heating rate differences that caused significant differences in thermotolerance in other studies were never reached 39 , 40 , meaning that the protective effect of food matrix viscosity was probably not due to the heating rate differences. 
 Secondly, the effect of a gelled food matrix is discussed. For conventional thermal treatments, it is known that cells which are surrounded by a gelled matrix experience alterations in cell development, membrane permeability, surface tension, osmotic pressure and metabolism, causing them to become more thermally resistant 12 , 13 . In the current study, the effect of a gelled matrix on RF heating inactivation does not always follow this trend, and was microorganism-dependent. L. monocytogenes cells embedded in a gelled matrix were more susceptible to RF heating than to conventional heating, indicating the presence of significant additional effects of RF in gelled matrices. The increase in resistance for gelled matrices which is observed for conventional heating seems to be less outspoken for RF heating at high temperatures. This effect may be related to the specific heating mechanism of RF. The large penetration depth of RF allows direct interaction with the microorganisms, with heating not being affected by limitations of conductive heat transfer in the gelled matrix as occurring during conventional heating. Espitia et al. 15 confirmed this phenomenon for the RF heating of L. monocytogenes in pure distilled water. The fact that this phenomenon did not exert a significant effect on microorganisms inactivation in liquid matrices in the current study may be related to the easier heat dissipation in liquid matrices. RF heating inactivation of S . Typhimurium seemed to be characterised by similar inactivation mechanisms compared to conventional thermal inactivation, although there were slight differences in the injuring mechanisms of the cells between the two heating methods. Therefore, the direct interaction of the RF energy and microbial cells, as observed for L. monocytogenes , seems not to occur for S . Typhimurium. As discussed before, the differences in protective effects against RF inactivation between the two microorganisms are possibly related to (i) the microorganism-dependent interaction with electromagnetic fields and/or (ii) specific microorganisms-dependent damage inflicted by the electromagnetic waves. 
## Conclusions 
 Specific microorganism-dependent indirect effects of RF heating at higher temperatures, additional to purely thermal inactivation effects, were brought to light. For L. monocytogenes , these indirect effects occurred only in the aqueous gel, while for S . Typhimurium, effects only occurred in the low and high viscosity liquids. Possible hypotheses for this phenomenon are fast heat development in microorganisms and damage caused to the microorganisms by electromagnetic waves. 
 The effect of food matrix rheology on RF heating inactivation was also investigated. First of all, increasing liquid matrix viscosity led to reduced RF heating inactivation of both microorganisms, similar to the effect for conventional heating. Secondly, the effect of the presence of a gelled matrix on RF heating inactivation was microorganism-specific. For S . Typhimurium, an increased resistance to RF heating inactivation was occurring in gelled matrices, similar to conventional heating. For L. monocytogenes , however, this increase in resistance was smaller than for conventional heating, possibly indicating direct RF energy absorption of Listeria cells unrelated to conductive heat transfer. 
 Future studies on indirect effects of RF energy on microorganisms should focus on the microscopic and molecular scale, e.g., DNA damage and gene expression. In addition, more knowledge has to be acquired on the effect of other food microstructural aspects on RF heating inactivation, e.g., the presence of fat droplets and/or fat content.
