## 1. Introduction 
 1 , 2 , 3 ]. The increasing demand for advanced in vitro models, driven by the need for improved physiological relevance and adherence to the 3Rs (replacement, reduction, and refinement of animal experimentation), has accelerated the development of three-dimensional (3D) cell-culture systems in fundamental research, drug development, and regenerative medicine. Compared with conventional two-dimensional cultures, 3D cellular models better reproduce tissue architecture, cell–cell interactions, and physiological functions, thereby improving the predictive value of in vitro studies [ 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 ]. 
 8 ]. The aim of 3D cell culture is to provide a microenvironment that more closely resembles in vivo conditions, thereby creating more representative models for preclinical studies, drug screening, and disease modeling [ 9 , 10 ]. 
 11 , 12 ]. Such variability can compromise experimental reproducibility and hinder large-scale implementation of these models in research and industrial settings. Consequently, the development of robust cryopreservation strategies is essential to enable the long-term storage, distribution, and standardization of encapsulated systems. Efficient cryopreservation would facilitate the establishment of ready-to-use cell banks, reduce production costs, improve batch consistency, and increase accessibility of complex cellular systems. 
 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 ]. This phenomenon happens during freezing and can compromise the structural integrity and viability of these complex constructs [ 17 ]. The stresses caused can thus impair cellular viability and metabolic activity, ultimately limiting the functional relevance and immediate usability of tridimensional models following thawing. 
 18 , 19 ] and recently in vivo [ 3 , 20 ]. Alginate-based microencapsulation has emerged as a promising approach for providing a three-dimensional microenvironment favorable for the maintenance of hepatic phenotype [ 21 ]. More recently, studies showed that hydrogels may also confer physical protection against freezing-induced damage [ 22 ]. Encapsulation within an alginate matrix therefore represents a practical strategy for the cryopreservation of 3D structures, providing protection against freezing damage and structural support and facilitating handling and storage. In encapsulated systems, where alginate influences heat and mass transfer, optimizing the cooling rate is particularly critical [ 23 , 24 ]. The cooling rate was selected based on studies showing that slower freezing improves cell survival and function compared to the conventional −1 °C/min [ 25 , 26 ], where cooling rates between 0.25 and 0.5 °C/min have been explored, supporting the use of a −0.3 °C/min rate. 
 27 , 28 ]. Additionally, the use of a programmable controlled-rate freezer allows the simultaneous processing of a larger number of cryovials and greater sample volumes, thereby enhancing reproducibility and scalability for downstream applications. 
 29 ]. In addition, its xenogeneic origin poses risks of pathogen transmission and immune reactions, making it unsuitable for clinical translation [ 30 ]. These limitations are particularly relevant for hepatic models, where functional readouts are highly sensitive to microenvironmental variations [ 31 ]. DMSO remains the standard permeating cryoprotectant owing to its efficacy in limiting intracellular ice formation. Nevertheless, its use is associated with well-documented cytotoxic effects (neurotoxicity [ 32 ] and functional effects [ 33 , 34 , 35 ]), including alterations in cell viability and modulation of hepatic gene expression programs. In hepatic systems, residual DMSO after thawing may also confound metabolic and functional assessments [ 36 ]. Collectively, these limitations highlight the need for xeno-free and reduced-DMSO or DMSO-free cryopreservation formulations, particularly for the preservation of hepatic aggregates intended for functional and toxicological studies.

## 2. Materials and Methods 
## 2.1. Cell Culture 
 ® cells (Biopredic International, Rennes, France) were cultured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were maintained in William’s E phenol red-free medium (Gibco) supplemented with HepaRG growth additive (ADD710C, Biopredic International, Rennes, France). The cells were seeded at an initial density of 2.5 × 10 4 cells/cm 2 and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO 2 . The medium was renewed three times per week until full confluency and differentiation at 14 days of culture. For further expansion or experimental use, HepaRG cells were detached enzymatically using 0.25% trypsin-ethylenediaminetetra acetic acid (EDTA) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Grand Island, New York, NY, USA). 
## 2.2. Alginate Microencapsulation 
 w / v ) alginate solution (Manucol LKK (FMC BioPolymer, Billingstad, Asker, Norway) (Guluronic/Mannuronic ratio 30:70), sterilized with successive filtrations with 0.8, 0.45, and 0.22 µm pore size membrane filters (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) at a density of 10 × 10 6 cells per mL. The suspension was loaded into a 5 mL syringe connected to the air-jet encapsulation setup linked to a 24 G nozzle. The air pressure was adjusted to 3.5 units, and the syringe pump was set to a flow rate of 9.5 mL/min. Uniform droplets were generated by coaxial air flow, enabling the needle to vibrate inside a nozzle. Beads were collected directly in a 154 mM CaCl 2 gelling bath. Beads were allowed to polymerize for 15 min at 4 °C, then washed with sterile culture medium to remove excess calcium ions. Encapsulated cells were maintained in HepaRG complete medium in a shaking plate (75 RPM), with medium replacement three times per week. Cultures were maintained for up to 13 days prior to analysis. One batch of encapsulation was composed of serial encapsulations of 750,000 cells/750 µL of sterile sodium alginate 1.5% ( w / v ).

## 2.5. Metabolic Activity Measurement by Presto Blue HS Assay

In viable cells, mitochondrial enzymes convert a non-fluorescent substrate: resazurin (blue, non-fluorescent) converts a fluorescent product (resorufin) by accepting electrons from cellular metabolic processes. A standard curve of known resorufin concentration was used to quantify metabolic activity.

## 4. Discussion 
## 4.1. Protocol-Level Differences in Post-Thaw Structure and Function

However, the protocols differed not only in nominal cooling rate (−0.3 versus approximately −1 °C/min) but also in the −10 °C hold and the −80 °C transfer and storage sequence. The present data therefore cannot demonstrate that −0.3 °C/min is intrinsically superior to −1 °C/min; a design varying one process parameter at a time would be required. 
## 4.2. Cryopreservation Negatively Impacts Cellular Activities
