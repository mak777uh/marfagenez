## Abstract 
 Globally, the companies that make commercial use of bamboo culms produce different kinds of solid waste rich in lignocellulosic biomass, which in some cases is not used and is discarded in landfills or incinerated in the open air; losing the possibility of recovering them and using them in other productive sectors. The research objective were to produce a biochar from Guadua agustifolia  Kunth sawdust, evaluate its potential environmental and agricultural use, obtain a biochar/TiO 2  composite to inactivate Escherichia coli and use the biochar as a soil conditioner in medicinal plants producing phenolic compounds and flavonoids. Biochar composite (produced at 300 °C for 1 h) involved TiO 2 at 450 °C for 1 h for inactivation of E. coli (initial concentration: 6.5 ± 0.3 Log 10 CFU mL − 1 ). For agriculture, 2% biochar was used to evaluate B. pilosa L. and G. angustifolia  plant growth for 90 days. The biochar/TiO 2  composite had a high photocatalytic activity on E. coli , generating a final count of 1.97 ± 0.2 Log 10 CFU mL − 1 after 60 min. Biochar (2%) increased the total phenol and flavonoid content in the medicinal plant B. pilosa L. and total phenols in G. angustifolia , tested at the nursery stage. This study provides new information on the conversion and use of G. angustifolia sawdust as an alternative for new bio-based materials with environmental and agricultural applications. In addition, obtaining biochar and composite could positively impact the bamboo production chain in Colombia because of renewable and globally accepted alternatives that help capture gaseous emissions causing the greenhouse effect.

## Introduction 
 In Colombia, several thermal floors favour a wide range of plant growth, with timber trees and grasses being of great commercial relevance 1 , which can be cultivated and sustainably harvested to obtain materials for different productive sectors of the country 2 . Associated with grasses, Guadua angustifolia Kunth is the most important bamboo species in Colombia and one of the largest in the world 3 , 4 . Due to Guadua angustifolia Kunth´s physical and chemical characteristics, technological interest in this variety of bamboo has increased, with 90% of it being used as a construction element in civil works and as an architectural element for housing 2 . On the other hand, it also serves handicrafts, kitchen utensils, musical instruments, furniture manufacture, etc 5 . Chemical compounds with medical potential, as well as additives and food supplements, can result from using bamboo leaves and shoots 6 – 8 . In Colombia, there are natural and planted “guadua cultivation” in the departments of Quindío (44%), Valle (14%), Cauca (13%), Risaralda (12%), Huila (6%), Antioquia (4%), Caldas (3%), Cundinamarca (1%) and Boyacá (1%) 1 , 2 , in which the main productive actors are farm owners, associated and independent producers, timber warehouses, artisans, furniture manufacturers and builders 1 . In this sense, the “guadua” production generates a representative number of direct and indirect jobs benefiting different communities 2 . 
 This productive sector also contributes to the fulfillment of some of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) of the United Nations in its agenda for 2030 9 by generating direct and indirect jobs in rural sectors and in cities, promoting the country’s development (Goal 1: end of poverty) 10 . By using residual lignocellulosic biomass, solid, liquid, and gaseous products with energy capacity can be obtained that help reduce the excessive use of petroleum-based fuels (Objective 7: accessible and non-polluting energy) 10 . Since guadua is a fast-growing graminea (5–6 years) compared to a timber tree (20–40 years), it becomes an alternative to reduce deforestation because its root system conserves and stabilizes the soil, making it an appropriate plant species for planting in hillside areas and along the banks of water sources (Objective 12: Responsible production and consumption) 10 . Finally, natural or planted guadua can store carbon that can range between 149.9 and 328.4 t CO 2 ha −1 , values that can vary according to age, area, and Latin American country where the plantation is located 10 , 11 ; representing a substantial contribution to the capture of gaseous emissions such as CO 2 9 , 12 . 
 Despite contributing significantly to the fulfilment of the SDGs, in Latin America, this productive sector faces a series of environmental and sustainability challenges 13 , remarking the generation of large amounts of solid waste, rich in lignocellulosic biomass 10 . In the entire production chain, up to 80% of waste can result, consisting of culms, qualified leaves, bark, chips, sawdust, shavings, and material that does not meet the quality specifications for marketing 12 – 17 . These are discarded in the plantations, incinerated uncontrolled, or disposed of in sanitary landfills, unsustainable environmental practices that favour the lost opportunity to re-incorporate them into the production chain 12 . In particular, sawdust contains variable amounts of Boron, which limits its direct use in soils because it affects plant growth and soil microorganisms and can leach into surface water, causing pollution 17 – 19 . 
 There are different physical and chemical processes to reduce the environmental impact of raw sawdust, with or without Boron offering conversion alternatives, such as the pyrolysis process, in which a controlled thermal conversion of sawdust occurs at temperatures ranging from 300 to 700 °C, in the absence of O 2 or reduced O 2 conditions 20 . The pyrolysis process generates higher value-added bioproducts, such as oils, gas and biochar 21 – 24 . The production of oils is at high temperatures and short residence times (fast pyrolysis) favoured; on the contrary, in slow pyrolysis, biochar (33%), oils (32%) and gas (35%) are produced in similar proportions, since it is carried out at low temperatures (maximum at 500 °C) and longer residence times than in slow pyrolysis 21 , 22 , 25 , 26 . With bamboo waste, pyrolysis is faster than wood waste due to the presence of potassium silicon; these elements act as a catalyst in combustion, contribute to oil cracking and favour gas production 20 , 27 , 28 . 
 The production of biochar from bamboo residues is one of the accepted methods for CO 2 removal (CDR), easily scalable that would comply with the decisions taken at the Climate Change Conference of Paris, where the Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) focused on the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions 29 , to reduce the concentrations of these gases and reduce their effect on climate change. In a study conducted by Lefebvre et al. 30 they reported the decisive role that biochar plays within global strategies for CO 2 removal, with a removal potential of 6.23 ± 0.24% of total greenhouse gases (GHG) in 155 countries studied (2020 data) and a removal of more than 10% of the emissions generated in 28 countries 30 . The study included part of the forest residues to apply the mathematical models; therefore, it is possible to think that bamboo residues were also part of this study. In this sense, the authors reported that in America, the countries with the potential for carbon dioxide removal through biochar production (Mt CO 2 e year − 1 ) are The United States, Brazil, Argentina, Canada and The Mexican United States, with values ranging between 468 and 25 Mt CO 2 e year − 1 30 . Although their study did not directly study Colombia, the authors present in the global map of CO 2 removal by biochar that our country could have a removal potential of 10 to 25 Mt CO 2 e year − 1 30 . 
 When the above is related to the bamboo production sector, according to the literature, it is expected that 80% of lignocellulosic residues could be produced from the processing of one ton of culms 12 , which is equivalent to 800 kg of lignocellulosic residues that, when converted into biochar at a temperature of 300 °C, could become approximately 264 kg of this new biomaterial (33% yield obtained in this study); biochar would also provide ash and fixed carbon, which could slowly disseminated into soils; therefore, it could be considered the product of a sustainable negative carbon technology that helps to counteract the effects of climate change and produces valorized biomaterials with diverse applications 10 , 12 . For example, for the production of renewable biofuels, authors such as Osman et al. 31 reported that the thermochemical conversion of solid waste represents approximately 20% of the biofuels used worldwide, and regarding biochar biodiesel production showed yields that vary between 32.8 and 97.75%. Additionally, it can serve as a catalyst or support (anode or cathode) in microbial fuel cells for hydrogen production 30 . 
 In relation to other potential environmental applications, biochar from “guadua” waste involves its use as an adsorbent for pollutant removal in soil and water 16 , 20 , 24 – 26 , 28 , 32 , 33 . Additionally, in a mixture with inorganic and organic compounds, bio-based composites have been developed that further enhance the adsorption capacity of biochar 17 , 34 – 36 and can be used as hybrid catalysts that increase the oxidation efficiency in photocatalysis or photo Fenton processes with ultraviolet and or visible light 23 , 37 – 45 . 
 Several authors reported the development of photocatalysts based on biochar and semiconducting oxides. In their work, Jin et al. 38 developed a recyclable ZnO-based photocatalyst supported on bamboo laminates, which maintained the removal efficiency of the dye Rhodamine B above 70% during three cycles of operation. Prihatiningsih et al. 43 used calcined bamboo leaves and mixed their ashes with TiO 2 to obtain a composite that had a mixture of the crystalline phases anatase, rutile and silica present, which favoured the photocatalysis process with ultraviolet radiation. On the other hand, Wang et al. 39 , synthesised and characterised a composite with photocatalytic activity employing bamboo biochar and TiO 2 by sol-gel technique. The authors demonstrated that the composite had a spectral response in both the ultraviolet and visible regions, generating an alternative to the restriction that TiO 2 has concerning its response or photoexcitation in the ultraviolet spectrum. In 2019, biogenic silica from bamboo leaves served to synthesise a TiO 2 /SiO 2 -based catalyst, which had a higher surface area than each component separately and exhibited photocatalytic activity in the degradation of methylene blue 42 . Zhang et al. 23 developed and characterized a carbon aerogel from bamboo cellulose fibres, which served to produce a third-generation hybrid catalyst with TiO 2 . The catalyst had excellent photocatalytic activity in the degradation of methylene blue with visible light, thanks to the porosity provided by the activated carbon (AC), which generated more active sites for TiO 2 binding 23 . 
 Wang et al. 44 used untreated bamboo Phyllostachys heterocycle (four-year-old) laminates as substrates for TiO 2 @KH-570 coating to obtain a biogenic composite with promising physical and chemical characteristics for the Materials Science and Technology area, such as the development of supported photocatalysts, adsorbents, self-cleaning surfaces, electromagnetic surfaces, and others. Wang et al. 44 reported the optimisation of the production conditions for a catalyst supported on bamboo charcoal fibres for use in the photo Fenton process. From the response surface experimental design, they obtained a recyclable magnetic catalyst (TiO 2 /Fe 3 O 4 /PDA/CF), which could be used in wastewater treatment as it favoured the removal of 90% of the dye methylene blue under UV radiation 44 , 45 . 
 Astuti et al. 37 developed and characterised a hematite-biochar-based composite from bamboo Dendrocalamus asper through microwave-assisted pyrolysis, in which the photocatalytic activity was by methylene blue decolourisation demonstrated. The hematite-biochar (HBC) composite had high adsorption capacity at pH 9.0 and at 240 min removed 90.56% of methylene blue, proving that the use of microwaves for biochar production is a novel alternative to conventional pyrolysis processes. We have characterised Guadua angustifolia longitudinal cuts of culms of different ages and heights (data under review for publication), observing that the cuts obtained from the bottom mature in the strain zone had a high percentage of fibre, for which the laminates were transformed into laminar biochar by slow pyrolysis and served to deposit titanium dioxide with photocatalytic activity on E. coli by simple sedimentation at acid pH. 
 Concerning the agricultural potential of bamboo-biochar, its use as an amendment and or conditioner of soils is due to its porous nature and richness in highly condensed carbon, increasing water retention, improving soil pH, and the availability of nutrients for the plant 16 , 33 , 46 , serves as a support for beneficial microorganisms (bacteria, actinobacteria, yeast fungi, mycorrhizal fungi, among others) 16 , 47 , and favours the establishment of beneficial microbial communities which reduces irrigation and crop fertilisation costs 48 . Sahoo et al. 22 produced and characterised a biochar using pigeon peas and bamboo, using slow pyrolysis, to determine the effect of temperature (400, 500, and 600 °C) on the physical and chemical properties of the biochar. The most efficient pyrolysis temperature was 600 °C as it improved the surface area, total pore volume, ash content, and fixed carbon in both types of biochar, showing that raw biomass composition and temperature have a high effect on biochar yield and its physical and chemical properties, which directly affect its use as a soil organic product. 
 Chaturvedi et al. 16 published a review on the properties of bamboo to produce charcoal and biochar with various applications; concerning its use as an organic amendment, they observed that by applying between 1 and 3% of biochar, the growth rate of tomato plants increased attributed to the contribution of magnesium, nitrates, calcium and phosphorus, which the biochar produced at 300 and 400º C makes. On the other hand, they highlight the positive effect of biochar applied to soils, especially at the level of changes in porosity, aeration, soil structure, nutrient exchange capacity, and the biological activity of mycorrhizal fungi. 
 In bamboo crops the use of biochar alone or in a mixture with other organic products favours the production of phenolic compounds; these substances are secondary metabolites produced by embryophyte plants through a mixed synthesis route called the phenylpropanoid route 8 , 19 , 49 – 51 . These compounds fulfill different plant functions such as (1) chemo-attractant molecules such as genistein, daidzein, and naringin; (2) defense against pathogens in the rhizosphere and phyllosphere; (3) allelopathic substances; (4) modulation of reactive oxygen species ROS; (5): Auxin transporter; (6) photoreceptor substances in leaves protecting the plant from UV-B rays 51 , 52 . Flavonoids (a class of phenolic compounds) can be part of new pharmacological products for cancer treatment because, unlike chemotherapy treatments, their action is dual and specific 19 , 53 , 54 . 
 Due to the importance of phenolic compounds and flavonoids as phyto-drugs, global demand has increased, and there is a need to improve plant yields since the amount produced by plants is low and does not meet market demand 19 , 55 . Some of the alternatives evaluated are the use of soil conditioners (fresh plant residues, compost, vermicompost, or biochar) 19 , 32 , 51 , mineral fertilisation, and use of microbial inoculants containing plant growth-promoting bacteria and other mutualistic symbiotic microorganisms 19 , 51 . 
 Therefore, this article aimed to produce and characterise Guadua angustifolia  Kunth sawdust biochar to determine its possible environmental and agricultural applications. Environmentally, a Biochar/TiO 2 Sigma ® composite and its photocatalytic activity (UV 253 nm ) assayed in the inactivation of E. coli as an indicator of faecal contamination in wastewater. Biochar also served as a soil conditioner, and to evaluate its influence on the production of total phenolic compounds and total flavonoids by using plants with medicinal potential determined, such as Bidens pilosa L. and G. angustifolia Kunth.

 Biochar produced at 300 °C, 1 h (BGAK) changes all the parameters studied; pH values (7.61 ± 0.26), bulk density (0.116 ± 0.006 g cm − 3 ), water retention (55 ± 2%), and average porosity of 59 ± 3% increased concerning raw sawdust (Table  1 ). On the contrary, the electrical conductivity and moisture content decreased, obtaining 0.844 ± 0.331 mS cm − 1 and 2.62 ± 0.64% (Table  1 ). The results of the proximate analysis showed condensation of part of the organic Carbon in the biochar (2.45 ± 0.578% FC), ash increased (11.47 ± 1.54%), and part of the TOC lost (final value: 46.323 ± 1.513%) at the volatile fraction (final value: 86.08 ± 2.32%), (Table  1 ). The elements expressed in percentages also changed for GAKS, obtaining 66.92% of Carbon, 26.71% of Oxygen, 5.09% of Silicon, and 1.28% of Potassium, and the O/C ratio was 0.399, indicating changes in aromaticity, stability, and polarity of the biochar produced by 1 h at 300 °C (Table  1 ).

 Thermogravimetric analyses (TGA) for GAKS, BGAK (300 °C, 1 h) and BGAK/TiO 2 composite after calcination at 450 °C are in Fig. S2 Supplementary Material, respectively. Three different stages for mass loss ranging from 50 to 296, 296–400 and 400–700 °C were observed. In the first stage, GAKS and BGAK presented a slight mass loss compared to the BGAK/TiO 2 composite since the latter did not change at these temperatures (50–296 °C). At approximately 296 °C, stage 2 or active pyrolysis began, in which the maximum loss was observed in the GAKS, followed by the BGAK and again in the BGAK/TiO 2 composite, no significant changes were observed 56 . The passive pyrolysis started at 400 °C and continued up to 700 °C, determining that GAKS and BGAK continued to lose mass but were less pronounced than those observed in stage 2 56 . About the BGAK/TiO 2 composite, the changes in mass loss were similar to those obtained in stages 1 and 2, demonstrating that the composite has more thermal stability due to the mixture of an organic material rich in condensed carbon and an inorganic material with semiconducting properties such as TiO 2 57 . 
## Environmental application of biochar 
 When mixing BGAK with TiO 2 in an aqueous solution at pH 2.0, both materials remain dispersed and separated in the first minutes of contact. However, at the end of the wet impregnation process assisted by mechanical agitation (2 h, 120 r.p.m.) and sonication (15 min.), the solution changed resulting in a more homogeneous mixture, which slowly sedimented during the drying process at 70 °C for 12 h, obtaining a dark grey powder (10. 2 ± 0.8 g) which changed colour (light grey) when calcined at 450 °C for 1 h and the initial amount introduced into the muffle was reduced, obtaining 6.87 ± 0.54 g which is equivalent to a yield of 67.35 ± 1.8% at the composite synthesis end.

 Regarding the composite after calcination at 450 °C, in Fig.  3 d, cylindrical and elongated structures were observed, with a smoother surface than GAKS (100 μm). Additionally, pore formation in BGAK and TiO 2 was still surface distributed in several areas (20 μm) (Fig.  3 e). However, the red box in Fig.  3 e highlights that upon calcination at 450 °C, TiO 2 was added to a more, polymorphism was maintained, and possibly the size of the aggregates decreased (2 μm scale) (Fig.  3 e, at the zoom). After calcination at 450 °C for 1 h, the percentage of the elements varied, obtaining 12.58% for Carbon, 48.83% for Oxygen, 12.78% for Silicon, 25.81% for Ti, and no potassium was detected (Fig.  3 f). 
 Regarding the FTIR spectrum, as presented in Fig.  2 , in the BGAK/TiO 2 composites without calcination and after calcination, absorption bands between 667 and 682 cm − 1 could be associated with Ti-O-Ti bonds, which not detection occurred in GAKS (raw sawdust), (Fig.  2 ). The formation of Ti-O-Ti bonds in the composite could be one reason why its thermal stability was superior to GAKS and BGAK. In the three stages of thermal degradation, no mass losses higher than 10% were observed (Fig. S2 Supplementary Material). 
## Photocatalytic inactivation of E. coli 226 
 The effect of BGAK/TiO 2 composites concentration on the inactivation of E. coli 226 is in Fig.  4 a; the initial concentration of E. coli 226 for this experiment was approximately 7.3 ± 0.65 Log 10 CFU mL − 1 . The dark composted did not decrease the initial E. coli concentration at any of the tested concentrations (0.1, 0.25 and 0.5% w/v) ( p  > 0.005); colony counts remained in the same order of magnitude obtaining values of 7.9 ± 0.2, 7.5 ± 0.9 and 7.5 ± 0.8 Log 10 CFU mL − 1 , for 0.1%, 0.25% and 0.5% w/v of the composite (Fig.  4 a). The E. coli counts after exposure only to ultraviolet light (UV 253nm ) were 5.7 ± 0.1, 5.9 ± 0.5 and 5.8 ± 0.4 Log 10 CFU mL − 1 ; representing an average decrease of 1.9 ± 0.2 log units after 30 min of UV photolysis. In the inactivation by photocatalysis (BGAK/TiO 2 /UV 253nm ), the final counts were 6.9 ± 0.5, 5.7 ± 0.1 and 2.0 ± 0.9, Log 10 CFU mL − 1 , for the 0.1, 0.25 and 0.5% (w/v) composites concentrations, respectively. Finally, with the adsorption control and in the dark for the BGAK/TiO 2 composite, the slight decrease in E. coli counts was due to a process of adsorption of the cells to the composite and is not considered cell inactivation 58 .

## Discussion 
 Sawdust can represent up to 20% of the residues generated during the industrial utilisation of bamboo culms, and its composition can vary depending on the type of bamboo and the chemical or physical treatments carried out on the culms during their utilisation 14 , 15 . In Table  1 , the GAKS had pH values of 5.03 ± 0.31, possibly related to the presence of boric acid used to preserve the culms and decrease chemical and biological deterioration 59 , 60 . The high percentage of volatile carbon (95.45 ± 1.45%) was associated with the cellulose and hemicellulose content, which, being fewer resistant polymers than lignin, are susceptible to a certain percentage of loss at temperatures above 200 °C 25 , 61 . On the other hand, the percentage of fixed carbon was low (1.020 ± 0.245%), which could be related to a lower lignin content in the sawdust 36 , 60 and the ash (3.53 ± 0.89%), which is the inorganic fraction of the sawdust were from silicon and potassium, generating an increase in electrical conductivity (1.022 ± 0.1 mS cm − 1 ) 15 . The presence of these two elements in bamboo sawdust is associated with the outer layer of the culm (epidermis), which is constituted by parenchymatous tissue rich in silicon 42 , and fast-growing grasses such as Guadua angustifolia can have high potassium contents 62 . The O/C ratio of 0.710 indicated that GAKS had a high content of hydrophilic groups associated with water, aliphatic and phenolic structures 25 , 63 . Similar values were by Sahoo et al. 22 obtained when using bamboo residues to produce biochar and had a low H/C ratio and high O/C ratio, confirming their aromatic nature with high hydrophilicity. 
 The biochar (BGAK) produced at 300 °C for 1 h showed physical and chemical changes due to the thermal transformation of sawdust rich in lignocellulosic biomass (Fig.  1 ). At this temperature, interstitial water is lost by dehydration, resulting in lower moisture content in the biochar (2.62 ± 0.64%), which in turn generates a material with lower density compared to GAKS (0.116 ± 0.006 g cm − 3 ) and smaller particle size (Fig. S1 d Supplementary Material) 44 , 61 , . The partial removal of polar and non-polar fractions from sawdust at 300 °C, such as aliphatic compounds, tars, and extractable compounds 64 , allowed BGAK to acquire higher water holding capacity (55 ± 2%) compared to raw sawdust (40 ± 3%) and increased its porosity (59 ± 3%); resulting in a larger surface area and the formation of pores of different sizes and shapes due to the release of volatile intermediates such as CO, CO 2 , CH 4 , and H 2 O (Fig.  1 c) 47 , 65 , . Additionally, the release of volatile compounds and the concentration of Si and K in the biochar increased the pH value from 5.03 ± 0.31 in GAKS to 7.61 ± 0.26 in BGAK (Table  1 ). Similar results obtained by Sahoo et al. 22 produced a biochar from bamboo and observed that as the temperature of the pyrolysis process increased, the pH increased (initial value 5.23 ± 0.0.04) to obtain values between 7.2 ± 0.0.26 in BGAK (Table  1 ). 
 Additionally, the results of the TGA analysis suggest that GAKS was the material with the lowest thermal stability of the three studied; the slight initial mass loss (50–150 °C) could be related to the evaporation of small amounts of water adsorbed to GAKS but are not responsible for modifying the chemical structure of the sawdust 66 , 67 . The changes between 220 and 296 °C suggest the beginning of the thermal decomposition of cellulose, and between 296 and 400 °C, the decomposition of hemicellulose 60 , 68 , 69 would be taking place (Fig. S2 Supplementary Material). These polymers, being homoglucans (cellulose) and heteroglucans (hemicellulose), in which different kinds of glycosidic bonds predominate, decompose at lower temperatures compared to the lignin polymer, which starts its decomposition at temperatures close to 300 °C 68 , 70 and continues between 400 and 700 °C, but at a lower rate 25 , 57 , 61 , 71 . Lignin as an aromatic polymer requires higher temperatures to reach complete volatilisation, and therefore, at 300 °C, a reorganisation process of aromatic subunits such as p-hydroxyphenyl, guaiacyl, and syringyl, which are considered intermediates of the thermal treatment of lignin that contribute to the formation of condensed carbon in biochar, took place 47 , 72 . 
 The percentages of fixed carbon in biochar were low (2.45 ± 0.578%) compared to other authors´ results, such as Sahoo et al. 22 , where the variation could be because the biochar evaluated by the authors was produced under a controlled and oxygen-free atmosphere. On the contrary, in this study, biochar production was carried out under reduced oxygen conditions, which resulted in volatilisation losses of 86.08 ± 2.32% 62 , 73 . The ash percentage (11.47 ± 1.54%) increased in the biochar concerning GAKS, and the percentages of Silicon (5.09%) and Potassium (1.28%) increased when compared to GAKS (Table  1 ) (Fig.  1 b, c). The reorganization, condensation of fixed carbon and the increase of ashes in the biochar could contribute to its thermal stability since TGA studies found more degradation at 296–400 °C (Fig. S2 Supplementary Material). Chaudhuri et al. 69 produced a biochar from bamboo waste at 400 °C and observed that the increase in fixed carbon and ashes increased thermal stability and improved its heat capacity 69 . 
 The decrease in the O/C ratio from 0.710 (BAKS) to 0.399 (BGAK) suggests that the biochar is mature and less polar due to the loss of OH and aliphatic groups 63 , 72 . The O/C ratio of 0.399 was close to 0.3, indicating that BGAK is stable and could be used in agriculture to improve the soil’s physical and chemical properties 47 . Authors such as Li et al., (2019) reported that a biochar with O/C ratios of 0.2 or lower could have a half-life in soil of more than 100 years and would have great potential to sequester carbon in soil, contributing to the reduction of gaseous emissions from soil 47 , 63 . 
 SEM analysis allows identifying the surface characteristics of GAKS and BGAK, providing valuable information about the surface and porosity of the two materials. In Fig.  1 a, b, GAKS had a rough and irregular surface, with the presence of cracks and wrinkles, which could be a characteristic of bamboo residues, and the mechanical sawing process of the culms could further increase the alteration of the sawdust surface 22 , 39 , 44 . Concerning BGAK, the observed changes in surface and morphological characteristics could be due to two reasons: The formation of different shapes and sizes pores was due to the release of volatile fractions during the pyrolysis process and with the different temperature gradients through which the raw sawdust passed until reached the constant temperature of 300 °C and stayed for one hour 22 , 24 , 37 , 65 . The transformation from a rough and wrinkled surface in GAKS (Fig.  1 b, d) to a less rough surface and more homogeneous one (Fig.  1 d) could be due to the rearrangement and condensation of carbon in the biochar at the expense of lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose, partially modified by the heat treatment at 300 °C 38 , 63 , 72 . 
 Other results that complemented and supported the changes generated by the pyrolysis process were the infrared spectroscopy analyses (Fig.  2 ). In the two materials (GAKS and BGAK), no defined signals were observed between 3400 –3200 cm − 1 corresponding to O-H stretches that are hydroxyl groups associated with adsorbed water 28 , 42 , 61 , which could be related to the drying of the materials before their characterisation and their low moisture percentages (Table  1 ) 36 , . The signal with low intensity between 2357 and 2368 cm − 1 could be C-H stretches in the CH 2 and CH 3 groups of the cellulose, which has a crystalline fraction, was not volatilised totally at 300 °C and possibly part of this polymer condensed on the fixed carbon of the biochar (Fig.  2 ) 39 , . Signals could also be related to the stretching of the amine groups (-NH 2 + and -NH + ), observed in crude materials and biochars produced from bamboo waste, according to research by Rajesh et al. 24 . The authors observed that the acidified biochar without contact with Palladium had signals between 2407 –2299 cm − 1 , which they identified as stretching of amino groups. The signals between 1583 –1201 cm − 1 are related to the presence of lignin and its ether bonds (C = C and C-O-C) presented a slight shift that agrees with the reorganisation and condensation of the carbon in the biochar (Fig.  2 ) 61 . Similar results to those obtained when producing biochar by conventional pyrolysis and by the hydrothermal method, in both lignin-related bonds do not disappear when producing biochar; on the contrary, their signal shifts and favours the formation of functional groups with heavy metal capacity such as Cadmium 36 . Other signals observed with low intensity correspond to cellulose and hemicellulose (1024 and 1122 cm − 1 ), presented a slight shift and softened, which helps to support that the totality of both polymers, were unreleased in the volatile fraction whose percentage in the BGAK was 86.08 ± 2.32% (Fig.  2 ; Table  1 ). 
 The wet impregnation method to produce the BGAK/TiO 2 composite mixed BGAK and TiO 2 in an acidified aqueous solution; the two materials had separate pH of 7.61 ± 0.26 for biochar and 6.0 ± 0.18 for TiO 2 ( https://www.sigmaaldrich.com , 2024). When placed in a solution with a pH of 2.0, their surface charges were modified, causing them to acquire positive charges due to hydrogen ions in the solution 61 . This modification could increase the electrostatic repulsion between BGAK and TiO 2 , slowing down the sedimentation process 61 . In turn, BGAK had a higher density (0.116 ± 0.006 g cm- 3 ) than TiO 2 (0.066 ± 0.002 g cm − 3 ), favoured BGAK to sediment before TiO 2, and acted as a substrate or support for TiO 2 aggregates 61 . For this reason, SEM images at different magnifications show that TiO 2 was not equally distributed over the whole BGAK surface (Fig.  3 a, b). The TiO 2 dispersed on the BGAK was responsible for modifying the atomic percentages, especially for C and O, because C decreased concerning the BGAK and O 2 increased, as presented in the EDS analysis (Fig.  3 c, f). The increase in oxygen percentage could be associated with the two O 2 atoms in the semiconducting oxide (TiO 2 ) 39 , 41 . 
 After calcination of the composite at 450 °C for 1 h, the BGAK underwent a second heat treatment, but with an increase of 150 °C concerning the pyrolysis temperature (300 °C), which again generated surface, chemical and physical changes in the TiO 2 -impregnated BGAK (Fig.  3 d, e). On the other side, the labile carbon could be volatilised to a decrease in the percentage of C (12.58%), and the biochar fixed carbon could be rearranged again to form even more condensed and stable structures (Table  1 ) 47 , which was evident from SEM studies in which the formation of elongated cylindrical structures with a smoother surface occurred (Fig.  3 d, e), which were not present in the non-calcined BGAK/TiO 2 (Fig.  3 a, b). In turn, the TiO 2 aggregates result compacted by calcination, contributing to the decrease in particle size and consequently larger surface area to be UV light irradiated (Fig.  3 e, top left zoom) 39 . A similar result by Wang et al. 23 when producing a biocarbon/TiO 2 -based composite and observing the formation of nanochannels after performing calcination, these channels could transport electrons to decrease the e/h recombination process when the TiO 2 photoexcitation process is performed 39 . On the other hand, IR analysis showed that upon calcination, the signals between 1583 –1201 cm − 1 and 667–682 cm − 1 intensified, suggesting that the oxygenated carbon groups (C = O and C-O) were more pronounced and favoured the anchoring of TiO 2 to the biochar. 
 The presence of C = O, C-O and Ti-O-Ti bonds in the composite could be responsible for conferring its thermal stability since, as can be observed in Fig. S2 Supplementary Material, the composite did not present a mass loss higher than 10% in any of the thermal decomposition phases revealed in the TGA analysis. Ba et al. 57 , suggested that TiO 2 can act as a reinforcing material when coating bamboo fibres to develop reinforced structural materials 57 . Authors reported that mixing biochar with different semiconducting oxides improves photocatalytic efficiency. Wang et al. 39 fabricated a composite with bamboo biochar and TiO 2 at 800 °C, observing that the thermal treatment favoured the formation of the state and rutile phases, whose crystallinity was high, being a fundamental parameter for the photocatalysis processes since if the material is not crystalline, the photoexcitation process and formation of the e-/h + pairs would not take place 39 . 
 Wang and Wang 74 , reported that metal oxides attached to biochar increase the adsorption of negatively charged contaminants (anionic dyes and possibly microorganisms), which would favour the early stages of the photocatalysis process in which adsorption to the semiconductor surface is essential for reactive oxygen species to perform photocatalytic oxidation 74 . Weidner et al., 75 reported that biochar with TiO 2 has more active sites for adsorbing contaminants, and biochar helps to decrease the recombination process by acting as an acceptor for the photogenerated electrons 75 . Finally, biochar could decrease the GAP of TiO 2 , allowing it to extend its spectral response to the visible spectrum, which would favour the photocatalysis process without the need to use ultraviolet light lamps 76 . 
 As presented in Fig.  4 a, the inactivation of E. coli 226 by photocatalysis was dependent on the concentration of the BGAK/TiO 2 composite, results that suggest that a higher amount of the TiO 2 composite provided more catalytically active centres to absorbed more photons and consequently generated more charge carriers (e-CB /h + VB pairs). These migrate to the surface of the photocatalyst and participate in redox reactions 77 ; for example, photogenerated holes (h + VB) react with adsorbed water (oxidative reactions) to form hydroxyl radicals (OH · ) and hydrogen ions (H + ), in turn, h + can react with hydroxyl ions (OH − ) and formed more OH · 78 . Conversely, in reductive reactions e-(CB) reduces O 2 (photogenerated electron acceptor) to form superoxide ion (O 2 ·− ), H 2 O 2, and other species derived from the same hydrogen peroxide (hydroperoxyl radical: HO 2 · ) that also contribute to the inactivation of bacteria such as E. coli 77 , 79 – 81 . 
 The results obtained in this study agree with those reported by authors such as Kassahun et al. 82 , who suggested that in photocatalysis, there is a direct relationship between the concentration of the catalyst and its photocatalytic activity due to higher photon absorption until the adsorption equilibrium of the TiO 2 particles is reached 83 . Additionally, the selected concentration of BGAK/TiO 2 (0.5% w/v) did not affect the passage of ultraviolet light and neither generate a shielding effect that would block the adsorption of photons by the electrons in the VB. 
 Another factor that favoured the photocatalytic inactivation of E. coli was the pH of the solution (initial value: 5.4 ± 0.1); under this condition, the BGAK/TiO 2 composite could acquire a positive surface charge due to the presence of O-H 2 + and O-OH + 37 , 80 , 84 and generated an electrostatic interaction with the functional groups of the bacterial wall (COOH − ) and in this way, the E. coli cells, could be placed on the composite, which when observed by SEM at low magnification had a size of more than 100 μm (Fig.  3 d). The cells were in direct contact with the TiO 2 aggregates deposited on the biochar and promoted the different inactivation mechanisms associated with reactive species and mechanical damage by the TiO 2 particles 82 . 
 Evaluation of the composites concentrations in the dark showed no decrease in counts, which means that the BGAK/TiO 2 composites and the initial pH of 5.4 ± 0.1 did not exert a negative effect on E. coli , and the inactivation could be due to the photocatalysis processes with the composites and UV photolysis (UV light only control), (Fig.  4 a). On the other hand, with UV light alone (UV photolysis) there was an average decrease of 1.9 ± 0.08 Log 10 CFU mL − 1 attributed to two possible mechanisms: The first one related to the direct absorption of UV light photons by nucleic acids and proteins 85 , since nucleic acids, contain monosaccharides and nucleotides with absorption maximum between 200 and 265 nm within the UV light range 86 . In DNA, two thymines form pyrimidine photodimers called cyclobutane pyrimidine (CPDs) and pyrimidine-6,4-pyrimidone (6,4PP), which are responsible for distorting the double helix and blocking DNA replication 85 , 86 . The second mechanism involves the photolysis of water and the production of OH · radicals but at a lower rate when compared to those produced when a semiconducting material is present alone or as part of a composite 87 . 
 In the kinetics of photocatalytic inactivation with the BGAK/TiO 2 composite, an accelerated decrease in E. coli concentration occurred during the first 60 min due to the production of OH · and other reactive O 2 or H 2 O 2 species 86 , 88 . The bacterial cell inactivation involves several steps that affect the cell structures and depend on the type of bacteria 86 , 88 , 89 . 
 The OH · primarily attacks the outer and inner membranes and generates peroxidation of polyunsaturated phospholipid 86 , 88 , causing alteration of permeability, osmotic imbalance and release of intracellular components, such as potassium and sodium ions 86 , 90 , cellular respiration is reduced by the photooxidation of coenzyme A 86 , 88 , the cell wall result less sensitive to reactive oxygen species than membranes, but as exposure time passes during the photocatalysis process, reactive oxygen species (ROS) begin to pass through the wall, becoming evident structural changes such as increased roughness, pore formation, fractures and ultimately complete disintegration with the release of amino acids such as N-acetyl glucosamine which can be oxidised to release CO 2 and amino groups 89 , 90 . 
 In photolysis, the first phase lasted 15 min., and the population decrease was from 6.57 ± 0.5 Log 10 CFU mL − 1 to 3.53 ± 0.1 Log 10 CFU mL − 1 , later a slowdown (tail) occurred between 15 and 30 min of exposure (Fig.  4 b), and secondary reactivation occurred between 30 and 60 min (Fig.  4 b). This behaviour of the surviving bacteria (shoulder effect) is related with different self-repair mechanisms, that involves catalase (E.C. 1.11.1.6), superoxide dismutase (E.C. 1.15.1.1) and photolyase (E.C. 4.1.99.3); enzymes, which repair pyrimidine dimers in the DNA molecule 87 , 88 . At the same time, the release of intracellular components due to cell lysis competed with the available OH · radicals, allowing cells that did not exhibit advanced injury to recover, causing the shoulder effect 83 . The intracellular components and products of mineralisation released also often have a protective function for living or lightly injured cells by acting as a screen blocking the passage of UV light 87 , 89 , 91 . 
 Concerning the reuse of the BGAK/TiO 2 composite, the results suggest that the inactivation up to cycle five is related to the photocatalysis process and from cycle six onwards UV photolysis would be the treatment responsible for the inactivation and to a lesser extent the action of the TiO 2 composite, due to the possible saturation with the surviving bacteria or with the cellular debris, since they block the active sites of the semiconductor that by having less quantity of reactive oxygen spices, the bacteria could activate repair mechanisms, due to which the cells recover and grow in the agarized medium 92 . 
 In relation with the use of biochar as soil conditioner, in both B. pilosa and G. angustifolia , the BGAK addition favoured the production of phenolic compounds, with results similar to those reported by different authors. Deng et al. 93 observed an increase of up to 30%, concerning controls, in the content of total polyphenols and total flavonoids in Cyclocarya paliurus plants planted in substrates with biochar, Phares et al. 94 reported similar increases in total phenols and total flavonoids in Vigna uguiculata when biochar was part of the planting substrate, and Zulfiqar et al. 95 worked on Alpinia zerumbet , state that the content of total phenols and total flavonoids increased by 15 and 1.5%, relative to the control. Plant changes occur in response to the physicochemical properties of the soil modifications 96 , 97 . At present work, the soil with BGAK showed a slightly higher value in electrical conductivity (EC) compared to the soil, where it increased from 0.36 to 0.42 dS m − 1 for B. pilosa and from 0.17 to 0.26 dS m − 1 for G. angustifolia . Variations in EC can affect the accumulation of secondary metabolites such as phenolic compounds 98 and plant growth. Our results agree with those reported 99 for Capsicum annuum L plants, where the higher EC improved the nutraceutical quality but decreased the plant biomass. Similar results occurred in different onion varieties, where the EC presented a highly positive correlation ( p  < 0.01) with the content of total phenols and total flavonoids 100 , 101 ; reporting that the increase in EC stimulates the production of phenolic acids, in this case, gallic acid, and flavonoids such as quercetin, myricetin, rutin and morin in fruits of Jaltomata procumbens . 
 Biochar increases tolerance to salt stress by adsorbing ions such as sodium 102 , 103 . Our results showed that the treatments containing BGAK increased the concentration of the element concerning the treatments without biochar; the variations were from 56 to 100 mg kg − 1 in B. pilosa and from 50.3 to 52 mg kg − 1 in G. angustifolia . These variations can generate salt stress in plants, which increases the production of reactive oxygen species that can cause damage to membrane lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, with a subsequent reduction in crop productivity 104 . In response to this damage, plants increase the synthesis of compounds such as flavonoids and phenols 105 , 106 . Another effect reported for the increase of sodium concentration in the soil substrate is the negative interaction with nutrients such as potassium, calcium, and magnesium, which leads to lower plant absorption of these elements 104 and the absorption of P due to the higher absorption of S, hurting plant growth and causing a positive effect on the synthesis of antioxidant compounds 107 . For biochar effect on potassium in the planting substrate, in this study, the element content increased in the BGAK treatments from 61.2 to 304 mg kg − 1 in B. pilosa and from 61.8 to 87 mg kg − 1 in G. angustifolia , increases attributed to the concentration during the pyrolysis process 108 . In addition to the direct supply of the element, biochar favours its solubilisation and availability in the soil by increasing its retention capacity in cation exchange sites 109 , 110 and the activity of organic acid-producing microorganisms. Despite the benefits in plant metabolism of this macronutrient, its excess causes a reduction of chlorophyll content in leaves, reducing the photosynthetic rate, as well as increasing the accumulation of pyruvic acid and phenylalanine 111 negatively impacting plant growth and increasing phenol and flavonoid content, as reported by Jin et al. 112 , Barzegar et al. 113 and Yener et al. 114 , for Lilium davidii , Foeniculum vulgare and Prunus avium , respectively. 
 Although under the conditions evaluated in this work, B. pilosa and G. angustifolia plants planted in soil with BGAK showed less growth compared to their controls, the increase in the content of bioactive compounds indicates the benefit of the application of BGAK for the production of plant biomass of species of medicinal interest, opening the possibility of its use in plant production systems related to the pharmaceutical industry. 
 On the other hand, the biochar application to soils has other beneficial effects, such as carbon sequestration and reduction of gaseous emissions that help mitigate the carbon footprint for the agricultural sector 115 . Additionally, biochar improves the physical and chemical properties of the soil, which also leads to changes in microbial communities 116 . As reported by several authors, biochar stimulates the increase of beneficial populations involved in organic matter transformation, phosphorus solubilisation, nitrogen fixation and biological control of several phytopathogens 116 – 118 . On the other hand, biochar also serves as a support for the immobilisation of beneficial bacteria and fungi in such a way as to protect them from physical and chemical factors that may affect their biological activity over time 47 . For this reason, biochar is considered a formulation agent for the production of bioinoculants or bio fertilisers in solid form, which makes it a multipurpose bioproduct that contributes to the improvement of sustainable agricultural practices 47 , 119 , 120 . 
 In this article, we produced a Biochar (Technology Readiness Levels, TRL5) for agricultural application and a Biochar-TiO 2 composite (TRL4) from bamboo ( G. angustifolia ) sawdust for environmental application. As mentioned in the “ Introduction ” section, one ton of bamboo culms generates approximately 80% of lignocellulosic residues (800 kg), and 20% of them are sawdust (160 kg), that when transformed into biochar at 300 °C (temperature used in this study and with a yield of 33%) (Table  1 ), would be equivalent to 52.8 kg of biochar. This yield is low since approximately 107.8 kg resulted in a loss as part of the volatile fraction. This yield increase requires procurement parameters optimization, such as reducing the oxygen concentration in the pyrolysis chamber (injection of N 2 pulses or addition of a chemical catalyst), designing and scaling up the reactor with a higher level of automation to control temperature and residence time 31 , 121 . 
 Associated with the type of reactor to convert 160 kg of sawdust into biochar, a steel or iron reactor will serve to operate in batches; it should have an internal chamber with a capacity of 160 ± 5 kg to place the sawdust and another external chamber to put the solid material that will act as fuel (charcoal or mineral), which could also be replaced by gas, electricity, microwaves or solar panels 121 , 122 . The pyrolysis process at 300 °C would extend for 1–2 h; for the capture of gaseous emissions (CO, CO 2 and H 2 S), it is necessary to remove them using biological alternatives 123 , such as the use of microalgae and sulfur-oxidizing bacteria either free or immobilized on the biochar to act as a biofilter installed in the upper part of the pyrolysis reactor 124 , 125 . 
 Once the biofilter is saturated, the biochar-based filler material with algal and bacterial biomass may be mixed into the soil as a dual-purpose biological conditioner 119 , 126 . On the one hand, the biochar would remain in the soil for several years, providing slow carbon release, sequestering gas emissions, and improving agricultural practices aimed at reaching carbon neutral levels 19 , 47 , 127 . Microalgae and sulfur-oxidizing bacteria would provide labile organic matter and nutrients stimulating plant growth through the mobility and availability of nutrients such as phosphorus, nitrogen and sulfur for plants 126 , 128 , 129 . 
 The production of the BGAK/TiO 2 composite through impregnation and activation at 450 °C is easy to scale up, and it is even possible to reduce the calcination temperature to less than 100 °C 61 . As shown in Table  3 , the composite can be reused 5 or 6 times without thermal reactivation, and once the photocatalytic activity decreases by more than 80%, the composite must reactivate at 100 ± 10 °C for 1 h 61 , 92 . The final environmentally and sustainable disposal of the compost (BGAK/TiO 2 ) can be by incorporating it as a filler mixture to other types of processes, either biological (composting) or thermochemical (pyrolysis at a higher temperature), to obtain a recycled biochar with TiO 2 concentrated in the ashes. 
 Finally, converting the guadua sawdust (160 kg) into biochar and TiO 2 composite (52.8 kg) is a way to contribute positively to the bamboo production sector in Colombia, specifically in the reduction of direct costs associated with the propagation of plant material in nursery and field. Because at the nursery level, peat is typically used alone or mixed with soil to propagate bamboo seedlings; this substrate in a 50 kg presentation has an approximate cost of 60 ± 10 $ USA and must be imported from Canada ( https://www.elsemillero.co/ , 2024). On the other hand, using biochar produced from guadua sawdust could reduce costs by 50% since approximately 50 kg of this biochar would cost between 30 and 40 $ USA if purchased directly from bamboo processing companies and without intermediaries ( https://www.elsemillero.co/ , 2024). 
 Concerning the production costs of the composite (BGAK/TiO 2 ), the biochar would continue to be produced in the same way, and the TiO 2 could be replaced by another type of titanium that is cheaper than the one used in this study (Sigma); for example, TiO 2 used in the pharmaceutical, paint and self-cleaning glass industries 119 , 119 , 119 , 131 . 
## Conclusions 
 The research generates biochar from Guadua angustifolia Kunts sawdust with environmental and agricultural applications. Environmentally, the BGAK/TiO 2 had physical and chemical characteristics that allowed it to behave as a composite with photocatalytic activity. The TiO 2 was deposited on the surface of the biochar to form aggregates compacted by the calcination process, which favoured the obtention of a surface with a greater surface area for the photon adsorption that allowed the accelerated inactivation of high concentrations of E. coli after 60 min of photocatalysis. Additionally, the reusability of BGAK/TiO 2 was successful for ten continuous 1-hour cycles, but in cycles 1–5, the bacterial inactivation was higher than 70%. 
 The use of BGAK as a soil conditioner during the nursery cultivation of G. angustifolia plants increased the content of total phenols, and in B. pilosa plants increased the content of phenols and total flavonoids in the leaves, making the incorporation of biochar to the planting substrate an alternative agronomic management for the production of plant material and a stimulator of the production of phytocompounds with phytotherapeutic interest. This study demonstrated the feasibility of converting an undervalued agro-industrial waste into two new biomaterial types, one with photocatalytic properties for domestic and non-domestic wastewater treatment and the other as an organic soil conditioner. In this way, the two sustainable and environmentally friendly alternatives for use and conversion can contribute to the recovery of the environment (water and soil). 
 The results show new possibilities for developing biomaterials from the by-products of the Bamboo production chain. Environmentally, the BGAK/TiO 2 composite can be modified to include metallic elements to transform the BPA of TiO 2 and broaden the spectral response to operate in the visible spectrum. In this way, it would serve for wastewater treatment in rural areas of the country that do not have a continuous electricity supply. 
 In agriculture, BGAK alone could support the immobilization of beneficial microorganisms that promote plant growth, either in the nursery or in the field, especially for the propagation of G. angustifolia and B. pilosa (plants with medicinal potential due to the production of phenolic compounds such as flavonoids). On the other hand, in biochar co-inoculated with beneficial microorganisms, a biodegradable polymer could be incorporated to transform it into a multipurpose biohydroretention agent, which would help reduce the amount and frequency of water irrigation for the plants. 
## Methods 
## Collection and sample preparations 
 Guadua angustifolia Kunth sawdust immunised with boric acid (GAKS) purchased from a timber sawmill located at the Cundinamarca department, Colombia, served for research assays. The sawdust was sieved using a No 10 (2 mm) sieve and dried at 50 ± 3 °C for 12 h using a HACEB ® kiln 132 . 
## Biochar production from G. angustifolia Kunth sawdust 
 Biochar production occurred in a 20 L Labtech™ muffle by introducing 250 g rectangular aluminium trays containing 100 g of GAKS into the muffle chamber. The pyrolysis process was carried out for 1 h at 300 ± 3 °C to obtain the biochar from Guadua angustifolia Kunts (BGAK) 132 . The GAKS and BGAK were determined in triplicate for pH 133 , 134 , moisture (%) 133 , 134 , electrical conductivity (ms cm − 1 ) 47 , water retention (%) 133 , porosity (%) 135 , bulk density (g cm − 3 ) 136 , total organic carbon TOC (%) 47 , 137 , fixed carbon (FC) 47 , 138 , volatile carbon (VC) 47 , 138 , ash (Ask) 47 , 138 , 139 , and biochar yield 140 . Elemental analysis was obtained from energy-dispersive X-ray scattering (EDS) results 22 , 47 . 
 Carl Zeiss/Stemi 305 ® stereoscope at 40 x magnification and scanning electron microscopy and semi-quantitative energy dispersive X-ray element analysis (SEM/EDS) observations 47 , 119 served for material characterisation; so as infrared spectroscopy with Fourier analysis (FTIR) was employed using a Shimadzu Tracer-100 IR Tracer to characterised the molecular absorption bands associated with the functional groups of GAKS, BGAK and the BGAK/TiO 2 composite. The spectrum obtained got a resolution of 4 cm − 1 and a range of 500 to 4000 cm − 1 with 15 steps 47 . In addition, measurement of mass loss as a function of temperature was performed by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) 68 . For these analyses, 15 to 20 mg of each sample were placed into a Shimadzu TGA-50 Series instrument, operating under an inert nitrogen atmosphere with a flow rate of 20 mL/min and a heating rate of 10 °C per minute. The temperature was ramped from 20 to 600 °C, and data processing used the LabSolutions TA 60 WS software. 
## Environmental application of BGAK 
## BGAK/TiO 2 composites production 
 The composites were prepared by mixing 1 g of BGAK and 1 g of Sigma ® TiO 2 (CAS number 13463-67-7), with 100 mL of deionised water at pH 2.0 (HCl 0.1 N). The solutions were mixed for 120 min at 120 r.p.m., sonicated for 15 min and transferred to glass vessels for drying in a HACEB oven at 80 ± 2 °C for 12 h. Subsequently, the composite recovered was macerated with mortar and calcined at 450 ± 2 °C for one hour in a LabTech ® flask. The resultant composite designated as Guadua angustifolia Kunth/TiO 2 biochar at pH 2.0 (BGAK/TiO 2 ) was observed before and after heat treatment under stereoscopy at 40x magnification and by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) at 1000x and 5000x magnification 119 . 
## Photocatalytic inactivation of Escherichia coli 226 
 To evaluate the photocatalytic activity of the BGAK/TiO 2 composite, the strain E. coli 226 served as an indicator of faecal contamination in water. E. coli 226 reactivated in nutrient broth for 12 hours at 200 r.p.m. and 30 °C (OD 540 nm of 1.33 ± 0.45 equivalent to 1.0 × 10 12 ± 1.0 × 10 1 colony forming units per millilitre, CFU mL − 1 ). Subsequently, 2 mL of strain solution served as inoculum for 500 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 98 mL of nutrient broth and cultured under the same conditions until reaching an Optical density (OD) of 0.270 (1.0 × 10 9 ± 1.0 × 10 1 CFU mL − 1 ). In a 2000 mL Erlenmeyer, 50 mL of the culture was mixed with 450 mL of distilled water (¼ ratio) to reach an initial concentration of the bacteria of 2.2 × 10 7 ± 1.0 × 101 CFU mL − 1 at pH 7.0 ± 0.3. The initial concentration of E. coli 226 expressed as colony-forming units per millilitre of sample tested (CFU mL − 1 ) was determined by performing decimal dilutions and surface seeding on nutrient agar 141 . 
 Triplicate of the three evaluated concentrations of the BGAK/TiO 2 composites (0.1, 0.25 and 0.5% w/v) were at 300 mL quartz beakers placed containing 100 mL of distilled water inoculated with E. coli (initial concentration: 2.2 × 10 7 ± 1.0 × 10 1 CFU mL − 1 ). The quartz vessels were later placed inside the UV photolysis system, consisting of a galvanised metal box containing two Philips UV 253 nm lamps at 20 cm from the lamps. Each quartz container was fitted with a hose connected to fish tank aerators to introduce air (100 mL min − 1 ) and keep the composites in suspension 47 . Once the lid of the galvanised box closed, were turned on the UV lamps for 30 min. At the end of the UV exposure, after the container’s removal, samples served to determine the final or post-treatment CFU mL − 1 and converted to Log 10 CFU mL − 1 141 . UV 253 nm photolysis (C1) and dark adsorption (C2) to the composites served as control. A comparison of means between concentrations and the control allowed determining the significant differences ( p  < 0.05) using the SAS 9.0 ® programme; the Origin 6.0 ® programme served for results plotting. 
 Once the concentration of composites that favoured the inactivation of E. coli was selected, inactivation kinetics were performed, and the E. coli 226 strain was cultured and dosed in sterile distilled water under the same conditions presented in the previous paragraph. For this kinetics, the 0.5 ± 0.01% (w/v) of BGAK/TiO 2 /pH 2.0 used were placed in triplicate into quartz beakers containing 100 mL of distilled water at pH 5.4 ± 0.1 inoculated with E. coli (3.50 × 10 6 ± 1.0 × 10 1 CFU mL − 1 ). The set-up of the system was the same as the previous experiment and samples were taken in triplicate at baseline, 5, 10, 15, 30, 60, 120, 180, 240, and 300 min to determine the surviving CFU mL − 1 . The results were expressed as Log 10 CFU mL − 1 and converted into percentage inactivation 141 . As controls, the UV 253 nm photolysis process, adsorption in the dark to the composites, and absolute control (water re-inoculated with E. coli , without composites and UV light) were evaluated. With the results expressed in the percentage of inactivation, a comparison of means between treatment and controls after 300 min allowed determining significant differences between them ( p  < 0.05). The SAS 9.0 ® software was used and Origin 6.0 ® was used to plot the results. 
## Reusability test of BGAK/TiO 2 composite after 10-fold continuous bacterial inactivation cycles 
 To determine the number of cycles for composites reused without reactivation, a new batch of the composites produced had similar parameters to those shown in Table  1 . Then, was repeated the inactivation kinetics assembly, and the E. coli (with an initial concentration of 3.5 × 10 6  ± 1.3 × 10 1 CFU mL − 1 ; 6.54 ± 0.2 Log 10 CFU mL − 1 , pH 5.4 ± 0.1) suspension placed; followed by 0.5% (w/v) of composite to carried out the photocatalytic treatment for 1 hour (cycle 1), (all in triplicate). At the end of the photocatalysis process, the composite was recovered by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min and placed again in the quartz glasses to perform cycle 2. The procedure was eight times more repeated (cycles), and at the end of each cycle, the final CFU mL − 1 was determined to calculate the inactivation percentage based on ten consecutive cycles. 
## Agricultural application of biochar (BGAK) 
 Nursery trials using BGAK mixed with soil allow for evaluating the effect of BGAK biochar as a soil conditioner, promoting plant with medicinal potential growth and as a stimulant of total phenol and flavonoid content in leaves. 
## Plant material 
 Bidens pilosa seeds from plants sown at Finca Marengo Universidad Nacional de Colombia 4°40’50.7 “N 74°12’59.3 “W and one-month-old plants propagated from the lateral branches of Guadua angustifolia natural stands from Finca El Bambusal Fundación Yarumo Jardín Botánico Forestal de Cundinamarca 5°10’51.4 “N 74°11’44.0 “W were assayed. 
## Experimental design 
 Two randomised assays involve B. pilosa and G. angustifolia independently. Assay took 90 days after planting and treatments for B. pilosa and 120 days after transplanting and G. angustifolia treatments 46 . Two treatments took place; in the first, the plants were sown in soil with BGAK at 2% (w/w), and in the second one, plants were in only soil sown; eighteen experimental units per treatment resulted for each plant species. Sampling included 12 plants per treatment from each experiment for aerial part dry weight (SDW) determination and soil chemical analysis; the other six plants served for phenol and total flavonoid content determination 46 . 
## Soil chemical analysis 
 Treatment samples from the experiments forwarded to the AGRILAB laboratory in Bogotá, Colombia serves for measurement of pH, electrical conductivity (EC), cation exchange capacity (CEC), percentage of soil organic matter (SOM), percentage of total nitrogen (N), various elements composition like potassium (K), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), sodium (Na), iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), boron (B), sulfur (S) and phosphorus (P). 
## Metabolite extraction and sample preparation 
 Leaves and foliage leaves from 6 plants of B. pilosa and G. angustifolia , respectively, were taken and dried in an oven at 37 °C for 72 h. For the extract, 300 mg of dried and ground material, mixed with 10 mL of the solvent (chloroform, methanol and water; 5:2.5:2.5 v/v), was vortexed for 1 min, sonicated for 10 min at 20 °C and centrifuged at 5000 r.p.m., and 20 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was filtered through 0.22 μm pore PTFE syringe filters (Thermo Scientific, Rockwood, TN) and stored at -80 °C until analysis 51 . 
## Determination of total phenol content 
 Total phenol content was determined by the Folin-Ciocalteu method 142 , using 0.2 mL of the extract mixed with 0.6 mL of distilled water. Then, 0.2 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu’s reagent, 1 mL 8% (w/v) sodium carbonate and 1 mL of distilled water were added and stirred for 30 min at 19 °C in the dark. ## Statistical analyses and data handling 
 To evaluate the effect of BGAK application on the plants, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied for the SDW data of B. pilosa , the T-Test for the SDW data of G. angustifolia and to evaluate its effect on phenol and total flavonoid content, once it was verified that the data were normal and their variances homogeneous through Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test (Tables S1 and S2 ), ANOVA (Table S3 ) and Kruskal-Wallis (Table S4 ) tests were applied. When there were significant differences between treatments, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied (Table S5 ).
