 Lactic acid bacteria (LAB), widely used as probiotics in the food industry, are typically freeze-dried to extend shelf life, although this process often compromises cell viability. While individual stress treatments have been shown to enhance bacterial survival, the potential of combined stresses remains underexplored. In this study, we investigated the effect of acid–cold cross-stress adaptation on the freeze-drying tolerance of LAB. The results demonstrated that cross-stress pretreatment significantly improved survival rates, fermentation performance, and storage stability. Specifically, Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus exhibited a 1.65-fold increase in survival and 20% higher viability after 21 days of storage. Mechanistic analysis revealed that this strategy preserved cell membrane integrity, elevated lactate dehydrogenase and ATPase activity, and increased the unsaturated-to-saturated fatty acid ratio. These findings highlight the synergistic benefits of multi-stress conditioning and provide a promising strategy for improving the industrial preservation of probiotic cultures.

## Introduction 
 LAB enhance both the flavor and quality of food while also offering various health benefits. They can colonize the gastrointestinal tract, helping to regulate intestinal inflammation (Guo et al., 2025 ), lowering cholesterol levels (Korcz et al., 2018 ), and exhibiting anticancer properties (Chen et al., 2024 ). Due to these advantages, they have become the most commonly used probiotics in the food industry, particularly in dairy and meat products such as yogurt, cheese, and fermented sausages. Their use not only supports fermentation and shelf-life extension but also contributes to the functional properties of livestock-derived foods. Maintaining their survival and metabolic activity is essential for maximizing their probiotic effects and ensuring consistent performance during food processing (Bisutti et al., 2015 ). However, their viability is influenced by physiological constraints and external factors such as processing techniques, storage conditions, and culture environments (Jiang et al., 2022 ). To enhance stability and prolong shelf life, LAB are often converted into powdered form. 
 Freeze-drying is a highly effective method for preserving the viability of LAB. This process involves freezing bacterial suspensions at subzero temperatures, followed by the removal of water through ice crystal sublimation under vacuum conditions, ultimately producing a stable powdered form (Yang et al., 2019 ). The resulting powder has high stability and can be conveniently applied to animal-based foods, including as direct-fed microbials in livestock products (Moayyedi et al., 2018 ). However, exposure to freezing and dehydration during the process imposes significant stress on the bacteria, potentially causing structural and physiological damage that compromises their viability and industrial utility (Jofre et al., 2015 ). 
 To improve survival during freeze-drying, three primary strategies have been explored: medium optimization, addition of protective agents, and stress pretreatments (Broeckx et al., 2017 ). Among these, stress pretreatments are considered highly practical due to their low cost and scalability. By exposing cells to sublethal acid, cold, heat, or osmotic conditions, bacteria can activate adaptive responses—such as the upregulation of stress proteins, modifications in membrane fatty acid composition, and accumulation of compatible solutes—that enhance their resilience (Gao et al., 2022 ; Moreira et al., 2021 ).In particular, cold and acid stresses are known to induce cold shock proteins (CspA, CspB), acid resistance systems (e.g., F0F1-ATPase), and membrane remodeling, all of which contribute to improved stability during subsequent processing (Liu et al., 2020 ). 
 Various stress treatments have been explored in recent studies to improve the survival of freeze-dried strains. For instance, Zhen et al. (Zhen et al., 2020 ) reported that heat-stressed Lactobacillus acidophilus heat-stressed, while Choi et al. (Choi et al., 2019 ) demonstrated improved storage stability in Lactobacillus brevis after cold stress. Moreover, the concept of cross-protection suggests that pre-exposure to one stress may enhance tolerance to another—for example, acid stress increasing cold resistance via shared regulatory pathways (Papadimitriou et al., 2016 ). However, the synergistic effects of acid–cold cross-stress, especially on the physiological functions and freeze-drying tolerance of LAB, remain underexplored. 
 In this study, three industrially relevant LAB strains were selected to investigate the effects of acid-cold cross-stress on their survival during freeze-drying. Bacterial tolerance to this preservation process was evaluated through analyses of membrane integrity, lipid composition, and enzymatic activity. Additionally, the functionality of the freeze-dried LAB was assessed in terms of their storage stability and fermentation performance in vitro. The findings provide a scientific basis for enhancing the performance of LAB in livestock-based food systems and optimizing probiotic formulation strategies for broader industrial application. 
## Materials and methods 
## Bacterial strain 
 The strains Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus ( L. rhamnosus ), Lactiplantibacillus plantarum ( L. plantarum ) and Limosilactobacillus fermentum ( L. fermentum ) employed in this study were generously provided by Hangzhou Wahaha Group Co., Ltd. Each strain was cultured by inoculating 200 µL of the bacterial suspension into 10 mL of de Man–Rogosa–Sharpe (MRS) medium, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. To ensure activation, the strains were subcultured for three successive generations. The resulting cultures were maintained at 4 °C prior to subsequent analyses. 
## Stress treatment and freeze-drying 
 An aliquot of 200 µL of activated culture was transferred into 10 mL of MRS medium and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Cells were subsequently harvested by centrifugation (7500 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), then washed twice with sterile saline (0.85% NaCl, w/v). For each wash, the pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of saline, centrifuged under the same conditions, and the supernatant discarded (Papadimitriou et al., 2016 ). After the second wash, the cells were harvested again and resuspended in 10 mL of fresh sterile saline for stress treatment. For cross-stress treatment (Fig.  1 ), the resuspended cells were first collected by centrifugation and then resuspended in saline adjusted to pH 4.0, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 90 min. After acid exposure, the suspension was washed, reconstituted in fresh saline, and subjected to cold treatment at 4 °C for 180 min. These parameters were selected based on preliminary optimization experiments (Supplementary Fig. S1 ), in which pH values (3.0–5.0) and temperatures (0–16 °C) were individually tested. pH 4.0 (90 min) and 4 °C (180 min) consistently resulted in the highest survival rates across L. rhamnosus , L. plantarum , and L. fermentum , and were therefore adopted as the conditions for subsequent cross-stress treatment. Upon completion of this two-step adaptation, cells were washed again and suspended in saline for subsequent processing. The final preparation was frozen at − 80 °C for 3 h, followed by freeze-drying using a vacuum lyophilizer (Labconco Corporation, Kansas, USA) for 24 h under 20 Pa, with the condenser set at − 50 °C.

## Assessment of cell morphology 
 Morphological alterations of LAB before and after freeze-drying were examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, H-7650; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). For sample preparation, 1 g of lyophilized cells was resuspended in 10 mL of sterile saline and maintained at 37 °C for 30 min. A small volume of the suspension was then transferred onto a clean porcelain surface. Subsequently, 0.03 mL of polyvinyl formal dissolved in chloroform was gently layered over the droplet and left to adsorb for 5 min. After natural drying at ambient temperature, the specimen was treated with 1 mL of phosphotungstic acid for 2 min, dried again, and observed under TEM. 
## Assessment of cell membrane properties 
## Membrane fluidity 
 One gram of lyophilized bacterial powder was rehydrated in 10 mL of 2 µM 6-diphenyl-1,3,5-hexatriene (DPH) solution, as described by Yang et al. (DPH) (Yang et al., 2023 ). The suspension was incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 1 h. After labeling, samples were transferred to black 96-well plates for fluorescence analysis. Measurements were performed using a Cary Eclipse spectrophotometer (Varian Inc., USA) with excitation and emission wavelengths of 360 nm and 427 nm, respectively. 
## Membrane permeability 
 β-Galactosidase activity, indicative of membrane permeability, was determined using o-nitrophenyl β-D-galactopyranoside (ONPG) as the indicator substrate (Haddaji et al., 2017 ). One gram of lyophilized bacterial cells was rehydrated in 10 mL of sterile saline and emulsified with an equal volume of n-hexadecane. A 1.8 mL aliquot of the emulsion was then mixed with 0.2 mL of 30 mM ONPG solution and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, absorbance was measured at 420 nm using a FlexStation 3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, USA). 
## Assessment of key enzyme activities 
 Two grams of lyophilized bacteria were suspended in 20 mL of sterile saline. From this, a 15 mL portion was subjected to ultrasonic disruption in an ice-water bath, using 5 s on/7 s off cycles for a total of 10 min. The lysate was then centrifuged at 7500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was used for subsequent enzymatic assays. 
 Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and Na/K-ATPase activities were determined using commercial kits obtained from Solarbio (Beijing, China) and Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China), respectively. Enzyme activity levels were normalized to total protein content and reported as units per milligram of protein. 
## Analysis of membrane fatty acids 
 One gram of lyophilized bacterial cells was rehydrated in 10 mL of sterile saline, washed twice, and collected by centrifugation (7500 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). The resulting pellet was reserved for lipid extraction. Fatty acids were isolated and methylated following the method described by Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2022 ), and analyzed using gas chromatography (GC; Agilent Technologies, California, USA). Chromatographic separation was carried out on a 30 m polyethylene glycol capillary column (0.5 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness), with helium as the carrier gas. The total and direct flow rates were maintained at 18.8 and 35 mL/min, respectively, under a pressure of 20 kPa. Injection and detection temperatures were set at 260 °C and 280 °C. The column temperature was set to initiate at 100 °C (held for 1 min), ramped at 6.5 °C/min to a final temperature of 250 °C, and held there for 25 min. Fatty acid content was calculated using the normalization approach, setting the total peak area as 100%. Individual components were reported as percentage values based on their relative peak areas. 
## Evaluation of storage stability 
 Lyophilized bacteria were aliquoted into Eppendorf tubes, sealed with parafilm, and maintained at 25 °C for 42 days. Cell viability was assessed every 7 days according to the procedure described under Assessment of survival rate. 
## Assessment of fermentative capacity 
 Two grams of lyophilized bacterial powder were introduced into 200 mL of MRS broth containing 1.2 g NaCl, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 72 h. In addition to the freeze-dried samples, fresh, non-lyophilized bacterial cultures were included as a reference to evaluate whether stress-adapted strains retained functionality comparable to that of the original live bacteria. Sampling was performed every 12 h to monitor changes in biomass, residual sugar, pH, and titratable acidity. Cell concentration was monitored spectrophotometrically at 600 nm with a FlexStation 3 reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, USA). Sugar concentration was analyzed via the sulfuric acid method (Liu et al., 2014 ). pH was recorded using a benchtop meter (Mettler-Toledo, Columbus, USA), while acid content was determined by titration based on the method described by Yang et al. (Yang et al., 2023 ). 
## Statistical analysis 
 All assays were conducted in triplicate using independent biological samples. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 (San Diego, CA, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was performed to assess significant differences among groups. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and statistical significance was defined as p  < 0.05 unless otherwise stated. 
## Results and discussion 
## Impact of cross-stress treatment on survival rate during freeze-drying 
 For probiotics to exert their functional properties, they must be present in adequate numbers. However, processing techniques such as freeze-drying often cause cellular damage, significantly reducing bacterial viability (Ge et al., 2024 ). Therefore, survival rate serves as a critical indicator for evaluating the effectiveness of the freeze-drying process. 
 As shown in Fig.  2 , the survival rates of three Lactobacillus strains varied under different environmental treatments. For L. rhamnosus (Fig.  2 A), the control group exhibited a survival rate of 41.49 ± 1.74%. Notably, cross-stress treatment significantly increased survival to 68.57 ± 3.32%, representing a 1.65-fold improvement over the control. Moreover, cross-stress treatment consistently outperformed individual stress treatments ( p  < 0.05). Similar trends were observed in L. plantarum and L. fermentum, where cross-stress exposure markedly improved cell viability compared to other conditions ( p  < 0.05). These results indicate that cross-stress conditioning confers superior protection to LAB during freeze-drying, surpassing the effects of single-stress approaches.

 L. rhamnosus ( A ), L. plantarum ( B ), and L. fermentum ( C ). Data are presented as mean ± SD ( n  = 3). Statistical differences were evaluated by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Bars with no letters in common are significantly different ( p  < 0.05) 
 LAB respond to environmental changes by activating metabolic and cellular defense mechanisms that enhance tolerance to external stressors (Streit et al., 2008 ). Prior studies have demonstrated that either acid or cold pretreatment can improve survival under adverse conditions (Bodzen et al., 2021 ; Choi et al., 2019 ). The current findings further suggest that sequential exposure to multiple stressors may activate more complex and robust protective pathways, thereby strengthening bacterial resilience and significantly enhancing post-drying viability (Zhuang et al., 2024 ). 
 This enhanced protection may involve not only changes in membrane composition and enzyme function, but also the upregulation of stress-responsive genes and synthesis of specific proteins, such as cold shock proteins (Csps) and acid shock proteins (Asps), which help preserve macromolecular integrity under stress (De Angelis & Gobbetti, 2004 ; Graumann & Marahiel, 1998 ). In addition, the accumulation of compatible solutes like trehalose and glycine betaine may stabilize cellular proteins and membranes during dehydration (Kandror et al., 2002 ; Welsh, 2000 ). Although these molecular responses were not directly investigated in this study, they are well documented in the literature and may underlie the improved viability observed following cross-stress treatment. 
## Morphological effects of cross-stress treatment on LAB 
 During freeze-drying, the formation of ice crystals and subsequent dehydration can severely disrupt cellular structures and impair bacterial function. To assess these morphological changes, TEM was employed in this study. 
 As shown in Fig.  3 , all three Lactobacillus strains maintained an intact rod-shaped morphology prior to freeze-drying. However, significant structural alterations were observed post-treatment, particularly in the control group. These included cell rupture and leakage of intracellular contents (red arrows), as well as depressions and gaps in the cell envelope (blue arrows), indicating extensive membrane damage. In contrast, cells pretreated with cross-stress retained their structural integrity, showing no signs of rupture or deformation. 
 Fig. 3 
 In addition to overall preservation, a slight elongation of L. rhamnosus cells was observed after freeze-drying, especially in the cross-stressed group. This morphological change was not evident in L. plantarum or L. fermentum . The elongation of rod-shaped bacteria may reflect adaptive physiological responses, such as altered septum formation, modified membrane fluidity, or changes in cell division dynamics under stress. These responses are potentially associated with the regulation of cell shape–related genes (e.g., ftsZ, mreB) and the expression of stress proteins involved in peptidoglycan remodeling and cytoskeletal maintenance (Cabeen & Jacobs-Wagner, 2005 ; Justice et al., 2006 ). 
 Overall, these findings suggest that cross-stress treatment contributes to the structural preservation of LAB during freeze-drying. This protective effect is consistent with the observations of Zhen et al. ( 2020 ), who reported that heat stress pretreatment preserved the structural integrity of Lactobacillus acidophilus . The underlying mechanisms may involve enhanced membrane resilience mediated by changes in fatty acid composition and the induction of membrane-associated stress proteins such as GroEL and DnaK, which help stabilize protein–lipid interactions during dehydration (De Angelis & Gobbetti, 2004 ; Kandror et al., 2002 ). 
 Taken together, the observed morphological stability likely reflects an integrated cellular response encompassing membrane protection, cell wall remodeling, and stress-induced protein expression. 
## Effects of cross-stress on membrane functional properties 
 Membrane fluidity is a critical factor influencing numerous cellular processes, including metabolism, signal transduction, and structural stability. Freeze-drying can disrupt this fluidity, potentially compromising the physiological functions of LAB (Bisutti et al., 2015 ). Therefore, changes in membrane fluidity were assessed following different stress treatments. 
 As illustrated in Fig.  4 A, significant differences were observed among the treatment groups, with cross-stress pretreatment showing the most pronounced effect ( p  < 0.05). The polarization parameter, which is inversely related to membrane fluidity, was significantly reduced in L. rhamnosus , L. plantarum , and L. fermentum (0.31 ± 0.03, 0.35 ± 0.03, and 0.26 ± 0.02, respectively) compared to the untreated control (0.52 ± 0.03, 0.49 ± 0.03, and 0.46 ± 0.02, respectively). These results indicate that cross-stress pretreatment markedly enhances membrane fluidity across all strains.

 A ), cell membrane permeability ( B ), LDH activity ( C ) and Na/K ATPase activity ( D ). Data are presented as mean ± SD ( n  = 3). Statistical differences were evaluated by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Bars with no letters in common are significantly different ( p  < 0.05) 
 This effect may be explained by freeze-drying-induced phase transitions, where water loss converts phospholipid membranes from a liquid crystalline to a gel state. This transition tightens fatty acyl chain packing, increases hydrophobic interactions, and leads to reduced membrane fluidity, ultimately impairing bacterial viability (Bisutti et al., 2015 ). However, cells exposed to sublethal stress can undergo metabolic adaptation to counteract membrane rigidity (Liu et al., 2014 ). 
 Specifically, cross-stress may stimulate the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, which introduce kinks into lipid chains, preventing tight packing and maintaining membrane fluidity under cold or acidic conditions (Annous et al., 1997 ; Cui et al., 2022 ). Additionally, membrane-associated chaperones such as GroES-GroEL and DnaK-DnaJ may be upregulated, helping stabilize integral membrane proteins and preserve membrane dynamics during dehydration (De Angelis & Gobbetti, 2004 ; Kandror et al., 2002 ). 
 In parallel, membrane permeability was evaluated to further assess functional integrity (Fig.  4 B). The control group exhibited the highest permeability values in all strains, indicating severe membrane damage—1.04 ± 0.06, 1.37 ± 0.05, and 0.89 ± 0.05 for L. rhamnosus , L. plantarum , and L. fermentum , respectively. This increase is attributed to the collapse of hydration shells around phospholipid head groups during freeze-drying (Louesdon et al., 2014 ). The resulting bilayer destabilization facilitates acyl chain rearrangement and van der Waals interactions, promoting rigidity and increased leakage. Consequently, elevated permeability can lead to cytoplasmic enzyme loss and reduced metabolic capacity (Morgan et al., 2006 ). 
 Beyond physical disruption, permeability changes may also reflect impaired regulation of porins and transport proteins, which are sensitive to cold and osmotic stress and controlled by transcriptional regulators such as σ^B and CtsR (Papadimitriou et al., 2016 ; Van De Guchte et al., 2002 ). 
 Cross-stress pretreatment significantly reduced membrane permeability, with values dropping to 0.62 ± 0.03, 0.95 ± 0.04, and 0.51 ± 0.03 for L. rhamnosus , L. plantarum , and L. fermentum , respectively. These findings demonstrate that cross-stress conditioning not only improves membrane fluidity but also helps preserve selective permeability. 
 Together, these protective effects likely stem from a coordinated cellular response, involving lipid remodeling, stress protein expression, and transcriptional regulation of membrane-associated functions. Such responses enable LAB to better withstand the physical and biochemical challenges imposed by freeze-drying. 
## Impact of cross-stress treatment on enzymes involved in cellular metabolism 
 LDH plays a critical role in converting lactic acid to pyruvate, a key step in energy metabolism for LAB. However, its enzymatic activity is often diminished during freeze-drying due to protein denaturation (Li et al., 2011 ), making LDH a useful marker for assessing stress-induced metabolic damage. 
 As shown in Fig.  4 C, LDH activity varied significantly across treatments. All stress-treated groups displayed higher activity than the control, with the cold-stress and cross-stress groups showing the most pronounced effects ( p  < 0.05). Among them, the cross-stressed group exhibited the highest LDH activity, suggesting superior protection against freeze-drying-induced enzyme inactivation. Similar findings were reported by Zhen et al. (Zhen et al., 2020 ), who showed that heat shock pretreatment preserved LDH activity in Lactobacillus acidophilus . Given that LDH is essential for lactic acid production (Singhvi et al., 2017 ), these results indicate that cross-stress pretreatment enhances the metabolic functionality and viability of LAB. 
 This protection may be attributed to the upregulation of molecular chaperones such as GroEL and DnaK, which assist in protein folding and prevent aggregation under stress (De Angelis & Gobbetti, 2004 ). Additionally, acid or cold exposure may activate global regulatory pathways, including the alternative sigma factor σ^B and heat-shock regulons, which coordinate the transcription of stress-response genes to maintain enzymatic function (Papadimitriou et al., 2016 ; Wemekamp-Kamphuis et al., 2004 ). 
 A similar protective trend was observed in Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity (Fig.  4 D), a key enzyme responsible for maintaining ion gradients and cellular homeostasis under osmotic stress. Freeze-drying typically reduces ATPase activity due to dehydration-induced membrane disruption (Papadimitriou et al., 2016 ). However, cross-stress treatment significantly preserved ATPase activity in all three strains, with values of 3.34 ± 0.27, 3.34 ± 0.26, and 2.90 ± 0.24 U/mg for L. rhamnosus , L. plantarum , and L. fermentum , respectively ( p  < 0.05). 
 This enhancement may result from stress-induced remodeling of membrane lipid composition and stabilization of membrane-bound enzyme complexes. Moreover, transcriptional regulation of ATPase subunits by stress-responsive elements may further support enzyme integrity and function under desiccation stress (Ghorbal et al., 2013 ; Roux et al., 2022 ). 
## Influence of cross-stress treatment on membrane lipids 
 The freeze-drying process, involving both low temperatures and dehydration, can markedly alter the membrane lipid composition of LAB. These changes may induce lipid phase transitions, oxidation, or crystallization, thereby disrupting membrane integrity and impairing physiological function (Velly et al., 2015 ). To elucidate these effects, this study analyzed the membrane fatty acid profiles of LAB strains subjected to different stress conditions. 
 As shown in Fig.  5 A–C, stress-treated groups exhibited a notable shift in membrane composition compared to the control. Saturated fatty acid (SFA) levels were significantly lower, while unsaturated fatty acid (USFA) concentrations were elevated in all strains subjected to stress, particularly under cross-stress conditions. The USFA/SFA ratios were highest in the cross-stressed group—0.60 ± 0.02 for L. rhamnosus and L. plantarum , and 0.54 ± 0.04 for L. fermentum —compared to the lowest ratios in the control group. These shifts are consistent with previous findings suggesting that environmental stress prompts LAB to adjust their lipid composition to enhance membrane flexibility and resilience (Haddaji et al., 2017 ).

 Such compositional remodeling is mediated by stress-responsive enzymes, including fatty acid desaturases and cyclopropane fatty acid synthase (CFAS), which introduce unsaturation or cyclopropane rings into fatty acid chains (Grogan & Cronan, 1997 ; Zheng et al., 1998 ). The expression of these enzymes is regulated at the transcriptional level by alternative sigma factors (e.g., σ^B, σ^E) and global regulators such as CtsR and Spx, enabling rapid membrane adaptation to acid, thermal, or osmotic stress (Bucur et al., 2018 ; Yoon et al., 2015 ). 
 In addition to the USFA/SFA ratio, the content of cyclopropane fatty acid C19:0 cyc also increased significantly following cross-stress treatment (Table  1 ; p  < 0.05). This molecule, derived from oleic acid methylation, has been associated with improved stress resistance by enhancing membrane rigidity and reducing permeability under extreme conditions (Chen & Ganzle, 2016 ).

 From a biophysical perspective, increased USFAs enhance membrane fluidity by introducing cis-double bonds, which create kinks that prevent tight packing of lipid tails. This flexibility facilitates membrane reorganization during dehydration–rehydration cycles and reduces the likelihood of phase transition–induced damage (Haddaji et al., 2017 ). In parallel, decreased SFAs improve membrane elasticity, and elevated C19:0 cyc levels further stabilize the membrane by forming rigid ring structures that reduce permeability and protect against osmotic, oxidative, and acid stress. 
 Therefore, the upregulation of CFAS and the associated enrichment in USFAs and C19:0 cyc likely reflect a coordinated lipid remodeling strategy that enhances membrane integrity and stress tolerance. These adaptations contribute to the improved survival and functional stability of LAB during freeze-drying. 
## Impact of cross-stress treatment on storage stability 
 During storage, LAB are exposed to various environmental stressors that can compromise their viability and functional integrity (Jiang et al., 2022 ). To assess this impact, we evaluated the long-term survival of freeze-dried LAB strains under ambient conditions. 
 As shown in Fig.  6 , all strains exhibited a gradual decline in viability when stored at 25 °C. However, survival rates differed significantly among treatment groups. By day 42, the control group showed the lowest survival—6.00 ± 2.94% for L. rhamnosus , 7.33 ± 2.05% for L. plantarum , and 0.00 ± 0.00% for L. fermentum . In contrast, cross-stress pretreatment markedly improved storage stability, with corresponding values reaching 38.33 ± 4.92%, 30.00 ± 4.08%, and 26.33 ± 2.94% ( p  < 0.05). These results indicate that cross-stress conditioning enhances the long-term preservation of freeze-dried LAB.

 This improvement may be partially attributed to membrane lipid remodeling. As shown in Table  1 , cross-stress treatment increased the proportion of USFA, which are known to maintain membrane fluidity and reduce oxidative vulnerability. Prior studies have demonstrated that higher USFA content helps preserve membrane structure and viability during extended storage (Fonseca et al., 2001 ; Hansen et al., 2015 ). 
 In addition to lipid-based protection, cross-stress pretreatment may also activate stress-responsive protein systems. Molecular chaperones such as small heat shock proteins (sHSPs) can prevent protein denaturation and aggregation under desiccation stress, preserving enzymatic activity over time (De Angelis & Gobbetti, 2004 ; Papadimitriou et al., 2016 ). Furthermore, stress adaptation may lead to the accumulation of compatible solutes such as trehalose and glycine betaine, which function as chemical chaperones that stabilize cellular macromolecules and enhance resistance to dehydration and oxidation (Kandror et al., 2002 ; Welsh, 2000 ). 
## Impact of cross-stress treatment on fermentation performance 
 LAB play a central role in the food industry by promoting shelf life, improving sensory qualities, and enhancing the nutritional value of fermented products (Peng et al., 2020 ). However, freeze-drying can impair their metabolic activity and reduce fermentation capacity. To evaluate the effect of cross-stress treatment on this functionality, we examined several fermentation-related indicators in freeze-dried strains. 
 Bacterial biomass was first assessed (Fig.  7 ). All groups showed similar growth patterns, but during the stationary phase, biomass levels in the cross-stressed group were significantly higher than those in the control ( p  < 0.05). Importantly, only the cross-stressed group reached biomass levels statistically comparable to those of original live bacteria ( p  > 0.05), indicating successful preservation of growth potential.

 L. rhamnosus ( A ), L. plantarum ( B ) and L. fermentum ( C ). Data are presented as mean ± SD ( n  = 3). Statistical differences were evaluated by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Different letters indicate significant differences ( p  < 0.05) 
 Metabolic activity was evaluated by measuring sugar consumption. As shown in Fig.  7 , all groups utilized sugars over time, but the cross-stressed group exhibited a significantly faster sugar depletion rate than the control ( p  < 0.05). Its sugar utilization profile closely matched that of the original live bacteria group, suggesting functional retention of core metabolic pathways. 
 Acid production was then analyzed via pH and total acidity measurements. In all groups, pH decreased rapidly and acidity increased sharply during the exponential phase, stabilizing after 36 h (Fig.  7 ). In the cross-stressed group, both pH and acidity levels were significantly different from the control but statistically indistinguishable from the fresh group ( p  > 0.05), indicating preserved acidification performance. These functional improvements may be attributed to stress-induced metabolic adaptations. Cross-stress treatment likely upregulated key glycolytic genes such as pfk and pyk, which facilitate sugar catabolism and lactic acid synthesis (Mozzi et al., 2001 ). Stress exposure may also enhance the expression of redox-regulating enzymes (e.g., NADH oxidases) and general stress proteins, promoting redox balance and sustaining metabolic flux during fermentation (Papadimitriou et al., 2016 ). Furthermore, as shown in Figs.  2 and 4 , enhanced viability and elevated enzyme activities in the cross-stressed group likely contributed to improved fermentation efficiency. 
 These findings are consistent with previous work by Yang et al. (Yang et al., 2023 ), who reported that pre-stress strategies such as heat shock improved the fermentative performance of freeze-dried LAB. 
## Conclusion 
 The freeze-drying process often compromises the activity and functionality of LAB, limiting their industrial application. This study demonstrates that cross-stress treatment effectively enhances bacterial preservation by maintaining membrane integrity and modulating lipid composition (Fig.  8 ). These benefits likely arise from integrated adaptive responses, including alterations in fatty acid profiles, activation of stress proteins, and regulation of genes involved in membrane and metabolic processes. Furthermore, cross-stress treatment improves long-term stability and fermentation performance, allowing freeze-dried LAB to retain viability and functionality comparable to fresh cultures. Given the widespread use of LAB in fermented dairy and meat products, this strategy shows strong potential to improve the performance of starter cultures and probiotics in livestock-based food systems. Overall, the proposed approach offers a promising solution for enhancing the quality and consistency of LAB applications in the food industry.
