## Abstract 
 Lactiplantibacillus plantarum microcapsules in three sequential stages. First, Kissinger kinetic analysis of eleven biopolymer candidates ( E a : 95.4–198.4 kJ/mol) combined with pH-dependent solubility profiling provided an empirical screening criterion for the preselection of coating materials; the activation energies are apparent values associated with a composite endothermic event and the glass transition temperature was not measured in this work. Second, a factorial design identified whey protein concentrate (WPC)–gum arabic as the most suitable wall system, yielding a post-drying survival of 97.76 ± 0.75% and showing a significant protein–polysaccharide interaction ( p < 0.0001). Third, a D-optimal mixture design optimized a four-component thermoprotectant blend composed of trehalose (29.7%), glycerol (25.7%), fructooligosaccharides (19.8%), and inulin (24.8%). The fitted model explained 91.9% of the response variability (R 2 = 0.919), and the optimized blend limited viability loss during thermal exposure at 92 °C for 3–8 min, consistent with complementary protective effects involving vitrification and membrane stabilization. The optimized system yielded 2.4 × 10 11 CFU/g after spray drying, a water activity of 0.23, and a Carr Index of 18.5%. Under simulated gastrointestinal digestion (INFOGEST 2.0), thermoprotectant-loaded microcapsules retained 1.5 × 10 7 CFU/mL of digesta after the gastric phase and delivered 1.2 × 10 8 CFU/mL of digesta to the intestinal phase, four to five orders of magnitude above unprotected microcapsules (1.0 × 10 3 CFU/mL of digesta). Overall, these results support a transferable formulation strategy that combines thermophysical screening with experimental design to develop probiotic ingredients with improved thermal stability for food applications.

## 1. Introduction 
 1 , 2 ]. Despite this evidence, the large-scale commercialization of probiotic products is constrained by a critical technological limitation that markedly compromises their clinical effectiveness. Viability losses occurring during industrial processing, storage in the absence of a continuous cold chain, and gastrointestinal transit may collectively reach 3–5 logarithmic cycles, resulting in a decline from initial concentrations of approximately 10 9 CFU/g to subtherapeutic levels (below 10 6 CFU/g). This limitation impacts both manufacturers and consumers; from an industrial perspective, it leads to reduced product efficacy, shorter shelf life, and increased costs associated with over-dosification strategies, while from the consumer standpoint it translates into products that fail to deliver meaningful health benefits [ 3 ]. 
 4 ]. Recent studies using Lactiplantibacillus plantarum have shown that when outlet temperatures exceed 70 °C, cell survival decreases markedly, with post-drying viabilities ranging from 75.70% to 99.73%, depending primarily on the coating material employed [ 5 , 6 ]. The choice of the encapsulating agent critically determines the extent of thermal protection afforded to probiotic cells during both the constant-rate and falling-rate drying periods [ 7 ]. 
 Lactiplantibacillus plantarum show that free cells exhibit substantial inactivation at temperatures above 50 °C, whereas alginate–soy protein microcapsules can retain 10 6 –10 8 CFU/mL after treatment at 72 °C for 90 s [ 8 ]. However, in complex acidic matrices such as fruit juices (pH 3.5 to 4.5), rapid dissolution of hydrophilic polysaccharides may cause premature capsule solubilization and early probiotic release, compromising targeted intestinal delivery [ 9 ]. 
 T g ) is a key determinant of amorphous matrix stability. Materials with T g values sufficiently above the storage or processing temperature provide superior protection by maintaining the encapsulant in a glassy, low-mobility state, whereas systems operating below their T g are prone to structural collapse, crystallization, and accelerated oxygen diffusion [ 10 , 11 ]. For instance, powders containing 10% gum arabic exhibit higher T g than maltodextrin, trehalose, or their binary blends, which explains the superior survival of L. plantarum during isothermal heating at 90 °C [ 12 ]. Trehalose, with an anhydrous T g of approximately 115 °C and molecular relaxation activation energies between 175 and 400 kJ/mol, is an effective thermoprotectant at 10 to 50% ( w / v ) in industrial processing [ 13 , 14 ]. Protein–polysaccharide matrices further enhance protection by combining film-forming proteins (whey protein isolate, soy protein, calcium caseinate, and food-grade collagen) with polysaccharides that contribute interfacial stability, enzymatic resistance, and structural flexibility, such as octenyl succinic anhydride (OSA)-modified cassava starch, gum arabic, and sodium alginate [ 15 , 16 ]. During spray drying, proteins undergo rapid surface denaturation at the air–droplet interface, forming a continuous viscoelastic film that limits heat and mass transfer to the particle interior, while the polysaccharide fraction raises the effective T g of the consolidated matrix and contributes to hydrogen-bond-mediated water immobilization [ 7 ]. In parallel, low-molecular-weight thermoprotectants including trehalose, fructooligosaccharides, inulin, and glycerol can increase the effective T g , replace structural water at phospholipid headgroup interfaces, and provide osmotic stabilization of the intracellular milieu, raising survival from negligible levels without inulin to 60 to 70% at 40% inulin and preserving up to 98% viability after two months of storage [ 4 , 17 ]. 
 18 ]. In particular, no systematic framework has been reported that combines thermal kinetic analysis of candidate materials with statistically designed screening of wall system combinations and thermoprotectant mixtures within a single, integrated experimental strategy. This work addresses this gap through a systematic three-stage experimental strategy for Lactiplantibacillus plantarum microencapsulation. First, Kissinger-based kinetic analysis and pH-dependent solubility profiling of eleven biopolymer candidates were used to establish quantitative preselection criteria. Second, a 3 × 2 factorial design evaluated binary protein–polysaccharide combinations to identify the wall system maximizing both post-drying survival and viable count. Third, a D-optimal mixture design optimized a quaternary thermoprotectant formulation to enhance probiotic survival under thermal conditions representative of food pasteurization, using cell survival after treatment at 92 ± 2 °C for 2 min as the design response; the formulations generated by numerical optimization were subsequently validated at longer exposure times (92 °C for 3, 5, and 8 min). The resulting optimized system was characterized for physicochemical properties and evaluated for gastrointestinal resistance using the standardized INFOGEST 2.0 protocol. We hypothesized that integrating thermophysical parameters (thermal transition energies and pH-dependent solubility) into the material selection process would yield encapsulation systems with superior thermal and gastrointestinal performance compared to empirically selected formulations. The resulting microcapsules are intended for use as a dry probiotic ingredient in thermally processed food products, namely pasteurized dairy and plant-based beverages, fermented dairy products, fruit-based drinks, and dry-blended or baked matrices such as powdered beverage mixes, cereal bars, and bakery fillings, where the powder must withstand a short heat treatment during processing and subsequent gastric transit before releasing viable cells in the intestine.

The strain was preserved as glycerol stocks (30%, v / v ) at −80 °C and subcultured twice in MRS broth at 37 °C and 100 rpm for 18 h prior to each experiment.

## 2.3.1. Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)

## 2.3.2. Activation Energy Calculation 
 E a ) associated with the endothermic thermal transitions of the coating materials was calculated using the Kissinger method [ 19 ]. This approach is based on the relationship between the heating rate ( β ) and the temperature at which the transition rate reaches its maximum ( Tp ), as described by the following equation: (1) ln β T p 2 = ln AR E a − E a R T p 
 β is the heating rate (°C/min); 
 Tp is the peak transition temperature (K); 
 A is the pre-exponential factor (min −1 ); 
 R is the universal gas constant (8.314 J/mol·K); 
 E a is the activation energy (kJ/mol). 
 E a was determined from the slope of the linear plot of ln( β / Tp 2 ) versus 1/ Tp , where the slope equals − E a / R [ 20 ]. Peak temperatures were obtained from DSC thermograms recorded at 5, 10, and 20 °C/min, and the coefficient of determination (R 2 ) was used to assess the goodness of fit of the linear regression [ 21 ]. The E a values obtained reflect the energy barrier for the composite process of bound water desorption and macromolecular chain relaxation, which governs the thermal stability of the amorphous matrix during drying and storage rather than covalent bond degradation of the polymer backbone [ 10 ]. This interpretation is consistent with previous work on amorphous carbohydrate mixtures, where the activation energy of endothermic transitions has been used as a proxy for structural stability and resistance to collapse under processing and storage conditions [ 22 ]. 
## 2.3.3. Solubility of Coating Materials and Thermoprotectants

Solubility was expressed as the percentage of dissolved solids relative to total solids initially dispersed [ 23 ]. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

## 2.5. Microencapsulation by Spray Drying 
## 2.5.1. Inoculum Preparation 
 Lactiplantibacillus plantarum cultures were prepared by inoculating 1% ( v / v ) of an activated culture into 500 mL of MRS broth and incubating at 37 °C and 100 rpm for 18 h. It was confirmed that the cultures had reached the early stationary phase (~10 9 CFU/mL) before harvest. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8000× g for 10 min at 4 °C, washed twice with sterile phosphate-buffered saline solution (PBS, pH 7.2, 0.85% NaCl), and resuspended in the encapsulation solution [ 27 ]. 
## 2.5.2. Preparation of the Feed Solution

The bacterial suspension was then added to the feed solution; the 10% ( w / v ) figure refers to the wall materials only and does not include the added bacterial biomass. In the thermoprotectant-loaded formulations, the optimized thermoprotectant blend was added at 2.5% ( w / v ) in addition to the wall system ( Section 2.4.2 ), so that the feed delivered to the dryer contained 12.5% ( w / v ) total solids, whereas the unprotected control contained 10% ( w / v ). The mixture was homogenized using a high-shear homogenizer at 5000 rpm for 1.5 min to ensure uniform dispersion of all components prior to atomization [ 28 ]. Preliminary standardization assays in which viable counts (CFU/mL) were measured before and after this step confirmed that high-shear homogenization did not reduce the viability of the bacterial culture prior to drying. The feed solution was maintained at 4 °C for no longer than 30 min before spray drying to minimize cell loss prior to processing.

## 2.6.1. Probiotic Survival After Spray Drying

Post-drying survival was calculated as the ratio of the viable count recovered in the powder to that of the corresponding feed suspension and, in parallel, as the reduction in log cycles between the two counts, as follows: (2) S ( % ) = N 1 N 0 × 100 ,   Δ log N = log N 0 − log N 1 
 1 is the viable count of the powder after drying and N 0 is the viable count of the corresponding feed suspension before drying, both expressed as CFU per gram of dry solids [ 23 , 29 ]. Referring both counts to the same dry-solids basis ensures that S represents the fraction of viable cells recovered after drying and is not affected by the dilution of the cells in the dry matrix. The reduction in log cycles (Δlog N) is reported in parallel, since it is the conventional metric in the microbiological literature and is less sensitive to small absolute differences at high survival levels. Because each stage of the experimental design was carried out with an independently prepared culture, N 0 was determined for every run rather than assumed constant across the design. This parameter quantifies the survival of the culture through the drying step and is not an encapsulation efficiency in the strict sense, since the latter requires washing the particle surface to remove non-encapsulated cells and quantifying only the fraction retained within the matrix, a procedure that was not performed in this work. Accordingly, the term survival is used throughout the manuscript, and the scanning electron micrographs are interpreted as qualitative evidence of particle morphology and wall continuity rather than as evidence of the spatial distribution of cells within or on the particles.

## 2.6.5. Evaluation of Gastrointestinal Resistance 
 L. plantarum under simulated gastrointestinal conditions was assessed using the INFOGEST 2.0 static protocol [ 32 ]. The simulated salivary, gastric and intestinal fluids (SSF, SGF and SIF) were prepared from the electrolyte stock solutions specified in the protocol (KCl, KH 2 PO 4 , NaHCO 3 , NaCl, MgCl 2 ·6H 2 O, (NH 4 ) 2 CO 3 and CaCl 2 ) at the concentrations given therein, and each digestive phase was carried out at the prescribed 1:1 ( v / v ) ratio of simulated fluid to sample, with CaCl 2 and the enzymes added to the final activities specified by the protocol. Oral phase: 0.5 g of powder was first reconstituted in 4.5 mL of water to give 5 mL of a 10% ( w / v ) food matrix, since the protocol requires a fluid food as the starting material; this reconstituted matrix was then mixed with 5 mL of simulated salivary fluid (pH 7.0, α-amylase 75 U/mL), corresponding to the prescribed 1:1 ( v / v ) ratio, and incubated at 37 °C for 2 min at 150 rpm. The water therefore serves to reconstitute the dry powder into the food matrix and does not dilute the simulated fluid, so the final electrolyte and enzyme concentrations in each phase are those of the standard protocol. Gastric phase: pH was adjusted to 3.0 with 1 M HCl, pepsin (2000 U/mL) was added, and the mixture was incubated for 120 min. Intestinal phase: pH was adjusted to 7.0 with 1 M NaOH, pancreatin (100 U/mL) and bile salts (10 mM) were added, and incubation continued for 120 min. Viable cell counts were performed at the end of each phase to quantify viable-cell recovery of L. plantarum in each gastrointestinal compartment [ 33 ].

The 3 × 2 factorial design was analyzed by two-way ANOVA including the protein × polysaccharide interaction term. Comparisons between the two optimized formulations, with and without thermoprotectant, involve only two groups and were therefore assessed by a two-sample Student’s t -test ( p < 0.05). For the in vitro digestion assay, viable counts were log 10 -transformed; within each digestive phase, the three treatments were compared by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test ( p < 0.05), and gastric and intestinal counts of each treatment, obtained from the same digestion run ( n = 3 independent runs), were compared by a paired Student’s t -test ( p < 0.05). For the D-optimal mixture design, the significance of individual and interaction terms was evaluated within the ANOVA of the fitted model rather than by pairwise post hoc comparison, because the runs are design points of the mixture space and not independent treatment groups. Activation energies obtained by the Kissinger method were derived by linear regression and are reported with their coefficients of determination. For experimental design analyses, Design-Expert software version 13 was used. A special cubic Scheffé polynomial model was fitted to the mixture-design data and validated based on residual analysis, coefficient of determination (R 2 ), adjusted R 2 , and lack-of-fit tests. ## 3.1. Thermal Characterization and Solubility of Coating Materials

Because these transitions correspond to a composite endothermic event dominated by the loss of free and weakly bound water, with possible contributions from desorption of more strongly bound moisture, rearrangement of hydrogen bonding, relaxation of the amorphous phase and overlapping protein transitions, the resulting Ea values are apparent descriptors of that composite event. They are not fundamental parameters of polymer-matrix stability, are not measurements of the glass transition, and are used here only as an empirical screening criterion. It should also be noted that the DSC experiment and the spray-drying process differ substantially: a few milligrams of sample are heated in a sealed pan in which water vapour is not freely removed and the vapour pressure may rise, over heating times of minutes. A higher apparent Ea therefore cannot be assumed to translate directly into greater protection during atomization and drying. Activation energies ranged from 95.4 kJ/mol (WPC; R 2 = 0.99) to 198.4 kJ/mol (guar gum; R 2 = 0.882), with microcrystalline cellulose (175.3 kJ/mol; R 2 = 0.996) and food-grade collagen (152.7 kJ/mol; R 2 = 0.994) showing the highest resistance to thermal transition among the materials considered potentially relevant for encapsulation. Gum arabic yielded an intermediate E a of 112.4 kJ/mol (R 2 = 0.970); although this R 2 was comparatively low, it was considered acceptable for the Kissinger method given the polydisperse molecular architecture of gum arabic (MW range: 250,000–2,000,000 Da), which generates partially overlapping thermal events [ 11 ]. Solubility measurements also showed marked pH-dependent behavior ( Figure 1 ). WPC and gum arabic displayed complementary solubility profiles at pH 4.0 and 8.0, which supported their subsequent selection as a binary wall system.

The linear mixture terms were jointly significant ( p = 0.007), indicating differential individual contributions of the four components to the thermoprotective response. These three terms were nevertheless retained because the special cubic Scheffé model is hierarchical: the significant three-component terms ABC, ACD, and BCD require the presence of the lower-order terms nested within them, and their removal would yield a non-hierarchical model whose coefficients are no longer invariant to the choice of component reference in the simplex coordinate system. The pattern of significant and non-significant pairs therefore indicates selective synergistic or antagonistic pairing effects within a model whose term structure is fixed by hierarchy rather than by stepwise selection. The ternary interactions ABC ( p = 0.017), ACD ( p = 0.002), and BCD ( p = 0.022) were all significant, indicating that higher-order interactions contributed to the response. The model explained 91.9% of the observed variance (R 2 = 0.919; adjusted R 2 = 0.781), and the difference between R 2 and adjusted R 2 (0.138) is consistent with the inclusion of higher-order polynomial terms necessary to capture the nonlinear behavior of the quaternary mixture space; importantly, the non-significant lack-of-fit confirms that this gap does not reflect systematic model inadequacy. The predicted R 2 was 0.234, and the difference relative to the adjusted R 2 indicates that the model, while adequate for describing the mixture space and locating the optimal region, has limited extrapolative capability. This behavior is expected for a ten-term special cubic model fitted to twenty runs: the resulting seven residual degrees of freedom give each design point high leverage, which inflates the leave-one-out PRESS statistic on which the predicted R 2 is based. For this reason, the optimum was confirmed experimentally rather than relying on model prediction alone ( Figure 3 b). Residual diagnostics are provided in Supplementary Figure S1 ; no observation exceeded the Bonferroni outlier limits (±5.60) and the residual variance was homogeneous across the predicted range. ## 4. Discussion 
## 4.1. Thermal Characterization and Solubility of Coating Materials: Thermophysical Basis for Rational Selection 
 16 ], who demonstrated that higher E a values in carbohydrate polymer blends directly correlated with greater core protection. The broad endothermic transitions observed between 100 and 165 °C are consistent with composite thermal events involving bound-water loss and macromolecular relaxation, as expected for amorphous biopolymer systems in which residual moisture acts as a plasticizer [ 10 , 11 ]. It should be noted that the E a values derived from these transitions represent the energy barrier for the coupled desorption–relaxation process, not for covalent bond degradation of the polymer backbone. Nevertheless, this parameter may still be functionally informative, since materials with higher apparent E a may be hypothesized to resist structural relaxation for longer periods at elevated temperatures, potentially providing thermal shielding during the transient high-temperature exposure inherent to spray drying [ 19 ]; this remains a hypothesis, since the glass transition temperature was not measured ( Section 3.1 ). The systematic shift of peak temperatures with increasing heating rate supported the application of the Kissinger model across all candidates [ 19 ]. For gum arabic, the fitted R 2 remained acceptable for comparative purposes and may reflect the well-known polydispersity of this material, which can broaden and partially overlap thermal events [ 11 ]. Despite their high E a values (198.4 kJ/mol for guar gum and 175.3 kJ/mol for microcrystalline cellulose), neither material was suitable for spray drying at 10% ( w / v ) under the conditions used in this study because both formed highly viscous or poorly dispersible systems that were incompatible with stable and uniform atomization [ 34 ]. This confirms that thermal transition energy is a necessary but insufficient selection criterion when evaluated in isolation, given that aqueous solubility, food-grade regulatory status, emulsifying capacity, and film-forming kinetics must be assessed concurrently [ 7 ]. Among protein candidates satisfying all functional criteria, WPC ( E a = 95.4 kJ/mol), SPI ( E a = 118.5 kJ/mol), and calcium caseinate ( E a = 127.8 kJ/mol) were selected because they represent systems with distinct thermal and interfacial properties: WPC combines whey protein-driven surface film formation with a lactose-rich amorphous phase that contributes to the overall T g of the dried matrix; SPI provides reduced solubility under acidic conditions (pI ≈ 4.5), potentially limiting premature cell release in acidic food matrices; and calcium caseinate offers a disordered micellar structure with higher intrinsic thermal transition energy but slower film-forming kinetics [ 15 , 16 ]. WPC is a multicomponent system containing β-lactoglobulin, α-lactalbumin, bovine serum albumin, immunoglobulins, and residual lactose (typically 35–55% depending on the concentrate grade), and its thermal behavior in DSC reflects the superposition of whey protein denaturation (onset ~65 °C for β-lactoglobulin) and lactose glass transition events. The relatively low apparent E a of WPC should therefore not be interpreted as intrinsically poor thermal suitability. Rather, its behavior likely reflects overlapping protein and lactose-related transitions, and the amorphous lactose fraction may have contributed additional stabilization to the dried matrix [ 35 , 36 ]. Among the polysaccharides, gum arabic was particularly attractive because of its surface-active proteinaceous fraction (approximately 2% by weight) and its functional suitability for food applications. OSA-modified cassava starch ( E a = 98.6 kJ/mol; contact angle 76.10°) was also considered a promising candidate because of its amphiphilic character and potential interfacial functionality. Solubility measurements at pH 4.0 and 8.0 ( Figure 1 ) confirmed differential pH-dependent profiles for the five candidates selected for factorial evaluation. The selection of pH 4.0, proximate to the isoelectric point of the major whey protein fractions (β-lactoglobulin pI ≈ 5.2; α-lactalbumin pI ≈ 4.2), and pH 8.0, representing mildly alkaline conditions encountered in the distal small intestine, was designed to capture the extremes of protein solubility behavior relevant to food processing and gastrointestinal transit. At pH 4.0, WPC exhibited reduced solubility consistent with charge neutralization and protein aggregation near the pI, which would contribute to wall integrity in acidic food matrices (pH 3.5–4.5) and during the initial stages of gastric transit. At pH 8.0, increased solubility was observed for both WPC and gum arabic, consistent with electrostatic repulsion above the pI, which would favor controlled wall dissolution and probiotic release under intestinal alkaline conditions [ 32 ]. These combined thermophysical and solubility criteria provided the quantitative rationale for advancing WPC, SPI, and calcium caseinate as protein factors and gum arabic and OSA-modified cassava starch as polysaccharide factors to the factorial design.

In addition, the residual lactose present in WPC (~35–55%, depending on concentrate grade) may have provided a further stabilization pathway through its contribution to the amorphous phase of the dried matrix. Amorphous lactose, with a T g of approximately 101 °C, can act as a co-vitrifier that increases the effective T g of the wall matrix, potentially extending the protective glassy state to higher temperatures and longer exposure times. This interpretation is consistent with the high survival observed, although the precise contribution of each WPC component to the overall thermal protection cannot be deconvoluted from the present experimental design and warrants further investigation using compositionally defined protein isolates. The poorer performance of calcium caseinate formulations ( Table 2 ), despite their comparatively high Kissinger activation energy (127.8 kJ/mol; Table 1 ), indicates that thermal transition parameters alone do not adequately capture encapsulation performance. This observation reinforces the need for a multicriteria formulation strategy in which thermal behavior is interpreted together with capsule morphology, barrier properties, and storage conditions, as highlighted by recent reviews on the microencapsulation of food bioactives [ 37 ]. Caseinate consists of open, disordered micellar aggregates whose rheological behavior under atomization conditions is predominantly viscous rather than viscoelastic [ 16 ], resulting in slower and less uniform film formation at the droplet surface, which allows thermal penetration to the particle interior before a continuous protective barrier is established. SPI–OSA starch produced the highest mean viable count (9.8 × 10 9 CFU/g), but it also showed the greatest inter-replicate variability (CV = 3.02%; Table 2 ). This reduced reproducibility may be related to structural and interfacial heterogeneity previously described for OSA-modified starch [ 38 ]. From a formulation perspective, this higher variability weakens the practical attractiveness of SPI–OSA starch despite its high mean count, particularly in comparison with the more reproducible WPC–gum arabic system. WPC–gum arabic was therefore selected as the optimal wall system. Because the two responses of the factorial design ranked different treatments first, the selection was made against an explicit multi-response criterion rather than on viable count alone. SPI–OSA-modified cassava starch gave the highest absolute post-drying count, but it was not selected for three reasons. First, its post-drying survival was substantially lower (66.11% versus 97.76%), indicating that its high absolute count reflects the load of the feed rather than protection during drying. Second, its between-replicate variability was the largest of the six combinations, which compromises reproducibility at scale. Third, the pH-dependent solubility profile of OSA-modified cassava starch was less favourable across the acidic and alkaline conditions tested, which is directly relevant to the intended gastrointestinal application. Survival, reproducibility, and pH-dependent solubility were therefore weighted above absolute viable count, since a formulation that preserves a high fraction of the cells it receives is more transferable than one whose apparent performance depends on the concentration of the inoculum.

Trehalose (29.7% in Mixture 5) likely contributed through intracellular vitrification. As a non-reducing disaccharide with an anhydrous T g of 115.9 °C, it can form cooperative hydrogen-bonding networks that immobilize lipid bilayers and cytoplasmic proteins in a glassy matrix, thereby reducing thermally induced conformational changes during rapid dehydration [ 13 , 17 ]. Trehalose has also been proposed to replace structural water molecules at the polar headgroup region of phospholipids, helping to preserve bilayer integrity during the dehydration associated with both spray drying and thermal challenge [ 39 ]. Glycerol (25.7%) may have contributed through a faster, membrane-directed effect. As a low-molecular-weight polyol (MW 92.09 g/mol), it can interact with phospholipid head groups during the initial stages of dehydration, potentially limiting lateral phase separation before trehalose-mediated vitrification becomes dominant [ 39 ]. This temporal complementarity is consistent with the higher individual survival values observed for glycerol monoformulations (Runs 2 and 7; 11.7–14.3%; Table 3 ) relative to trehalose monoformulations (Runs 3 and 8; 10.6–11.4%; Table 3 ), as well as with the improved performance observed when both were combined. However, glycerol is also expected to depress the T g of the amorphous matrix because of its low molecular weight and plasticizing effect. The fact that glycerol-containing formulations still showed higher survival under the acute thermal challenge used here (92 °C, 3–8 min) suggests that membrane-associated stabilization may have outweighed any T g -depressing effect under these conditions. Whether this balance would shift during prolonged storage at ambient temperature, where matrix mobility becomes more critical, remains to be determined. FOS (19.8%) and inulin (24.8%) likely contributed primarily at the extracellular level through osmotic buffering and matrix-forming effects. Their high degree of polymerization (FOS: DP 3–10; inulin: up to 60) would also be expected to limit membrane penetration, which is consistent with the comparatively low survival of pure inulin formulations (Runs 12–13; 8.0–8.3%; Table 3 ) [ 26 ]. Within the quaternary mixture, FOS and inulin likely acted as extracellular matrix-forming components that slowed moisture migration during thermal challenge and contributed to the overall T g of the dried system through their relatively high molecular weight, thereby complementing the protection associated with trehalose and glycerol. The increase in viable counts from the gastric to the intestinal phase for both encapsulated formulations warrants careful interpretation. Even with this limitation, the overall pattern remains consistent with the central design logic of this study: pH-dependent wall solubility can be exploited to combine greater resistance under gastric conditions with increased release under intestinal-like conditions. Although the screening pH values (4.0 and 8.0) do not exactly reproduce the gastric (pH 3.0) and intestinal (pH 7.0) conditions of the INFOGEST protocol, they captured the same directional behavior of lower solubility under acidic conditions and higher solubility under neutral-to-alkaline conditions.

Thermal transition energy ( E a ) alone was not a sufficient selection criterion, but interpreted together with film-forming behavior and pH-dependent solubility it identified WPC–gum arabic as the most suitable binary wall system (97.76 ± 0.75% survival; significant protein–polysaccharide interaction, p < 0.0001). Thermoprotection at 92 °C depended on higher-order multicomponent interactions ( p ≤ 0.022), and the optimized quaternary blend (trehalose 0.297, glycerol 0.257, FOS 0.198, inulin 0.248; total 2.5% w / v ) improved post-drying viability by 2.45 log CFU/g and maintained viable counts of the order of 10 7 to 10 8 CFU/mL of digesta throughout simulated INFOGEST 2.0 digestion. The comparison with the unprotected control is equally informative: WPC–gum arabic microcapsules without internal protectants were effectively inactivated during the gastric phase, retaining counts of the order of 10 3 CFU/mL of digesta. The protein–polysaccharide wall therefore acts mainly as a physical barrier and as a vehicle for intestinal release, and is not by itself sufficient for gastric survival; the internal osmotic and membrane-stabilizing protectants are what make gastric transit survivable. It should also be noted that free cells of this acid-tolerant strain retained higher absolute counts than the encapsulated systems, so the gastrointestinal benefit demonstrated here is relative to unprotected microcapsules rather than to free cells.
