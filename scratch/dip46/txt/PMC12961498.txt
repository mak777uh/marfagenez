## Introduction 
 Their deposition in biological tissues is the
pathological hallmark of more than 40 human amyloidoses, including
Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and dialysis-related
amyloidosis. In these diseases, amyloid
formation occurs at the earliest stage of the pathogenic cascade.
Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms
underlying amyloid formation is essential for developing effective
strategies to prevent amyloidosis. 
 − 
 The phase transition of denatured monomers into amyloid fibrils
can occur when the monomer concentration exceeds its thermodynamic
solubility. It should be noted that the solubility of denatured monomers
differs from that of the native state. 
 , 
 In general,
denatured monomers are less soluble compared to their native counterparts, so that extensive denaturation of native monomers
often facilitates amyloid formation. 
 The reaction reaches
equilibrium when the concentration of remaining monomers equals its
solubility. In addition to the thermodynamic aspect, the kinetics
of amyloid formation have been extensively investigated using chemical
kinetics. 
 , 
 These studies have revealed detailed molecular
mechanisms underlying amyloid formation of IDPs, under a variety of reaction conditions. 
 However, the folding reaction between native and denatured monomers
should also be taken into account in the description of the overall
system. Previous studies have successfully captured the thermodynamic
equilibrium among native monomers, denatured monomers, and amyloid
fibrils by linking the two-state transition between native and denatured
monomers with the solubility-limited phase transition between denatured
monomers and amyloid fibrils. 
 , 
 Nevertheless, the quantitative
analysis of amyloid formation kinetics, in relation to the folding
reaction, has not yet been addressed. 
 2 -microglobulin (β2m) and α-Syn, 
 , 
 representing natively folded proteins and IDPs, respectively. We
focus on the nucleation rate as a key kinetic parameter under varying
salt concentrations and temperatures, which modulate both the solubility
of denatured monomers and the equilibrium between native and denatured
states. 
 − 
 Detailed kinetic analyses are often hindered by the
inherent low reproducibility of the reaction originating from the
stochastic nature of nucleation and the high sensitivity of amyloid
formation to ambient conditions. 
 , 
 To overcome
these limitations, we develop a miniaturized ultrasonic platform (μHANABI:
micro-HANdai Amyloid Burst Inducer) that enables reproducible high-throughput
analysis of amyloid formation kinetics. This platform exploits ultrasonication to robustly accelerate amyloid
formation in supersaturated solutions of denatured monomers. 
 − 
 Using μHANABI, we quantify the nucleation rates of β2m
and α-Syn under well-controlled conditions and analyze the results
within the framework of the classical nucleation theory (CNT). 
 , 
 Furthermore, nucleation-dependent amyloid formation governed by
denatured-monomer solubility is linked to native-denatured folding
equilibria using a two-state transition model.

structural transition from random-coil monomers to β-sheet-rich

around 3 h, reaching a plateau after approximately 5 h. The average
lag time for nucleation of the α-Syn samples is 3.3 ± 0.2
h (mean ± SD), corresponding to a CV value of 6.2%. Achieving
highly reproducible amyloid formation assays is essential for quantitative
analysis of the physicochemical mechanisms underlying amyloid formation. However, reproducibility is often limited by
the stochastic nature of the rate-limiting nucleation step, which
leads to large variability in amyloid formation kinetics. 
 , 
 Indeed, previous studies have reported CV values of the lag time
ranging from 10 to 25%, even under optimized conditions with ultrasonic
or shaking agitation. 
 , 
 In comparison, μHANABI
substantially improves assay reproducibility, reducing the CV value
of the lag time by approximately half. 
 Cavitation bubbles undergo periodic expansion and shrinkage
synchronized with the acoustic field. During the expansion phase,
protein monomers are adsorbed at the bubble interface, whereas subsequent
shrinkage under positive acoustic pressure condenses the adsorbed
monomers. This local increase in protein concentration can accelerate
nucleation events. 
 , 
 To achieve high well-to-well
reproducibility of amyloid formation kinetics, independent samples
should be exposed to a uniform acoustic field. Accordingly, μHANABI
is designed with an axially symmetric configuration of sample wells
and the ultrasonic transducer, enabling reproducible and simultaneous
kinetic measurements with minimal intersample variability.

## Implications toward Clinical Treatment of Amyloidosis 
 , 
 suppressing nucleation in vivo represents a promising therapeutic
strategy for amyloidosis. Importantly, our results suggest that effective
intervention strategies may differ fundamentally between IDPs and
natively folded proteins. 
 surfactant micelles, or de novo-designed peptides. In contrast,
for natively folded proteins, stabilizing the native state is also
an effective strategy to inhibit amyloid nucleation by shifting the
equilibrium away from denatured monomers. This principle has been
validated clinically in transthyretin (TTR) amyloidosis, where benzoxazole
compounds such as Tafamidis bind to and stabilize the native tetramer,
thereby suppressing amyloid formation. 
 − 
 Similarly, in other
systemic amyloidoses caused by natively folded proteins, including
β2m- and immunoglobulin light-chain amyloidoses, disease-associated
mutations often destabilize the native structure. 
 , 
 These observations suggest that small molecules designed to stabilize
native conformations may offer a general therapeutic strategy for
amyloidoses derived from folded proteins.
