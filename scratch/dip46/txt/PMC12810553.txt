## Introduction 
 Physicochemical characterization of agro-industrial by-products such as broad bean leaves and beetroot leaves, which are primarily an abundant source of proteins and other compounds with high nutritional value, is essential to evaluate their nutritional potential and functional properties, both of which are critical for their integration into the human diet and the development of innovative food products ( Heppner & Livney, 2023 ; M. Kumar et al., 2022 ; Režek Jambrak et al., 2025 ). These characterizations typically include proximate composition (moisture, protein, fat, ash, fiber), mineral and vitamin profiles, phytochemical content (e.g., phenolics, flavonoids, betalains), and techno-functional properties such as water-holding capacity, oil-binding capacity, solubility, emulsifying ability, and thermal behavior ( Balandrán-Quintana, 2018 ; Guo et al., 2024 ; Martinez et al., 2024 ; Ostojić et al., 2025 ). Thermal analysis techniques are essential tools for elucidating the physicochemical transformations that occur in food systems during processing, storage, and preparation. By monitoring temperature-induced changes in the thermal, structural, and functional properties of food components, these methods provide critical insights for food science, technology, and quality assurance ( Roos, 2003 ). The application of thermal analysis for the characterization of agro-industrial waste leaves is poorly represented in the literature. More recently, studies have investigated the thermal behavior of dried sugar beet pulp, leaves, and leaf fractions ( Ostojić et al., 2025 ). Utilization of a scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDS) in plant research is well documented in the literature ( Gins et al., 2018 ; Mantovani et al., 2018 ; Matthaeus et al., 2020 ; Pathan et al., 2008 ; Shakeel et al., 2021 ; Šoštarić et al., 2023 ; Sugumar & Guha, 2025 ) . SEM analysis of plant leaf gives high-resolution information about general surface morphology (smooth or rough texture of the leaf) and microstructure of epidermal cells, guard cells, trichomes, stomata (their size, shape, number and distribution). These data are important in determining development stages of the leaf, damage caused by preparation procedures (drying, powdering), possible pollutant deposition on the surface, etc. Coupled with EDS, elemental composition of the features of interest observed by SEM can be determined, for example presence of nutrients and contaminants (heavy metals and fertilizers), as well as average composition of bigger areas and distribution of chemical elements on the leaf surface. By combining SEM/EDS with Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), detailed information about functional groups and chemical bonds of leaf constituents such as cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, proteins, lipids, pectin are obtained, as well as changes due to different treatments, stress and pollution ( Abidi et al., 2014 ; Boeriu et al., 2004 ; Canteri et al., 2019 ; Ribeiro da Luz, 2006 ). FTIR technique is also often used for analysis of secondary metabolites (phenolics, flavonoids), thus obtaining information on antioxidant activity ( Abidi et al., 2014 ; Agatonovic-Kustrin et al., 2021 ; Boeriu et al., 2004 ; Canteri et al., 2019 ; Johnson et al., 2020 ; X. Lu et al., 2011 ). Contrary to SEM/EDS and FTIR, there is not much data in the literature on the characterization of plant leaves. by X-ray diffraction (XRD) technique. The most publications concentrate on biosynthesis of metal and metal oxide nanoparticles such as Ag, Pd, CuO, TiO 2 , ZnO, SnO 2 , WO 3 ( Akintelu et al., 2021 ; Al-darwesh et al., 2024 ; Deokar et al., 2018 ; Matussin et al., 2020 ; Parameshwaran et al., 2013 ; Suresh et al., 2015 ; Tran et al., 2022 ) from leaf extracts, where XRD is key method for determination of crystal phase composition and crystallographic parameters of obtained nanophases. To the best of our knowledge, only powdered leaf samples of mango Mangifera indica ( Dey et al., 2022 ) and cucumber Cucumis sativus ( Chandra & Alam, 2022 ) were investigated from the crystallographic point of view. XRD technique can give information regarding cellulose which can exist in both crystalline and amorphous phase, type of crystalline cellulose polymorph (I – native and II and chemically treated), degree of crystallinity ( Al-Awa et al., 2023 ; Chakraborty et al., 2022 ; Choi et al., 2018 ), influence of chemical and physical (powdering) treatment, mineral content as influence of drying (calcium oxalate, potassium salts, silica) ( Gómez-Espinoza et al., 2021 ), changes in crystallinity by comparing fresh and dried leaf. Quantitative analysis by XRD can be used to determine percentage of crystalline cellulose, ratio of different cellulose polymorphs, and percentage of minerals. 
 Comprehensive physicochemical analysis of plant leaves is not well documented in the literature particularly for the beetroot ( Beta vulgaris subsp. vulgaris ) and broad bean (Vicia faba) leaves. The present study also introduces an approach for developing a comprehensive characterization, both qualitative and quantitative, which will provide comparative measurements of different plant samples and influence of different preparation/pretreatment methods. 
 Accordingly, the objective of this study is to perform a detailed physicochemical, characterization of dried powders of the beetroot ( Beta vulgaris subsp. vulgaris ) and broad bean (Vicia faba) leaves. This will provide critical data to support the development of efficient, non-thermal protein extraction strategies from these underutilized leafy biomasses. The novelty of this research lies in the comprehensive analysis of leaves that are typically discarded as waste in the food industry. By applying an extensive analytical approach, this study offers a complete insight into the potential for upcycling these leaves and valorizing them to their full functional potential. 2. 
## Materials and methods 2.1. 
## Sample preparation 
 Beans ( Vicia faba L.) and red beetroot ( Beta vulgaris subsp. vulgaris ) were cultivated in the foothills of the continental part of the Republic of Croatia. Specifically, beans were grown in the fields of NutriS d.o.o. and red beetroot in the fields of Naturala d.o.o. in the Slatina region. The leaves of the plant materials were manually separated in the field. The leaves were dried at indirect sunlight, at average temperature between 25 and 30 °C, a dry matter content of over 90 % was reached. After reaching a satisfactory dry matter value, the dried leaves were grinded in a Retsch GM 200 mill (Retsch, Düsseldorf, Germany) and manually sieved with a Retsch sieve (Retsch, Düsseldorf, Germany) to a particle size of <500 μm (leaves powder of broad bean – Bblp <500 and red beetroot dried leaves powder -Rblp <500) and to a particle size of 500–1000 μm (leaves powder of broad bean - Bblp 1000 and red beetroot dried leaves powder -Rblp 1000). The dried, crushed and sieved leaves were used for further analysis. 2.2. 
## Determination of chemical composition 
 Moisture, crude ash, fat, and protein contents were determined according to the standard methods HRN ISO 6496, 2001 ; 2008; HRN ISO 5984, 2023 ; HRN ISO 6492, 2001 ; HRN EN ISO 5983-2, 2008 , respectively. 2.3. 
## Thermal analysis 
 Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was performed on dried leaves powders of broad bean ( Vicia faba) and Beetroots ( Beta vulgaris subsp. vulgaris ) with different particle sizes: Bblp <500 μm, Rblp <500 μm Bblp 1000 and Rblp 1000). The DSC Q1000 differential scanning calorimeter, with RCS cooling system TA Instruments, New Castle, Delaware, USA was used. The instrument was calibrated for temperature and enthalpy according to the standard instructions of the manufacturer using standard metal indium ( Gatta et al. 2006 ). All experiments were performed under a nitrogen atmosphere (99.999 % purity), with a flow rate of 50 mL/min in the DSC cell and 60 mL/min in the TGA furnace for thermogravimetric analysis. Samples were weighed using a Mettler Toledo AE 163 analytical balance, with 8–9 mg used for TGA analysis and 4–5 mg for DSC analysis. Prior to thermal analysis, the powder samples were homogenized by thorough mixing. The homogenized powders were then used directly for both TGA and DSC measurements. All TGA and DSC measurements were performed in triplicate. For DSC analysis, samples were sealed in aluminum pans using a TA Blue Press and scanned in two cycles. The first cycle ranged from 20 °C to −90 °C at a cooling rate of 5 °C/min. After equilibrating at −90 °C, the second cycle was conducted from −90 °C to 250 °C, also at a heating rate of 5 °C/min. 
 Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was carried out using a Q500 thermogravimetric analyzer TA Instruments, New Castle, Delaware, USA under a nitrogen atmosphere. The samples were heated from room temperature to 700 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C/min. All measurements were performed in triplicate. 2.3.1. 
## Kinetics of thermal degradation 
 To evaluate the kinetic parameters associated with the thermal degradation of Bblp and Rblp samples, the peak region corresponding to the main degradation event (approximately 120–400 °C) was extracted from the TGA curves. The experiments were carried out at four distinct heating rates: 5, 10, 15, and 20 °C/min. The kinetic analysis followed the recommendations provided by the ICTAC Kinetics Committee for data collection and computational analysis ( Vyazovkin et al., 2011 ; Vyazovkin et al., 2022 ). The apparent activation energy (E) of the degradation process for analyzed samples was determined using the differential, non-isothermal isoconversional method proposed by Friedman (1964) , as expressed in Eq. For each fixed α, the activation energy (Eα) was obtained from the slope of the linear regression of the left-hand side of Eq. (1) plotted versus 1/ T α,i , where T α,i is the temperature associated with the conversion degree α at the i -th heating rate. The Eα values were determined for α ranging from 0.05 to 0.95, with a step increment of 0.05. The natural logarithm of the pre-exponential factor corresponding to each conversion level, ln(A α ), was estimated based on the compensation effect as described by Vyazovkin et al. (2007) . Finally, using the obtained E, A, and T parameters at the maximum degradation peak, the rate constants (k p ) for the thermal degradation process of the examined samples were calculated according to the Arrhenius equation. 2.4. 
## Scanning electron microscopy with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDS) 
 To characterize morphology and elemental composition, samples were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy dispersive X-Ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDS). Analyses were performed on JEOL JSM 6610 LV (Japan) scanning electron microscope. Samples were recorded at different magnifications ranging in interval x 40 - x 600. Micro -elemental analysis was performed using X-Max Large Area Analytical Silicon Drift connected with INCA Energy 350 Microanalysis System (for detection of elements Z > 5 with detection limit ∼0.1 mas. % and resolution of 126 eV). Before SEM measurements were conducted, samples had been positioned on adhesive tape fixed to specimen tabs and then ion sputter coated with carbon using LEICA SCD005 sputter coater. EDS analysis was performed in selected points and regions. 2.5. 
## Determination of water activity (aw) 
 Water activity (aw) was measured at 25 °C using a Novasina LabSwift-aw meter (Novasina AG, Lachen, Switzerland). 2.6. 
## X-ray powder diffraction (XRPD) analysis 
 To investigate crystal nature of sample's constituents, XRPD technique was used. Samples were loaded in holders and pressed by a clean glass slide to ensure coplanarity of the powder surface with the surface of the holder. Samples were recorded at room temperature using Rigaku Smartlab automated multipurpose X-ray diffractometer in θ-θ geometry (the sample in horizontal position) in para-focusing Bragg-Brentano geometry using D/teX Ultra 250 strip detector in 1D XRF suppression mode with CuK α1,2 radiation source (U = 40 kV and I  = 30 mA). The XRD pattern was collected in 2–70°2θ, with step of 0.01°, and data collection speed of 2°/min. The crystal phases were identified in dedicated Rigaku PDXL 2.0 software (with implemented ICCD PDF-22016 database). The detection limit of the instrument is 1 %. 2.7. 
## Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) 
 FTIR technique was employed to get insight into functional group and compounds present in samples. The infrared spectra are recorded with the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer Nicolet™ iN10 Infrared Microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in reflectance mode using the Smart Orbit unit diamond attenuator of total reflection (ATR). The spectra are recorded in the wavenumber interval from 4000 to 500 cm −1 , with the resolution of 4 cm −1 . 2.8. 
## Statistical analysis 
 The results are expressed as mean values accompanied by standard deviations (±SD). Differences among group means were assessed through one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and, where appropriate, Tukey's Honest Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc test and t -test were applied. Statistical significance was considered at p  < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using XLSTAT software (version 2014.5.03; Addinsoft, New York, USA) integrated into Microsoft Excel. 3.

White spots in the micrographs of samples Bblp <500 μm and Bblp 1000 μm Fig. 3 (a b) (Spectra 3 and 8, respectively) correspond to high concentration of iron (Fe), 64.5Wt% and 44.6Wt%, which can originate from contamination during sample preparation. 3.5. 
## X-ray powder diffraction (XRPD) 
 Fig. 4 (a) shows comparison of XRPD patterns of dried leaf samples Bblp <500, Bblp1000, Rblp <500 and Rblp 1000, and identification of diffraction peaks using ICDD database ( The International Centre for Diffraction Data | Materials Identification with the Powder Diffraction File, 2025 ). XRPD patterns of Bblp <500 and Bblp 1000, Fig. 4 , showed no defined diffraction peaks, while only broad halo can be observed indicating amorphous structure of its constituents. Bio-organic compounds in leaves (i.e. cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, flavonoids, chlorophylls) are amorphous or of low crystallinity and cannot be explicitly detected using XRD. Broad halo in interval ∼ 15–30°2θ indicating amorphous nature of bio-organic components consist of several humps overlapping each other and correspond to: cellulose in intervals 15–17°2θ and 20–23°2θ, hemicellulose in interval 18–20°2θ and lignin in interval 18–22°2θ, but also from amorphous silica with very broad hump ∼15–30°2θ, most likely in the form of phytoliths ( Agarwal et al., 2017 ; Choi et al., 2018 ; Gea et al., 2020 ; Nawaz et al., 2019 ; Saber et al., 2022 ; Yapuchura et al., 2019 ) . Contrary to Bblp samples, both Rblp samples showed crystalline nature of some of its constituents. XRPD patterns of Rblp<500 and Rblp1000 are almost identical with small variation in peak intensity ratios. The diffraction peaks of the highest intensity in XRPD patterns of both samples of root beet leaves, Rblp<500 and Rblp1000, originate from oxalate crystal phases, i.e. hydrated calcium oxalate (CaC 2 O 4 ·H 2 O) and hydrated magnesium oxalate (MgC 2 O 4 ·2H 2 O). Peaks at 14.9, 15.2, 24.3, 30.0, 30.6, 31.4, 35.9 and 38.1 2θ can be ascribed to diffraction from (−101), (−110), (020), (−202), (310), (121), (112) and (130) crystal planes, respectively, of monoclinic CaC 2 O 4 ·H 2 O crystal phase (PDF card 20–0231), while peaks at 18.0, 23.1, 28.1, 35.2, 37.8, 43.2, 44.5, 48.8 and 60.5°2θ are attributed to diffraction from (200), (002), (−4 0 2), (021), (202), (−223), (−6 0 2), (023) and (−6 2 5) crystal planes, respectively, of monoclinic MgC 2 O 4 ·2H 2 O crystal phase (PDF card 28–0625). Detected inorganic mineral salt is cubic crystal phase of potassium chloride KCl (PDF card 41–1476), with peaks at 28.2, 40.6 and 50.2 2θ corresponding to diffraction from (2 0 0), (220) and (222) crystal planes, respectively. In addition to sharp diffraction peaks, broad halo in interval ∼ 15–25°2θ can also be observed, corresponding to bio-organic minerals as in the case of Bblp samples. According to EDS results, for both Bblp and Rblp leaf samples, some crystal forms of Ca, Mg sulfate and phosphate may also exist in low amounts and are most likely below detection limit of XRD technique. ## Discussion 4.1. 
## Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) 
 There is a limited amount of literature available on the thermal analysis of red beetroot (Rblp) and broad bean leaves powders (Bblp). Ostojić et al. (2025) investigated the thermal behavior of sugar beet leaves and pulp as a function of their compositional characteristics. 
 In the present study, the thermal behavior of dried leaf samples Bblp 500 μm, Bblp 1000 μm, Rblp 500 μm, and Rblp 1000 μm, was generally consistent with previously reported results for sugar beet leaves ( Ostojić et al., 2025 ). All samples exhibited a broad endothermic transition in the temperature range from 30 °C to 150 °C (Fig. S1). Additionally, a second endothermic peak was observed between 150 °C and 200 °C, but only in the DSC thermograms of the dried broad bean leaves. 
 The initial endothermic peak, common across all samples, is attributed to moisture evaporation and relaxation phenomena typical of low-moisture biopolymer systems. The disruption of hydrogen bonds within the polymer matrix, formed through polymer–polymer interactions, results in structural relaxation, which manifests as an endothermic event ( Rouilly et al., 2006 ). Also, in the temperature range from 30 °C to 150 °C, the thermal denaturation of protein powders occurs, indicating the unfolding of peptide chains ( Cruz-Padilla et al., 2025 ). 
 The second endothermal peak, observed in the DSC curves of the beetroot leaves (Rblp 500 μm, and Rblp 1000 μm) (Fig. S1 and Table 1 ) is consequence of the cellulose and hemicellulose thermal degradation ( Ostojić et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2024 ). 
 The existence of this second peak observed between 150 and 200 °C only in the DSC thermograms of Rblp samples (Fig. S1, Table 1 ), could be also attributed to the thermal transition of cellulose nanocrystals according to the study by P,Lu & Hsieh (P. Lu & Hsieh, 2010 ) eventually present only in the Rblp. According to P. Lu and Hsieh (2010) , the appearance of small endothermic peaks around 160 and 210 °C in the DSC curves of cellulose nanocrystals, which are absent in those of cellulose powder, indicates a distinct decomposition mechanism, likely involving direct solid-to-gas phase transitions catalyzed by surface sulfate groups. On the other hand, this may imply that in the Bblp samples (Fig. S1 and Table 1 ) cellulose is not in a nanocrystalline state. It is also a small probability that the second peak originates from oxalates ( Lozano et al., 1989 ), which are more abundant in the samples of Rblp as it was shown by, XRPD patterns and that is why it can be observed on the DSC curve. 
 Moisture migration, alongside the redistribution of simple sugars in dried plant materials, can significantly influence the glass transition temperature (T g ). Malomar et al. (2024) demonstrated that reducing sugar content in dried products can elevate T g , thereby eliminating the need for anti-caking agents. 
 Glass-transition behavior is a powerful tool for understanding the quantification of water mobility in products and controlling the shelf-life of products. Therefore, water activity and glass-transition temperature are complimentary to each other to quantify the water migration pattern in products precisely ( Chaudhary et al., 2011 ). 
 In current study, a glass transition temperature (T g ) , around 15 °C was detected across all dried leaf samples, indicating comparable amorphous phase behavior and supporting the observation of consistent moisture-related thermal transitions. Also, amorphous phase of all samples was detected by XRPD in current study. Malomar et al. (2024) found that freeze-dried tomato powders thermograms obtained in aw between 0.11 and 0.53 shows the existence of T g at a temperature of around 1 5 °C, as it was found in the present study ( Table 1 ). Also, the aw value of the samples in the present study were about 0.5, what correspondents to the aw value found for samples of tomato powders in the study of Malomar et al. (2024) . It can be proposed that glass transition (T g ) at 15 °C, found in present study, corresponds to the glass transition of a matrix formed by water and simple sugars present in the samples. This phenomenon is due to a separation of the phase of polymer, which is taking place between proteins and plasticizers (simple sugars), which is typical for systems formed by blends of polymers ( Malomar et al., 2024 ). The second T g found by same authors at temperature around 50 °C is incorporated in the first endothermal transition found also in present study (Fig. S1), and is consequence of the biopolymer (cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin) relaxation ( Rouilly et al., 2006 ). 
 DSC curves of all powder leaves samples in current study showed an exothermic deflection that represents final thermal decomposition ( Zhang et al., 2024 ). It can be observed from the DSC curves (Fig. S1) and corresponding data ( Table 1 ) that there is a slight variation in the peak temperatures (T p1 ) of the first endothermic transition among the leaf samples with different particle sizes. 
 The peak temperatures (T p1 ) of the dried broad bean leaves Bblp <500 μm is 79.2 °C while the same sample with higher particle size Bblp 500-1000 μm has peak temperature (T p1 ) of the first endothermal thermal transition T p1  = 67.6 °C suggesting higher thermal stability of the Bblp<500 μm Also, the samples of the dried beetroot leave with smaller particle size (Rblp <500 μm) has a higher peak temperature T p1  = 80.3 °C than same sample with higher particle size Rblp 500-100 μm, T p1  = 71.96 °C, suggesting greater thermal stability of Rblp <500 μm. As it is known, particle size can influence thermal behavior of the samples, according to authors who studied the influence of the particle size of the sugar beet pulp ( Huang et al., 2018 ). They concluded that particle size affected the DSC endothermal transition peak temperature, but no consistent trend was observed. It has been concluded ( Huang et al., 2018 ) that factors other than particle size might influence behavior within this temperature range. Huang et al. (2018) suggested that the exposure of polysaccharide and protein groups, which increases as particle size decreases, could reduce the melting temperature within this temperature range. According to the results obtained for enthalpy ΔH p1 ( Table 1 ) there is no influence of the particle size of the studied samples on enthalpy of transition, meaning that there is no destabilization of the samples by the influence of particle size. In our study it was found that T p1 temperature is lower in samples with higher particle size, so it can be emphasized that further research is needed to clarify the specific mechanisms involved, which was also noted by Huang et al. (2018) . 4.2. 
## Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and derivative thermogravimetry (dTG) 
 The TGA/dTG curves of the thermal degradation processes for dried broad bean and red beetroot leaf powders exhibit similar trends (Fig. S2). The initial mass loss, corresponding to the evaporation of water, is comparable among all samples, ranging from approximately 5.7 to 7.8 %. This indicates that the samples can be classified as low-moisture biopolymers, which tend to undergo similar thermal degradation pathways ( Ostojić et al., 2025 ; Rouilly et al., 2006 ). 
 Thermal degradation for all samples initiates within a similar temperature range, approximately between 170 and 190 °C. This is consistent with the Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) results obtained in the present study, as well as with previous findings reported in the literature ( Ostojić et al., 2025 ; Zlatanović et al., 2019 ). 
 Subsequent mass loss stages can be attributed to the decomposition of specific biopolymeric components. The second weight loss is primarily due to the degradation of low-molecular-weight compounds, such as simple sugars, in the temperature range from 120 to 170 °C ( Verma et al., 2020 ). The corresponding dTG peak is partially overlapped with the third mass loss peak. The third, fourth, and fifth mass loss events correspond to the thermal decomposition of more complex macromolecules, including proteins and polysaccharides (cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin) (V. Kumar et al., 2018 ). Kumar et al. (2018) studied the thermal degradation of sugarcane leaves and found that the large weight loss observed in Zone II, which corresponds to mass losses II, III, and IV in the present study, is primarily due to the thermal degradation of hemicellulose and cellulose molecules. In Zone III, which corresponds to mass losses V and VI in the current study, volatile complex organic compounds are formed through the pyrolysis of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. As previously noted, the sixth mass loss observed between 550 and 700 °C in the samples of Bblp <500 μm and Bblp 1000 μm was not prominent., whereas it was well-defined in the TGA curves of Rblp <500 μm and Rblp 1000 μm (Fig. S2, Table 2 ). Lu and Hsieh (2010) reported that cellulose powders and cellulose nanocrystals exhibit distinct thermal decomposition mechanisms. A similar behavior can be inferred from the present results, suggesting that cellulose in the Rblp samples is partly in nanocrystalline form, while in the Bblp samples it remains amorphous or non-nanocrystalline. These findings are consistent with the DSC results obtained in this study, indicating that the presence of cellulose nanocrystals influences the overall thermal degradation behavior of the Rblp samples. 
 The variations observed between samples in the TGA and DSC results obtained in the present study can be primarily attributed to differences in their chemical composition (Table S1) and to the varying proportions and structural forms (crystalline or amorphous) of the compounds present in the dried leaf powders. 4.3. 
## Kinetics of thermal degradation 
 The dependence of activation energy (E) on the extent of conversion (α) curves obtained by applying the Friedman isoconversion method ( Fig. 1 ) showed similarity among samples and can be compared to the result obtained for fiber-rich leaf fraction (FRLF) and sugar beet fibrous leaf pulp (FLP) ( Ostojić et al., 2025 ). Also, the obtained E and lnA values agree with literature ( Ostojić et al., 2025 ). 
 Differences in particle size among the same leaf samples (e.g., Rblp<500 μm vs. Rblp 1000 μm; Bblp<500 μm vs. Bblp 1000 μm) did not significantly affect the overall thermal degradation profiles as observed from thermogravimetric analysis. However, when applying kinetic analysis to evaluate thermal decomposition behavior through the kinetic triplet (E, ln A, and kp), notable differences in the degradation rate constant (k p ) were observed between samples of different particle sizes ( Table 3 ). 
 The most pronounced variation was found in the Rblp samples: the sample with particles <500 μm had a k p value of 46.12, while the 1000 μm sample exhibited a significantly higher k p of 91.62. In the case of Bblp samples, differences in k p values were also observed, but to a lesser extent Bblp 500 μm showed a k p of 10.61, compared to 18.27 for Bblp 1000 μm. These findings indicate that higher particle sizes tend to be more prone to thermal deterioration, less thermally stable, and decompose more readily. This trend was found in the present study based on the obtained k p values. It is well known that thermal stability was found to be influenced by particle size, as smaller particles with higher surface-to-volume ratios exhibited lower onset and peak transition temperatures due to enhanced surface reactivity and faster heat and mass transfer, whereas larger particles showed slightly higher transition temperatures, indicating greater apparent thermal stability ( Halahlaha et al., 2024 ). Barrial-Lujan et al. (2025) recently studied how particle size affects bioactive properties of fava bean pod powder. They found that finer powder (45 μm) had higher total phenolics and stronger antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, likely due to improved extractability, while coarser powder (200 μm) showed higher vitamin C, lower estimated glycemic index, and greater bioaccessibility of phenolics and chlorophylls, indicating that particle size influences both extractability and digestive release of bioactive compounds. In contrast to these commonly reported trends, the present kinetic analysis clearly demonstrates that samples with smaller particle sizes exhibit lower k p values, indicating slower degradation rates and higher thermal stability compared to larger particle fractions. This stabilization effect is probably consequence of samples specific chemical composition and requires further investigation in the future. 4.4. 
## SEM, EDS, XRD, FTIR 
 SEM technique is widely used to analyze morphology and structure of the plant samples. There are detailed studies of how preparation methods of plant samples for SEM analysis ( Yuan et al., 2020 ) affect their morphology. The drying process itself damages the epidermal surface and causes the shrinkage of the plant tissue but is found to cause no significant loss of the nutrients ( Lewicki & Pawlak, 2003 ). From the micrographs it can be observed that epidermal surface is to some extent damaged. Elements with fibrous structure can be observed on both Bblp <500 μm and Bblp 1000 μm sample micrographs, Fig. 2 . (a, d, e). This is particular noticeable in Fig. 2 (e), where two different types of morphology can be observed: fibrous structure representing cellulose fibers with parts detaching from the fiber walls which are most probably lignin, and rough wave-like structure which contain stomas and represent guard cells. Stomas are elliptical in shape and seem to be non-affected by drying process, while shrinkage of the tissue in the form of wrinkles around stomas can be observed ( Fig. 2 (f)). Detection of cellulose nanocrystals in dried leaves by SEM analysis is not possible due to hydrogen bonding of nanofibrils and their tendency to form aggregates, which are hard to distinguish as cellulose nanocrystals by microscopic techniques. Several methods of isolation of nanofibrils are proposed in the literature (P. Lu & Hsieh, 2010 ). 
 By comparing results from EDS, Table S2, no significant difference in elemental composition was observed between analyzed samples. EDS analysis showed high concentration of carbon (C) and oxygen (O) indicating dominant presence of organic compounds including cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, pectin, chlorophyll. Presence of K and Cl, which was assumed to originate from mineral salt KCl, was confirmed by XRD, which usually crystalizes during drying process. Presence of Ca and Mg can be ascribed to crystal phases of hydrated calcium oxalate (Ca C 2 O 4 ·H 2 O) and hydrated magnesium oxalate (Mg C 2 O 4 ·2H 2 O), which showed the most intense diffraction peaks in both XRPD patterns of samples Rblp <500 μm and Rblp 1000 μm. Also, this is in accordance with obtained DSC results for Rblp as a second endothermic (Fig. S1) peak was observed only in the beetroot leaf samples, occurring between 147 and 200 °C and can be assigned to the oxalate and cellulose nanocrystals presence in the sample according to the literature ( Lozano et al., 1989 ; P. Lu & Hsieh, 2010 ). 
 FTIR spectra of Rblp <500 μm and Rblp 1000 μm samples ( Fig. 4 b) also showed vibrational bands at wavenumbers characteristic for C 2 CaO 4 ·H 2 O and C 2 MgO 4 ·2H 2 O, 1615 cm −1 and 1315 cm −1 (antisymmetric carbonyl stretching vibrations) and OH vibrations at 3300 cm −1 , 780 cm −1 and 520 cm −1 ( Maurice-Estepa et al., 2000 ), which is in accordance with XRD and DSC results ( Lozano et al., 1989 ). 
 Broad halo in interval ∼ 15–25 2θ detected in XRPD patterns ( Fig. 4 A) in both Bblp and Rblp samples, corresponds to amorphous silica most likely in the form of phytoliths, but also from other bio-organic constituents in amorphous for. Even though Bblp samples showed similar elemental composition to Rblp samples, no apearence of diffraction peaks indicate amorphous or low crystallinity phases. Organic phases like cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, pectin, flavonoids, chlorophylls, are mostly of amorphous nature and are not suitable for analysis by XRPD. FTIR analysis of bio-organic compounds is well studied in the literature (P. Lu & Hsieh, 2010 ). 
 By analyzing FTIR spectra of Bblp and Rblp samples, no significant difference can be observed, indicating that in general the same functional groups are present in booth samples, although small variation in intensity band ratio can be observed. ## Conclusion 
 The thermal behavior of broad bean (Bblp) and red beet (Rblp) leaf powders with different particle sizes (500 and 1000 μm) was generally consistent with previous findings for sugar beet leaves. A glass transition temperature around 15 °C was observed in all samples, indicating similar amorphous phase behavior and moisture-dependent transitions, comparable to other low-moisture plant matrices. An additional endothermal peak between 150 and 200 °C appeared only in Rblp samples, likely related to cellulose nanocrystals. Thermogravimetric analysis revealed typical multi-stage degradation of low-moisture biopolymers ( Rouilly et al., 2006 ), with the initial mass loss beginning near 170–190 °C, followed by decomposition of low-molecular-weight compounds ( Verma et al., 2020 ) and later stages attributed to proteins and polysaccharides (V. Kumar et al., 2018 ). A final mass loss between 550 and 700 °C, more evident in Rblp samples, further supports the presence of nanocrystalline cellulose fractions. Kinetic analysis indicated that smaller particle sizes correspond to greater thermal stability. This particle size–dependent stabilization is likely influenced by differences in surface chemistry and composition. 
 SEM micrographs revealed the presence of stomata in all samples, with larger pores (∼20 μm) in coarse powders and smaller or deformed pores (∼10 μm) in finer ones, reflecting the mechanical effects of grinding. Elemental analysis confirmed that carbon and oxygen predominate, with potassium and calcium as major minerals and minor sodium and magnesium contributions, consistent with the composition of green plant tissues. 
 XRPD patterns exhibited broad halos without distinct diffraction peaks, confirming an amorphous structure in both powders, while weak crystalline features detected only in Rblp samples were attributed to oxalates. FTIR spectra supported these findings, showing characteristic OH and amide bands associated with polysaccharides and proteins. 
 Overall, both leaf powders exhibited amorphous, low-crystallinity structures, similar degradation behavior, and particle-size-dependent thermal stability. These results enhance understanding of structure–property relationships in agro-industrial leaf byproducts and support their potential application in sustainable food materials.
