## INTRODUCTION 
 2015 ; Hammes et al.,  2011 ; Ma & Nussinov,  2010 ; Pauling,  1948 ; Richard et al.,  2018 ; Schramm et al.,  2008 ). Upon binding of the substrate, the enzyme undergoes a conformational change from the inactive, unliganded open form to a catalytically active closed form, thereby stabilizing the enzymatic reaction transition state via the induced‐fit model (Hegazy & Richard,  2025a ; Richard,  2019 ). In contrast, some enzymes exist in multiple conformations, and ligand binding stabilizes the active conformation, thereby shifting the population toward the active state through a conformational‐selection mechanism (Boehr et al.,  2009 ; Weikl & Paul,  2014 ). The binding energy from the interaction of the enzyme with the substrate in the active site can be utilized to correctly position the reactive portion of a substrate relative to the functional groups by favorable ionic, hydrogen bonding, van der Waals, and hydrophobic interactions of the transition state to lower the activation energy of the chemical reaction. Meanwhile, the binding energy from the flexible substrate‐binding loop with the non‐reacting portions of the substrate can be used for inducing the conformational change to close the loops, in which waters are excluded from the active site, and making enhanced interaction with the transition state to increase the rate for the reaction (Amyes et al.,  2001 ; Amyes et al.,  2005 ; Chen et al.,  2014 ; Jencks,  1975 ; Johnson et al.,  2016 ; Leemhuis et al.,  2002 ; Leferink et al.,  2014 ; Whitty et al.,  1995 ; Wolfenden & Snider,  2001 ). Despite extensive evidence, the precise contribution of loop‐associated hydrophobic interactions to catalysis remains incompletely defined. 
 1986 ; Kulkarni et al.,  2017 ; Ponnuswamy,  1993 ; Richard et al.,  2016 ; Rose & Wolfenden,  1993 ). Hydrophobic interactions result from the tendency of nonpolar amino acids to aggregate and avoid aqueous environments, thereby creating stable hydrophobic cores within proteins and enzymes. This tendency results in burying hydrophobic residues, which can stabilize the folded state and bind with the nonpolar portion of ligands. Hence, optimal hydrophobic interactions within enzyme active sites are crucial for substrate binding and specificity, and for stabilizing the transition states, thus enhancing catalytic efficiency. Modifying either the hydrophobicity of the enzyme's binding site or substrate by altering the amino acid residues has been demonstrated to have a significant impact on catalytic efficiency in subtilisin and chymotrypsin (Estell et al.,  1986 ; Hansch & Coats,  1970 ; Wangikar et al.,  1995 ). To quantitatively characterize the hydrophobicity of amino acids, different methodologies have been conducted by measuring the propensity of these residues to transition from aqueous to hydrophobic environments, such as organic solution, the transmembrane region of a protein, and theoretical calculation (Fauchere & Pliska,  1983 ; Kyte & Doolittle,  1982 ; Radzicka & Wolfenden,  1988 ; Simm et al.,  2016 ; Wimley & White,  1996 ; Zhao & London,  2006 ). These different approaches give various hydrophobic scales with significant differences among scales, which causes difficulty in elucidating their role in enzyme catalysis quantitatively. To mimic amino acids in the peptide structure, Fauchère and Pliska quantified amino acid hydrophobicity by measuring the partitioning of N‐acetyl‐amino‐acid amides in an octanol/water system compared to that for glycine. This study gives the hydrophobic parameters (π R ) of the side chains of the 20 amino acids and the related free energy for transfer for each amino acid measured from H 2 O to octanol relative to glycine (Fauchere & Pliska,  1983 ). Thereby, the value of π R may provide a basis for evaluating binding energy from the hydrophobic interaction involved in enzyme catalysis. 
 2017 ; Benach et al.,  1999 ; Ladenstein et al.,  2008 ; Savino et al.,  2016 ; Tanaka et al.,  1996 ). These changes in loop conformation upon substrate binding are consistent with the induced fit model, in which binding with reactants induces the conformation changes and arranges into a form suitable for transition‐state formation, and implicates their involvement in enzyme catalysis. However, the precise contribution of loop–substrate hydrophobic interactions in SDR catalysis remains unclear. 3α‐Hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase/carbonyl reductase (3α‐HSD/CR) from Comamonas testosteroni (E.C. 1.1.1.50) is a member of SDR superfamily that oxidizes androsterone with NAD + via a steady‐state ordered Bi Bi mechanism, in which NAD + binds first and NADH is released last (Chang et al.,  2007 ; Hwang et al.,  2013 ; Skalhegg,  1975 ) (Figure  1a,b ). Catalysis proceeds through deprotonation of the C3 hydroxyl group of androsterone by the conserved residue Y155, enabling hydride transfer to the C4 position of the NAD + nicotinamide ring (Hwang et al.,  2005 ). Crystal structures reveal a flexible substrate‐binding loop (residues P185–P219) with an unresolved region spanning residues of 188 TETPLLQAGLQDPRYGESIAK 208 in the apoenzyme (Chang et al.,  2009 ; Grimm et al.,  2000 ). Structure prediction by the program AlphaFold3 ( https://alphafoldserver.com/ ) suggests that this loop adopts a helix‐turn‐helix fold, and hydrophobicity analysis identifies L193 residue as the most nonpolar position based on the F.P. hydrophobic scale through the ProtScale program (Figure  1c,d ). Together with the hydrophobic nature of androsterone, this system provides a suitable model for quantifying the role of hydrophobic interactions of a flexible substrate‐binding loop with a hydrophobic substrate in enzyme catalysis. The hydrophobic parameter (π R ) represents the difference in log D values between a modified side chain and glycine, and provides the related free energy for transfer for each amino acid measured from H 2 O to octanol relative to glycine. A greater partitioning of N‐acetyl‐amino‐acid amides into the n‐octanol phase corresponds to a higher log D value and a larger π R value, characterized by increased hydrophobicity. To prevent generating additional H‐bonds, extra‐electrostatic interactions, and unpaired charges in the active site, which may interfere with the hydrophobic interaction investigated in the enzyme reaction, we replaced L193 with the amino acid residues of W, I, F, M, V, P, A, T, and G with different hydrophobic side chains for the study. ## Functional role of the flexible substrate‐binding loop

Conformational changes and loop motions are widely implicated in solvent exclusion, the precise positioning of catalytic residues and substrates, the optimization of transition‐state interactions, and the facilitation of product release (Johnson & Holyoak, 2010 ; Schramm et al.,  2008 ). The shape complementarity between the enzyme and substrate in the active site is a major determinant of binding energy and specificity, and catalysis is promoted by enforcing near‐attack conformations and by forming active‐site cages that are structurally complementary to transition‐state geometries (Bruice & Benkovic,  2000 ; Fersht,  1999 ). Substrate‐induced conformational changes from the open inactive conformation (E O ) to the closed active conformation (E C ) have been characterized in triosephosphate isomerase, glycerol 3‐phosphate dehydrogenase, and orotidine 5'‐monophosphate decarboxylase. In these enzymes, loop and side‐chain motions promote catalysis by stabilizing the closed active state (E C ) via the binding energy of direct protein–substrate phosphodianion interactions, the side chain‐side chain packing networks, and reshaping the active‐site microenvironment to favor transition‐state formation (Amyes & Richard,  2013 ; Hegazy & Richard,  2025a ; Hegazy & Richard,  2025b ; Malabanan et al.,  2010 ; Richard et al.,  2018 ). 
 + ‐bound binary complex show overall similarity but lack resolution of the flexible substrate‐binding loop (Grimm et al.,  2000 ). MD simulations revealed that this loop adopts a helix–turn–helix structure, open in the apoenzyme and closed in both the WT‐NAD + binary and the WT‐NAD + ‐androsterone ternary complexes. Closure of the NAD + binary complex is critical for subsequent substrate binding, and the ternary complexes display distinct conformations and interactions depending on the substrate. These findings are consistent with an ordered Bi Bi kinetic mechanism, in which NAD + binds first and induces the conformational closure that prepares the active site for substrate entry. In the WT ternary complex with androsterone, the NAD + nicotinamide ring reorients toward androsterone to enable hydride transfer in the reaction. The hydrophobic side‐chain of L193 modulates the local environment together with a salt bridge between R201 and E148 to stabilize the loop closure in the WT‐NAD + binary complex. Upon binding with androsterone, L193 moves toward the A‐ring of androsterone, accompanied by an additional Y202‐N152 hydrogen bond. This closed form expels solvent from the active site and enhances hydrophobic packing between L193 and androsterone, which may further provide a force to stabilize the transition state and lower the activation energy for facilitating the reaction. Thus, the flexible loop appears to function as a dynamic gate to permit substrate access when open and shield the active site when closed. R201 and Y202 serve as gatekeepers, forming key salt‐bridge and hydrogen‐bond interactions by interacting with E148 and N152, respectively, to reinforce the closed state. Substituting R201 or Y202 with alanine disrupts these contacts, reducing catalytic efficiency by 2.7 and 38‐fold, respectively. This is consistent with MD simulations of the ternary complex, in which the Y202‐N152 hydrogen bond and the R201‐E148 salt bridge stabilize the active closed conformation and help tune the active‐site microenvironment to favor formation of the hydride‐transfer transition state. Y202 plays a more direct role in organizing the catalytically competent closed‐loop state, likely due to its position at the loop tip and closer proximity to the substrate region. In addition to the Y202‐N152 hydrogen bond, the aromatic ring of Y202 may contribute to local packing and hydrophobic contacts with the bound androsterone, whereas R201 appears to function primarily through the R201‐E148 interaction network that supports loop closure. 
 Comamonas testosteroni belongs to the short‐chain dehydrogenase/reductase (SDR) superfamily. Sequence alignments across SDR enzymes typically reveal low overall sequence identity but conservation of motifs, including the N‐terminal Gly‐X 3 ‐Gly‐X‐Gly cofactor‐binding sequence and the catalytically important Ser‐Tyr‐Lys‐Asn tetrad, whereas the substrate‐binding loop is among the most divergent regions (Filling et al.,  2002 ; Kallberg et al.,  2002 ). To assess conservation within bacterial 3α‐hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases, we retrieved protein sequences from Gram‐negative species in the NCBI Reference Sequence database (RefSeq, http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/RefSeq ) and performed multiple‐sequence alignment. Notably, L193, R201, and Y202 within the substrate‐binding loop are highly conserved among Gram‐negative orthologs (Figure  S7 ), supporting a functionally important role for this loop region. Broader phylogenetic analyses of more distantly related homologs will be needed to determine whether these residues govern active‐site loop function more generally. 3.2

Comparative analyses with truncated substrates of 2‐decalol and cyclohexanol showed reduced catalytic efficiencies and smaller slopes in hydrophobicity‐activity correlations, indicating that L193 primarily interacts with the steroid A‐ring, while distal rings contacts provide additional binding energy for the stabilization of the transition state. Importantly, variants that deviate from simple hydrophobicity trends underscore the role of shape complementarity, in which changes in side‐chain size, branching, or polarity can impose steric constraints or disrupt packing, thereby impairing productive substrate orientation in both the ground state and the transition state. Thus, hydrophobicity contributes to stabilization of the closed active form, but optimal catalysis requires a geometrically compatible packing interface between L193, the loop, and the steroid. Thermal stability assays confirmed that substitutions at L193 did not affect global protein folding, emphasizing that observed effects were functional rather than structural.
